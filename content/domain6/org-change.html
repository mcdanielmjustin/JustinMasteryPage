Introduction to Organizational Change

Consider this scenario: A large behavioral health organization decides to transition from paper-based clinical documentation to a fully integrated electronic health record system. Leadership announces the change, purchases the software, and schedules a go-live date — yet six months later, clinicians are still keeping shadow charts on paper, administrative staff are entering data incorrectly, and morale has plummeted. The technology itself was never the problem. What failed was the organizational change process — the way the transition was planned, communicated, and managed from start to finish. This kind of story plays out in hospitals, schools, corporations, and government agencies every day, and it illustrates why understanding how organizations change is one of the most practically important topics you will encounter on the EPPP.

Organizational change encompasses the processes, strategies, and models used to transition organizations from their current state to a desired future state. But that dry definition only hints at the complexity involved. Change touches every level of an organization simultaneously — individual employees who must learn new behaviors, teams that must renegotiate roles and workflows, and entire cultures that must shift their assumptions about "how things are done here." Whether the change is a modest restructuring of a single department or a wholesale transformation of an organization's mission, the same fundamental dynamics are at play: people must let go of familiar routines, navigate uncertainty, and ultimately adopt new ways of working.

Understanding change is critical because most organizational interventions — whether involving restructuring, culture change, or new technology implementation — require systematic change management to succeed. Research consistently shows that a large percentage of organizational change initiatives fail to achieve their intended outcomes, and the primary reasons are rarely technical. Instead, failures stem from inadequate leadership support, poor communication, unaddressed employee resistance, and a lack of attention to the human side of the transition. For psychologists working in organizational settings — whether as consultants, coaches, or embedded practitioners — the ability to diagnose barriers to change and design interventions that address them is a core professional competency.

The EPPP tests your knowledge of several foundational frameworks in this area, including classic change models that describe the stages organizations move through during transitions, organizational development interventions that facilitate change at individual, group, and system levels, the psychological dynamics of resistance to change, and the powerful role that organizational culture plays in either supporting or undermining change efforts. As we move through this chapter, you will encounter theorists and models that have shaped how psychologists and organizational leaders think about transformation — beginning with one of the most influential and enduring frameworks in the field, developed by social psychologist Kurt Lewin in the mid-twentieth century.

Before we dive into specific models, it is worth pausing to appreciate why psychology has so much to offer this domain. Organizations are, at their core, systems of human behavior. Policies, structures, and technologies are important, but they only produce results when people use them effectively. This means that every organizational change initiative is fundamentally a behavioral change initiative — and that makes it squarely within the psychologist's area of expertise. The principles of learning, motivation, group dynamics, and social influence that you have studied throughout your training are the very tools that distinguish successful change efforts from failed ones. With that foundation in mind, let us turn to the model that started it all.

Lewin's Change Model

Kurt Lewin (1947) developed one of the most influential models of planned change, conceptualizing the change process as moving through three sequential stages.

Lewin's Three-Stage Model

  1. Unfreezing: Creating motivation and readiness for change by disrupting the current equilibrium. This involves helping people recognize that the current situation is inadequate, reducing resistance, and building urgency. Techniques include presenting disconfirming data, creating a felt need, and ensuring psychological safety.
  2. Changing (Moving): Implementing the actual change -- learning new behaviors, values, attitudes, or structures. This stage involves cognitive restructuring, identification with new role models, and scanning the environment for new information.
  3. Refreezing: Stabilizing the change by integrating new behaviors into the organizational culture, norms, and practices. This involves reinforcing new patterns, establishing supporting structures, and ensuring the change is sustained over time.

Lewin's Force Field Analysis

Force field analysis is Lewin's diagnostic tool for understanding the forces that support and resist change. The current state is maintained by an equilibrium between:

  • Driving forces: Factors pushing toward change (competitive pressure, new technology, declining performance, leadership vision)
  • Restraining forces: Factors resisting change (habit, fear of the unknown, resource limitations, organizational inertia)

EPPP Key Concept

Lewin argued that reducing restraining forces is generally more effective than increasing driving forces for producing lasting change. Increasing driving forces without addressing restraining forces can create tension and instability. This insight has important implications for managing resistance -- work to understand and reduce resistance rather than simply pushing harder for change.

Kotter's Eight-Step Model

John Kotter (1996) expanded on Lewin's model to provide a more detailed, practical framework for leading organizational change:

Kotter's Eight Steps

  1. Establish a sense of urgency: Examine competitive realities; identify crises and opportunities (unfreezing)
  2. Create a guiding coalition: Assemble a group with enough power and influence to lead the change
  3. Develop a vision and strategy: Create a clear vision to direct the change effort and strategies for achieving it
  4. Communicate the change vision: Use every vehicle possible to communicate the new vision and strategies
  5. Empower broad-based action: Remove obstacles, change systems or structures that undermine the vision (changing)
  6. Generate short-term wins: Plan for and create visible performance improvements; recognize and reward contributors
  7. Consolidate gains and produce more change: Use increased credibility to change structures, systems, and policies
  8. Anchor new approaches in the culture: Articulate the connections between new behaviors and organizational success (refreezing)

Kotter emphasized that the stages should be followed sequentially and that the most common errors include failing to establish sufficient urgency (Step 1) and declaring victory too early before changes are anchored in the culture (Step 8).

Organizational Development (OD)

Organizational development is a planned, systematic approach to improving organizational effectiveness through the application of behavioral science knowledge. OD is distinguished by its emphasis on humanistic values, collaboration, and long-term change.

Action Research

Action research, developed by Kurt Lewin, is both a research approach and a change methodology. It involves a cyclical process:

  1. Problem identification: Recognizing that a change is needed
  2. Data gathering: Collecting information about the problem (surveys, interviews, observations)
  3. Data feedback: Sharing findings with organizational members
  4. Action planning: Collaboratively developing interventions based on the data
  5. Implementation: Carrying out the planned changes
  6. Evaluation: Assessing the effects and recycling through the process

Cummings and Worley (2009) elaborated on the action research cycle by describing a comprehensive model of planned change that unfolds across distinct phases. During the initial phase — often called entering and contracting — the foremost task is identifying the relevant client(s) who should be engaged in addressing the organization's issues. This step is critical because change efforts can stall or misfire when the wrong stakeholders are at the table. The "relevant client" is not always the person who initially contacts the consultant; rather, it includes those individuals or groups who have the authority, information, and motivation necessary to define the problem accurately and commit resources to solving it. Only after these key clients are correctly identified can the practitioner negotiate expectations, clarify roles, and establish a working agreement that gives the change process a solid foundation.

Example

A human resources director contacts an OD consultant about high turnover in the engineering division. Although HR initiated the request, the relevant clients may actually be the engineering division's leadership team and front-line managers, because they possess direct knowledge of the problems and the authority to implement changes in their unit. The consultant's first task is to identify and engage these stakeholders before proceeding to data collection.

A program logic model is a visual diagram that maps out the essential components of a program and illustrates the hypothesized causal chain linking resources to intended outcomes. Logic models serve as planning, implementation, and evaluation tools — they clarify what a program is supposed to do, how it is supposed to work, and what results it aims to achieve. In organizational development and action research contexts, logic models help stakeholders develop a shared understanding of an intervention before it is launched, monitor fidelity during implementation, and evaluate whether the program produced its intended effects.

Core Components of a Program Logic Model

Resources (Inputs): The human, financial, organizational, and material assets invested in the program (e.g., staff time, funding, technology, expertise).
Activities: The specific actions the program undertakes using those resources (e.g., training workshops, coaching sessions, process redesign meetings).
Outputs: The direct, countable products of program activities (e.g., number of employees trained, sessions held, materials distributed).
Outcomes: The changes expected as a result — often divided into short-term (knowledge and attitude shifts), intermediate (behavior change), and long-term (organizational performance improvement) outcomes.

Example

An organization launches a leadership development initiative. The logic model specifies resources (budget of $50,000, two external coaches, assessment instruments), activities (360-degree feedback, monthly coaching, peer learning circles), outputs (40 managers completing the program, 240 coaching hours delivered), and outcomes (improved leadership self-efficacy in 3 months, higher subordinate engagement scores in 6 months, reduced turnover in 12 months). This visual map guides both the implementation team and evaluators through each stage of the action research cycle.

OD Interventions

Flexible work schedules (flextime) represent a quality of work life intervention that allows employees some degree of choice regarding when they begin and end their workday, typically within parameters set by the organization. A landmark meta-analysis by Baltes, Briggs, Huff, Wright, and Neuman (1999) synthesized research on the effects of flextime across multiple outcome variables, providing the most comprehensive picture of its organizational impact.

The meta-analytic results revealed a clear hierarchy of effects. Flextime produced its largest positive impact on reducing absenteeism, suggesting that schedule flexibility helps employees manage personal obligations that might otherwise result in missed workdays. The second-strongest effect was on objective productivity measures, followed in descending order by employee satisfaction with the schedule itself, overall job satisfaction, and finally self-rated performance, which showed the weakest benefit. Notably, flextime did not appear to have a significant effect on supervisor-rated performance.

These findings carry practical significance for organizations considering flexible scheduling. The pattern suggests that flextime works primarily by removing barriers to attendance and enabling employees to work during their most productive hours, rather than by broadly transforming how employees feel about their jobs or perceive their own effectiveness.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember the rank ordering of flextime benefits from the Baltes et al. meta-analysis: absenteeism reduction was strongest, followed by objective productivity, satisfaction with the schedule, job satisfaction, and self-rated performance (weakest). The key takeaway is that flextime's most robust effects are on behavioral outcomes (attendance, productivity) rather than attitudinal ones.

Meta-analytic research on the compressed workweek reveals a nuanced pattern of effects across different work outcomes. The strongest and most consistent finding is a positive impact on job satisfaction: employees working compressed schedules report meaningfully higher satisfaction than those on traditional five-day schedules. The effect on job productivity is also positive but notably weaker and more variable across studies — some organizations see modest productivity gains, while others find no significant change. Most ambiguous are the effects on absenteeism: research results are mixed, with some studies showing reduced absences (presumably because there are fewer workdays to miss), while others find no reliable difference or even slight increases, possibly due to fatigue-related sick days from longer shifts.

Outcome Variable Direction of Effect Strength / Consistency
Job Satisfaction Positive Strong and consistent
Job Productivity Positive Weaker and more variable
Absenteeism Unclear Mixed findings across studies

Clinical Note

For EPPP preparation, be able to rank the three outcomes: satisfaction benefits are clearest, productivity gains are modest, and absenteeism effects remain inconclusive. This differential pattern is a commonly tested distinction.

Compressed Workweek

A scheduling arrangement in which employees work the standard number of weekly hours in fewer days — most commonly four 10-hour days (a "4/40" schedule) instead of five 8-hour days.

Research on alternative work schedules has consistently identified the compressed workweek as having a notably strong beneficial effect on job satisfaction. In meta-analytic reviews, compressed schedules outperform other flexible arrangements (e.g., flextime, telecommuting) on this specific outcome. Employees report greater satisfaction largely because the extra day off provides more continuous personal time, reduces commuting frequency, and enhances perceptions of schedule control. Organizations considering quality-of-work-life (QWL) improvements should note this finding: when the primary goal is to boost employee satisfaction, implementing a compressed schedule offers one of the most well-supported structural interventions available. However, practitioners should weigh this benefit against potential drawbacks, including fatigue from longer daily shifts and possible coverage gaps, which may vary by industry and job type.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that among alternative work schedule arrangements, the compressed workweek stands out for its robust positive relationship with job satisfaction — stronger than its effects on other outcomes such as productivity or absenteeism.

Quality circles (QCs) are small groups of employees — typically 6 to 12 — who voluntarily meet on a regular basis to identify, analyze, and propose solutions to work-related problems, particularly those involving quality, efficiency, and working conditions. Originating in Japan during the 1960s as part of the broader quality movement influenced by W. Edwards Deming and Kaoru Ishikawa, quality circles became widely adopted in Western organizations during the 1980s.

A defining characteristic of quality circles is their advisory role: members investigate issues within their work area and then present recommendations to management, but they do not have the authority to implement changes unilaterally. Management retains decision-making power over whether and how to act on the group's proposals. QC members typically receive training in problem-solving techniques, statistical methods, and group facilitation to support their analytical work.

Example

A quality circle on a manufacturing floor notices a recurring defect at one stage of the assembly process. The group meets weekly, collects data on defect rates, conducts root-cause analysis using fishbone diagrams, and ultimately presents management with a recommendation to recalibrate equipment settings and adjust the inspection protocol. Management reviews the proposal and authorizes implementation.

Research on quality circles shows mixed long-term results. Initial gains in morale and modest productivity improvements are common, but sustaining engagement over time can be difficult — particularly if management frequently rejects recommendations or fails to provide feedback, which can erode member motivation and trust in the process.

Common OD Interventions

  • Survey feedback: Systematically collecting data through questionnaires, analyzing results, and feeding them back to organizational members for discussion and action planning
  • Team building: Activities designed to improve team functioning, including clarifying roles, improving communication, and resolving conflicts
  • Process consultation: An OD consultant helps the client organization perceive, understand, and act upon process events (communication patterns, decision-making, conflict) -- the consultant facilitates rather than prescribes solutions (Schein, 1969)
  • Sensitivity training (T-groups): Small, unstructured groups that increase self-awareness and interpersonal sensitivity. Less commonly used today due to ethical concerns and variable effectiveness.
  • Quality of work life (QWL): Programs to improve the overall work experience, including job redesign, participative management, and flexible work arrangements
  • Total quality management (TQM): Organization-wide approach emphasizing continuous improvement, customer focus, and employee involvement (Deming, Juran)

TQM is grounded in three essential features that distinguish it from other improvement initiatives. First, continuous improvement (often referred to by the Japanese term kaizen) reflects the philosophy that quality is never a finished state — processes should be perpetually refined through incremental enhancements rather than dramatic overhauls. Second, full employee involvement means that quality is everyone's responsibility, not just management's; frontline workers are empowered to identify defects, suggest solutions, and participate in cross-functional quality teams. Third, data-supported decision-making requires that changes be driven by statistical analysis and objective measurement rather than intuition or anecdote — tools such as control charts, Pareto analysis, and root cause analysis ensure that interventions target verified sources of variability. Together, these three pillars create a systematic, organization-wide commitment to quality that is participative, evidence-based, and ongoing.

Total Quality Management (TQM) rests on several interconnected principles that distinguish it from piecemeal improvement efforts. The primary emphases include: (1) continuous improvement (often referred to by the Japanese term kaizen), which treats quality enhancement as an ongoing, never-finished process rather than a one-time project; (2) total employee involvement, meaning that responsibility for quality extends to every organizational member, not just managers or quality inspectors; (3) customer satisfaction as the ultimate criterion of organizational success, with both external customers and internal customers (other departments or units) considered; and (4) data-driven decision-making, which requires systematic measurement of processes and outcomes using statistical tools such as control charts, Pareto analysis, and process flow diagrams. W. Edwards Deming, one of TQM's intellectual architects, stressed that most quality problems arise from flawed systems and processes rather than from individual worker errors — a perspective that shifts managerial attention toward redesigning systems rather than blaming employees.

OD interventions are commonly categorized by their level and focus. Technostructural interventions target the organization's technology, structure, and work design — examples include job enrichment, job enlargement, work redesign, total quality management, and reengineering efforts. By contrast, process consultation falls under the category of human process interventions, which address interpersonal dynamics, communication patterns, group functioning, and leadership processes. Understanding this classification is important for the EPPP: process consultation helps clients perceive and understand behavioral dynamics within the organization, and its focus on relational and communicative processes places it squarely outside the technostructural category. Other human process interventions include team building, sensitivity training, and third-party conflict resolution.

Intervention Category Focus Examples
Human Process Interpersonal and group dynamics Process consultation, team building, T-groups, survey feedback
Technostructural Work design, technology, and organizational structure Job enrichment, TQM, reengineering, structural redesign
Human Resource Management People management systems Performance appraisal, reward systems, career development
Strategic Organization-environment alignment Culture change, mergers/acquisitions, learning organizations

Appreciative Inquiry

Appreciative Inquiry (AI), developed by David Cooperrider and Suresh Srivastva (1987), represents a fundamental departure from traditional organizational development approaches. Rather than identifying problems and deficits to fix, AI focuses on discovering what works well in an organization and amplifying those strengths.

The 4D Cycle of Appreciative Inquiry

  1. Discovery ("What gives life?"): Identifying and appreciating the best of what currently exists -- peak experiences, core strengths, and positive moments in the organization's history through interviews and storytelling
  2. Dream ("What might be?"): Envisioning what the organization could become if its strengths were fully leveraged. Members collaboratively create a shared image of a preferred future.
  3. Design ("What should be?"): Crafting the social architecture -- developing provocative propositions (design statements) that describe the ideal organization and bridge the best of the present with the desired future
  4. Destiny ("What will be?"): Implementing the design through ongoing innovation, commitment, and action. This phase sustains the change through empowerment and continuous learning.

Strengths-Based vs. Deficit-Based Approaches

Traditional OD follows a deficit-based model: identify a problem, diagnose causes, develop solutions, and implement fixes. Appreciative Inquiry inverts this by asking "What is working?" rather than "What is broken?" The assumption is that organizations grow in the direction of what they study -- focusing on problems generates more problem-finding, while focusing on strengths generates energy, innovation, and positive change. This makes AI particularly useful when organizations need to build morale and engagement during transformational change.

EPPP Key Concept

For the EPPP, know that Appreciative Inquiry is a strengths-based OD intervention that contrasts with traditional problem-solving approaches. The 4D cycle (Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny) is the key framework. AI is rooted in social constructionism -- the idea that organizations are socially constructed and can be reimagined through positive dialogue.

Resistance to Change

Resistance to change is a natural response that can stem from individual and organizational factors. Understanding the sources of resistance is essential for managing change effectively.

Sources of Resistance

Individual sources:

  • Habit and inertia -- comfort with the familiar
  • Fear of the unknown -- uncertainty about consequences
  • Economic insecurity -- concerns about job loss or reduced pay
  • Selective perception -- filtering information to maintain current beliefs
  • Loss of control -- feeling powerless in the change process

Organizational sources:

  • Structural inertia -- built-in mechanisms that maintain stability
  • Group norms -- pressure to conform to established patterns
  • Threat to expertise -- changes may make specialized knowledge obsolete
  • Threat to power relationships -- changes may redistribute authority
  • Previous failed change efforts -- cynicism about new initiatives

Strategies for Overcoming Resistance

Kotter and Schlesinger (1979) identified six strategies, ranging from collaborative to coercive: (1) Education and communication -- explain the rationale; (2) Participation and involvement -- involve resistors in planning; (3) Facilitation and support -- provide training and resources; (4) Negotiation and agreement -- offer incentives; (5) Manipulation and co-optation -- give resistors a symbolic role; (6) Explicit and implicit coercion -- use authority to force compliance (last resort).

Organizational Culture

Imagine walking into two different psychology clinics on the same day. In the first, the waiting room is sleek and minimalist, clinicians greet each other by first name, and every door is open — supervision happens spontaneously in the hallway. In the second, the décor is formal, staff address each other by title, and closed-door policies govern nearly every interaction. Both clinics may provide excellent care, but something fundamentally different is at work beneath the surface. That "something" — the invisible force shaping how people behave, communicate, and make decisions — is organizational culture.

Having just explored how individuals resist change and the strategies leaders can use to address that resistance, it's worth asking a deeper question: why does resistance arise so predictably in certain organizations but not others? The answer often lies in culture. Organizational culture refers to the shared assumptions, values, beliefs, and norms that shape behavior within an organization. It functions like an organization's personality — largely invisible to those inside it, yet powerfully determining what gets rewarded, what gets punished, and what simply feels "normal." When a proposed change runs against the cultural grain, resistance isn't a bug; it's the immune system doing exactly what it was designed to do.

Edgar Schein (1985, 2010), widely regarded as the foundational theorist in this area, proposed that culture is not a single thing but rather operates at three distinct levels — each progressively harder to see and harder to change. Understanding these levels is essential for anyone who wants to lead organizational change rather than simply impose it.

Schein's Three Levels of Organizational Culture

  1. Artifacts: Visible, tangible elements (physical layout, dress code, rituals, logos, published values) -- easy to observe but difficult to interpret
  2. Espoused values: Stated strategies, goals, and philosophies -- what the organization says it values (may or may not align with actual behavior)
  3. Basic underlying assumptions: Unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs about human nature, relationships, and reality -- the deepest and most difficult to change level, but the essence of culture

To make Schein's model concrete, consider a community mental health center that proudly displays a banner reading "We Value Innovation" in its lobby. The banner is an artifact — visible to anyone who walks in. The stated commitment to innovation is an espoused value — what the organization publicly claims to prioritize. But when a new clinician proposes using a telehealth platform and is met with skepticism, bureaucratic hurdles, and quiet discouragement from senior staff, a basic underlying assumption has been revealed: that "real" therapy happens face-to-face and that deviating from established methods is risky. Notice that the assumption was never written in any policy manual. It didn't need to be — it was simply "the way things are done here."

This layered structure explains why surface-level changes so often fail to transform organizations. A hospital can redesign its mission statement, roll out new uniforms, and restructure its org chart — all artifact-level changes — without touching the basic underlying assumptions that actually drive clinician behavior. Schein argued that genuine culture change requires excavating and confronting these deep assumptions, which is precisely why it is so difficult and why it provokes such intense resistance.

Culture change is among the most challenging forms of organizational change because basic assumptions are deeply embedded and often unconscious. Successful culture change typically requires leadership modeling — leaders must embody the new values visibly and consistently, not merely endorse them in memos. It also demands alignment of reward systems so that behaviors consistent with the desired culture are reinforced, hiring practices that select for cultural fit with the emerging (not legacy) culture, and sustained attention over years rather than months. Quick cultural "makeovers" almost invariably revert once leadership attention shifts elsewhere, because the deeper assumptions were never genuinely altered.

For the EPPP, remember that Schein's three levels move from the most visible and easiest to change (artifacts) to the most invisible and most resistant to change (basic underlying assumptions). A common exam strategy is to present a scenario and ask you to identify which level of culture is being described. If you can see it — it's an artifact. If the organization says it but may not live it — it's an espoused value. If it operates silently, shaping behavior without anyone questioning it — you've found a basic underlying assumption, and you've found the true heart of organizational culture.

EPPP Focus: Key Testable Concepts

Commonly Tested Topics for the EPPP

  • Lewin's three stages: unfreeze, change (move), refreeze
  • Force field analysis: driving forces vs. restraining forces; reducing restraining forces is more effective
  • Kotter's eight steps: establish urgency, guiding coalition, vision, communicate, empower, short-term wins, consolidate, anchor in culture
  • Action research: cyclical process of data gathering, feedback, planning, action, evaluation
  • Process consultation (Schein): consultant helps client understand process events, does not prescribe solutions
  • Survey feedback: collect data via questionnaires, feed results back to members for collaborative planning
  • Sensitivity training (T-groups): unstructured groups for interpersonal awareness; less common today
  • Appreciative Inquiry (Cooperrider & Srivastva): strengths-based OD; 4D cycle: Discovery, Dream, Design, Destiny
  • AI contrasts with deficit-based problem-solving; focuses on what works rather than what is broken
  • Schein's three levels of culture: artifacts (visible), espoused values (stated), basic assumptions (deepest)
  • Basic underlying assumptions are the deepest, most resistant to change, and represent the essence of culture
  • Resistance stems from both individual factors (fear, habit) and organizational factors (structural inertia, norms)
  • Participation and involvement is generally the most recommended strategy for reducing resistance
