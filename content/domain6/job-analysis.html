Job Analysis

Task analysis, KSAOs, job descriptions, competency modeling
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Introduction to Job Analysis

Job analysis is the systematic process of collecting and analyzing information about the content, context, and requirements of a job. It serves as the foundation for virtually all human resource functions, including selection, training, performance appraisal, compensation, and legal compliance.

For the EPPP, you must understand the distinction between different approaches to job analysis, the concept of KSAOs, and how job analysis information feeds into performance appraisal systems.

Approaches to Job Analysis

Task-Oriented Job Analysis

Task-oriented analysis focuses on what the worker does -- the specific tasks, duties, and responsibilities that comprise the job. This approach produces detailed descriptions of work activities.

Functional Job Analysis (FJA)

Developed by Sidney Fine, FJA classifies jobs based on the level of complexity in three domains:

  • Data: How workers interact with information (synthesizing, coordinating, analyzing, compiling, computing, copying, comparing)
  • People: How workers interact with others (mentoring, negotiating, instructing, supervising, diverting, persuading, speaking-signaling, serving, taking instructions)
  • Things: How workers interact with objects and equipment (setting up, precision working, operating-controlling, driving-operating, manipulating, tending, feeding-offbearing, handling)

Each domain is arranged hierarchically, with higher levels subsuming lower levels. FJA was used to develop the Dictionary of Occupational Titles (DOT).

Worker-Oriented Job Analysis

Worker-oriented analysis focuses on the human attributes and behaviors needed to perform the job, rather than the tasks themselves. This approach emphasizes what the worker brings to the job.

Position Analysis Questionnaire (PAQ)

Developed by McCormick, Jeanneret, and Mecham (1972), the PAQ is the most well-known worker-oriented instrument. It contains 194 job elements organized into six divisions:

  1. Information input: How the worker obtains information (visual, non-visual sources)
  2. Mental processes: Reasoning, decision-making, planning, information processing
  3. Work output: Physical activities and tools used
  4. Relationships with others: Interpersonal requirements
  5. Job context: Physical and social work environment
  6. Other characteristics: Schedules, demands, responsibilities

A major advantage of the PAQ is that it allows quantitative comparison across jobs, making it useful for compensation systems. A limitation is that it requires a college reading level and may be too abstract for some applications.

Job Descriptions and Job Specifications

Job analysis produces two primary documents:

Job Description vs. Job Specification

  • Job description: A written summary of the tasks, duties, responsibilities, and working conditions of a job. It describes what is done. Used for recruitment, orientation, and defining role expectations.
  • Job specification: A written summary of the minimum qualifications (KSAOs) a person must possess to perform the job successfully. It describes who can do the job. Used for selection and placement decisions.

Example: Distinguishing Description from Specification

Job description excerpt (Clinical Psychologist): "Conducts psychological assessments, provides individual and group psychotherapy, develops treatment plans, and participates in multidisciplinary treatment team meetings."

Job specification excerpt: "Doctoral degree in clinical psychology from an APA-accredited program, state licensure as a psychologist, minimum 2 years post-licensure clinical experience, knowledge of evidence-based treatments."

O*NET and Modern Job Analysis

The Occupational Information Network (O*NET) is the U.S. Department of Labor's comprehensive online database that replaced the Dictionary of Occupational Titles (DOT). O*NET uses a content model organized around six domains:

  • Worker characteristics: Abilities, occupational interests, work values, work styles
  • Worker requirements: Skills, knowledge, education
  • Experience requirements: Training, experience, licensure
  • Occupational requirements: Generalized work activities, organizational context, work context
  • Workforce characteristics: Labor market information, occupational outlook
  • Occupation-specific information: Tasks, tools, and technologies

Comparable Worth and Job Evaluation

One of the most consequential applications of job analysis data is in establishing equitable compensation structures. Job evaluation is the process of systematically determining the relative worth of jobs within an organization to set fair pay levels. When job analysis reveals that positions held predominantly by one gender are compensated differently from positions of equivalent complexity held predominantly by the other gender, organizations face a comparable worth challenge.

Comparable worth (sometimes called pay equity) is the principle that jobs requiring equivalent levels of skill, effort, responsibility, and working conditions should receive equivalent compensation, regardless of whether those jobs are traditionally held by men or women. For example, a female-dominated occupation such as nursing and a male-dominated occupation such as facilities engineering might require comparable levels of education, decision-making complexity, and responsibility for others' welfare. Comparable worth procedures use quantitative job evaluation data — often derived from instruments like the PAQ that allow cross-job comparisons — to identify pay disparities that cannot be explained by legitimate job-related factors.

Example

A state government conducts a job evaluation and discovers that administrative assistants (85% female) and maintenance technicians (90% male) receive similar scores on compensable factors such as required knowledge, problem-solving demands, and accountability. Despite this equivalence, maintenance technicians earn 20% more. A comparable worth analysis would flag this discrepancy and recommend pay adjustments to achieve equity across these gender-segregated roles.

Comparable worth differs from the legal standard of "equal pay for equal work" established by the Equal Pay Act of 1963, which applies only when men and women perform substantially identical jobs. Comparable worth extends this logic to different jobs that are nonetheless equivalent in their demands and value to the organization. While comparable worth has been more widely implemented in public-sector settings, the underlying job evaluation methodology remains a core application of job analysis across all organizational contexts.

Job Evaluation

A systematic process for determining the relative worth of jobs within an organization by examining their content and assigning value based on compensable factors. Job evaluation is used to establish equitable pay structures rather than to analyze tasks or select employees.

Job evaluation is distinct from job analysis, though it relies heavily on job analysis data. While job analysis identifies what a job entails and what qualifications are needed, job evaluation determines how much a job should be compensated relative to other positions in the organization. The most widely used approach is the point method, in which each job is rated on several compensable factors—dimensions the organization considers worthy of pay, such as required skill level, mental and physical effort, degree of responsibility, and working conditions. Each factor is assigned a weight reflecting its importance, and jobs receive points on each factor according to predetermined scales. The total point value for a job determines its position within the organization's pay hierarchy.

Other job evaluation methods include the ranking method (ordering jobs from highest to lowest overall value), the classification method (slotting jobs into predefined grade levels, as used in the U.S. federal General Schedule system), and the factor comparison method (ranking jobs on each compensable factor separately and assigning dollar values). The point method remains dominant because of its systematic, quantitative nature and its ability to provide a defensible rationale for pay differences across positions.

Job Evaluation Method Approach Key Feature
Point Method Assigns numerical points to compensable factors Most precise and widely used; quantitative
Ranking Method Orders whole jobs from most to least valuable Simplest but least precise; no factor breakdown
Classification Method Slots jobs into predefined grade categories Used in government systems (e.g., GS levels)
Factor Comparison Ranks jobs on each factor and assigns dollar amounts Complex; combines ranking with monetary values

Clinical Note

Job evaluation is frequently tested on the EPPP in the context of compensation and comparable worth. Understanding that the point method assigns numerical values to compensable factors—and that the total point score determines a job's monetary worth—is essential for distinguishing job evaluation from job analysis and job classification.

Competency Modeling

Competency modeling is a newer, more strategic approach that identifies the broader competencies (integrated sets of knowledge, skills, abilities, and personal attributes) needed for effective performance across a role, job family, or entire organization. Unlike traditional job analysis, which focuses on specific tasks, competency modeling emphasizes organizational strategy and culture.

EPPP Key Distinction

Traditional job analysis is more detailed, legally defensible, and task-specific. Competency modeling is broader, more future-oriented, and aligned with organizational strategy. Both approaches have value, but for legal defensibility in selection contexts, traditional job analysis is preferred because of its specificity and established methodology.

Performance Appraisal Methods

Performance appraisal is the systematic evaluation of employee job performance. Job analysis directly informs the development of performance criteria. Methods vary in their focus on traits, behaviors, or results.

Forced Distribution Method

A performance appraisal approach that requires raters to sort employees into predetermined categories according to a fixed percentage distribution (e.g., 10% "exceptional," 20% "above average," 40% "average," 20% "below average," 10% "poor"). This method is sometimes called "rank and yank" when tied to termination decisions for the lowest-rated group.

The forced distribution method compels managers to allocate employees across a set of performance categories that follow a specified distribution, often resembling a normal curve. Organizations adopt this approach to combat leniency and central tendency errors—because managers cannot rate everyone as "above average," meaningful differentiation among employees is guaranteed. General Electric famously used a "20-70-10" system under Jack Welch, categorizing employees as top performers, vital middle contributors, or bottom performers slated for development or removal.

The central limitation of forced distribution is that the prespecified categories may not reflect the actual distribution of employee performance within a given work unit. If a manager leads a team of genuinely high-performing individuals, the method still forces a proportion into low-performance categories, resulting in inaccurate and potentially demoralizing evaluations. Conversely, in a struggling unit, some poor performers may be classified into higher tiers simply to fill the quota. This mismatch between imposed categories and real performance levels can undermine employee trust, damage morale, and increase turnover among strong performers who feel unfairly rated. Legal challenges have also arisen when forced distribution systems produce adverse impact against protected groups.

Example

A software development team of 10 engineers has been exceptionally productive, with all members exceeding their quarterly targets. Under a forced distribution system requiring 10% in the lowest category, the manager must still designate one engineer as a poor performer despite objective evidence to the contrary. This misclassification may prompt the unfairly rated employee to seek employment elsewhere.

Beyond rating scale approaches, several comparative (relative) methods exist for evaluating employee performance. These techniques require the evaluator to directly compare workers against each other rather than against an absolute standard.

Paired Comparison Technique

A performance evaluation method in which every employee is systematically compared with every other employee, one pair at a time. For each pair, the rater selects the higher performer. The employee who is chosen most frequently across all pairings receives the highest overall ranking.

The paired comparison method yields highly reliable rankings because it forces deliberate, focused judgments for each dyad. However, the technique becomes increasingly impractical as the number of employees grows, because the total number of required comparisons follows the formula n(n – 1) / 2, where n is the number of employees. For a group of 10 employees, a rater must make 45 separate comparisons. For 50 employees, the number jumps to 1,225 comparisons—a burden that is both time-consuming and cognitively fatiguing. This scalability problem is the method's most significant drawback.

Number of Employees (n) Required Comparisons [n(n-1)/2]
5 10
10 45
20 190
50 1,225

Other comparative methods include simple ranking, in which all employees are ordered from best to worst, and forced distribution, in which employees are sorted into predetermined categories (e.g., top 10%, middle 70%, bottom 20%). Simple ranking avoids the combinatorial explosion of paired comparisons but makes it difficult to distinguish among employees in the middle of the distribution. Forced distribution ensures variability in ratings but may misrepresent group performance when most employees are genuinely high or low performers.

Rating Scale Methods

Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scales (BARS)

Developed by Smith and Kendall (1963), BARS use specific behavioral examples (critical incidents) as anchors for each point on the rating scale. Each performance dimension has its own scale with behavioral descriptions at different effectiveness levels.

Advantages: Reduces ambiguity, provides clear behavioral standards, involves employees in development

Disadvantages: Time-consuming and expensive to develop, specific to each job

Behavioral Observation Scales (BOS)

Developed by Latham and Wexley (1977), BOS ask raters to indicate the frequency with which an employee exhibits specific behaviors, using a 5-point frequency scale (Almost Never to Almost Always). Unlike BARS, which rates typical performance level, BOS measures the frequency of desired behaviors.

360-Degree Feedback

360-degree feedback (multi-source assessment) collects performance evaluations from multiple perspectives: supervisors, peers, subordinates, and sometimes customers and the employee themselves. This comprehensive approach provides a more complete picture of performance.

Example: 360-Degree Feedback Sources

A manager receives feedback from: (1) their supervisor on strategic thinking, (2) peers on collaboration, (3) direct reports on coaching and communication, (4) customers on responsiveness, and (5) self-assessment on all dimensions. Discrepancies between self and others' ratings can highlight blind spots.

Common Rating Errors

Criterion Contamination

A measurement problem that occurs when scores on a criterion (performance measure) are systematically influenced by factors unrelated to actual job performance, thereby reducing the validity of the criterion.

Criterion contamination represents one of the most significant threats to the accuracy of performance measurement. It occurs when a performance rating or outcome measure captures variance attributable to something other than the employee's true job effectiveness. One classic source of contamination is knowledge of predictor scores: if a supervisor knows that an employee scored exceptionally well on a selection test, this knowledge may inflate subsequent performance ratings regardless of the employee's actual on-the-job behavior. Similarly, if a supervisor knows an employee was hired despite marginal test scores, the supervisor may unconsciously rate that individual more harshly.

Other sources of criterion contamination include opportunity bias (differences in resources, territories, or caseloads that affect measured outcomes independently of effort or skill), group membership effects (ratings influenced by demographic characteristics rather than performance), and environmental factors (economic conditions that affect sales figures regardless of individual effort). Criterion contamination can be distinguished from two related concepts:

Concept Definition Effect on Measurement
Criterion Contamination Criterion score includes variance from non-performance factors Inflates or deflates scores; introduces systematic error
Criterion Deficiency Criterion fails to capture all relevant aspects of performance Underrepresents the full performance domain
Criterion Relevance The degree to which the criterion measures actual job performance Represents the valid, useful portion of the criterion

Example

A hiring manager shares with a department supervisor that a newly hired employee earned the highest score on the cognitive ability test used during selection. Over the next year, the supervisor consistently rates this employee as "exceeding expectations," partly because the supervisor expects superior performance based on the test result. This foreknowledge contaminates the criterion measure, making it impossible to determine whether the high performance rating reflects genuine job behavior or the supervisor's biased expectations.

Performance appraisal methods can be broadly categorized as absolute or relative approaches. Absolute methods evaluate each employee against a fixed standard (e.g., rating scales, BARS, BOS), while relative methods compare employees directly to one another (e.g., forced ranking, paired comparisons). A significant advantage of relative approaches is their ability to minimize several common rater biases. Because relative methods require the rater to differentiate among employees—ranking some higher and others lower—they inherently prevent the rater from clustering all evaluations at the top of the scale (leniency), at the bottom (severity/strictness), or in the middle (central tendency). The very structure of relative measurement forces discrimination between performance levels.

However, relative methods carry their own limitations. They do not indicate the absolute level of performance—a top-ranked employee in a weak group may still perform poorly by objective standards. They can also foster unhealthy competition among workers and are difficult to use for providing specific developmental feedback. Additionally, relative rankings become legally vulnerable when used as the sole basis for employment decisions, because they lack the behavioral specificity that courts favor. For these reasons, many organizations combine relative and absolute methods to gain the bias-reduction benefits of comparison while retaining the descriptive richness of criterion-referenced scales.

Clinical Note

When an EPPP question asks about reducing leniency, strictness, or central tendency errors through the appraisal method itself (rather than rater training), the correct answer typically points to relative rating techniques such as forced distribution or paired comparisons, because these methods structurally prevent uniform ratings.

Frame-of-reference (FOR) training has been shown to be one of the most effective methods for reducing rating errors. FOR training teaches raters to use common standards and behavioral examples when evaluating performance.

Frame-of-reference (FOR) training achieves its effectiveness by targeting the root cause of most rating errors: raters applying different personal standards when evaluating the same performance. The primary objective of FOR training is to teach raters to accurately differentiate among varying levels of employee effectiveness by establishing a shared evaluative framework. During training, raters review performance dimensions, study behavioral examples representing high, moderate, and low performance, practice rating standardized vignettes, and receive feedback on the accuracy of their judgments. Through this process, raters internalize a common reference point that aligns with organizationally defined standards, rather than relying on idiosyncratic beliefs about what constitutes "good" or "poor" work. Research consistently demonstrates that FOR training outperforms other approaches — such as rater error training, which merely teaches raters to avoid specific biases — because it directly improves rating accuracy rather than simply altering the statistical distribution of ratings.

Frame-of-reference (FOR) training works by teaching raters to recognize that job performance is multidimensional—an employee may excel in one area (e.g., technical proficiency) while performing poorly in another (e.g., interpersonal communication). During FOR training, raters learn to distinguish among different effectiveness levels within each dimension by studying behavioral examples that represent poor, average, and outstanding performance. Through guided practice and group discussion, raters develop a shared conceptual framework—a common "frame of reference"—so that all evaluators apply consistent standards when assigning ratings. This shared understanding reduces idiosyncratic interpretations and minimizes errors such as halo, leniency, and central tendency. Research consistently shows FOR training to be superior to other rater training approaches, including rater error training (which simply teaches raters to avoid specific biases) because FOR training targets the underlying cognitive processes involved in performance evaluation rather than merely suppressing surface-level errors.
