Job satisfaction, burnout, work-life balance, occupational stress
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Introduction to Job Satisfaction and Occupational Stress

Job satisfaction and occupational stress are among the most widely studied outcomes in I/O psychology. They influence employee well-being, performance, absenteeism, and turnover. The EPPP tests knowledge of the major theoretical frameworks, the construct of organizational commitment, and the burnout syndrome.

Job Satisfaction and Health Outcomes

Beyond its effects on turnover and performance, job satisfaction has meaningful implications for employee health. A large-scale meta-analysis by Faragher, Cass, and Cooper (2005) systematically reviewed the accumulated research on the association between job satisfaction and health. Their analysis confirmed that satisfaction at work is significantly related to both mental health and physical health outcomes. Critically, the relationship was substantially stronger for mental health variables — including depression, anxiety, self-esteem, and general psychological well-being — than for physical health indicators such as cardiovascular problems or somatic complaints.

These findings underscore that chronic dissatisfaction with one's work is not merely an attitudinal inconvenience; it constitutes a genuine risk factor for psychological distress. Low job satisfaction was most strongly linked to burnout, lowered self-esteem, and elevated rates of depression and anxiety. The physical health associations, while statistically significant, were more modest in magnitude, suggesting that the pathway from dissatisfaction to physical illness may be partially mediated through psychological mechanisms such as stress, negative affect, and health behaviors.

Example

A clinical psychologist working in a managed care setting reports persistent dissatisfaction with excessive caseloads and limited autonomy. Over time, she develops symptoms of depression and anxiety that interfere with her personal life. Although she has not yet developed cardiovascular or immune-related problems, the Faragher et al. findings predict that her mental health is at considerably greater immediate risk than her physical health. Organizational interventions targeting her satisfaction — such as caseload adjustments or increased decision-making latitude — could serve a protective mental health function.

A longstanding question in I/O psychology concerns the direction and strength of the relationship between job satisfaction and job performance. Early research suggested the correlation was modest (around .17), leading some scholars to question whether "a happy worker is a productive worker." However, more recent meta-analyses by Judge, Thoresen, Bono, and Patton (2001) found a corrected correlation of approximately .30, indicating a meaningful association. Critically, the strength of this relationship depends on situational moderators — most notably, the degree to which performance is contingently rewarded.

When pay, bonuses, promotions, and other reinforcers are directly tied to individual productivity, both satisfaction and performance tend to be higher, and the correlation between them is strengthened. This finding aligns with reinforcement theory and expectancy theory: employees who see a clear link between their effort, their output, and the rewards they receive develop more positive attitudes toward their work and are motivated to sustain high performance levels. In contrast, when rewards are distributed uniformly regardless of individual contribution, the satisfaction-performance relationship weakens because high performers may feel inequitably treated while low performers receive the same outcomes.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that the satisfaction-performance link is not simply a matter of "happy workers work harder." The relationship is strongest in environments where contingent reinforcement systems are in place — that is, where valued outcomes like compensation and recognition are closely coupled with measured productivity. This means organizational design and reward structures serve as key moderators of the satisfaction-performance relationship.

Job Satisfaction

Job satisfaction is an evaluative judgment about one's job that reflects cognitive and affective responses to job experiences. It can be assessed as overall (global) satisfaction or as satisfaction with specific facets (pay, supervision, coworkers, promotion, work itself).

Dispositional Approach

The dispositional approach suggests that job satisfaction is partly determined by stable personality traits. Research shows that satisfaction levels remain moderately consistent across jobs and over time, suggesting a dispositional component.

  • Positive affectivity (PA): Individuals high in PA tend to experience more positive emotions and greater satisfaction across situations
  • Negative affectivity (NA): Individuals high in NA tend to experience more negative emotions and report lower satisfaction
  • Core self-evaluations (CSE): Judge et al. (1997) identified a higher-order trait comprising self-esteem, generalized self-efficacy, locus of control, and emotional stability that predicts job satisfaction

Locke's Value-Percept Theory

Edwin Locke (1976) proposed that job satisfaction results from the perception that one's job fulfills important work values. Satisfaction = (Vwant - Vhave) x Vimportance. Greater discrepancy between what is wanted and what is received leads to dissatisfaction, but only if the dimension is important to the individual.

Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model

The Job Characteristics Model (Hackman & Oldham, 1976) identifies five core job dimensions that produce critical psychological states leading to satisfaction and motivation:

Five Core Job Characteristics

  1. Skill variety: The degree to which a job requires different skills and talents
  2. Task identity: The degree to which a job involves completing a whole, identifiable piece of work
  3. Task significance: The degree to which the job has a meaningful impact on others
  4. Autonomy: The degree of freedom and independence in scheduling and procedures
  5. Feedback: The degree to which the job itself provides clear information about performance

The Motivating Potential Score (MPS) = [(Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance) / 3] x Autonomy x Feedback

Autonomy and feedback are multiplied because if either is zero, motivating potential is zero regardless of other dimensions.

EPPP Key Concept

The Job Characteristics Model includes a moderator: Growth Need Strength (GNS). The model predicts that the relationship between job characteristics and outcomes is strongest for individuals with high GNS -- those who have a strong desire for personal growth, learning, and challenge. Individuals with low GNS may not respond positively to enriched jobs.

Organizational Commitment

Organizational commitment reflects an employee's psychological attachment to and identification with their organization. The most widely accepted model is Meyer and Allen's (1991) three-component model:

Three Components of Organizational Commitment

ComponentBasisReason for Staying
Affective commitmentEmotional attachment and identification"I stay because I want to"
Continuance commitmentPerceived costs of leaving (investments, lack of alternatives)"I stay because I need to"
Normative commitmentSense of obligation and loyalty"I stay because I ought to"

Research consistently shows that affective commitment has the strongest positive relationship with desirable outcomes (job performance, organizational citizenship behavior, attendance) and the strongest negative relationship with turnover. Continuance commitment has weaker or even negative relationships with performance, as employees may stay but not be highly motivated.

Example: Continuance Commitment

A psychologist who has accumulated a generous pension, built a strong referral network, and lives close to her clinic may feel "locked in" despite dissatisfaction. Her years of investment and the perceived difficulty of starting over create high continuance commitment. She stays but may be disengaged -- illustrating why continuance commitment predicts retention but not performance.

Organizational Citizenship Behavior (OCB)

Having explored the different forms of organizational commitment — and how a clinician can remain at her workplace for years due to sunk costs rather than genuine engagement — we now turn to the flip side of that coin: what does it look like when employees go above and beyond, not because they have to, but because they genuinely want to? Think of the psychologist who stays late to help a colleague prepare for a difficult case presentation, or the intake coordinator who proactively reorganizes the scheduling system to reduce patient wait times. No one asked them to do these things. No bonus is attached. Yet these small, voluntary acts are the invisible glue that holds organizations together.

Dennis Organ (1988) gave this phenomenon a name: Organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) refers to individual behavior that is discretionary, not directly or explicitly recognized by the formal reward system, and that in the aggregate promotes the effective functioning of the organization. In other words, OCBs are the "extra-role" behaviors that go beyond what a job description formally requires. They are the reason some teams feel cohesive and supportive while others — even those staffed with equally competent individuals — feel fragmented and cold. OCBs don't show up on performance reviews, but they profoundly shape the culture and effectiveness of every workplace.

Organizational Justice and Job Satisfaction

What compels someone to engage in these generous extra-role behaviors? One of the most powerful drivers is the perception that their workplace is fair. The relationship between OCBs and organizational justice highlights the broader role that fairness perceptions play in workplace attitudes and behavior. When employees feel treated fairly, they reciprocate with citizenship behaviors; when they sense injustice, they withdraw not just effort but goodwill. Organizational justice is typically conceptualized in three forms: distributive justice (perceived fairness of outcomes and resource allocation), procedural justice (perceived fairness of the processes used to determine outcomes), and interactional justice (perceived fairness and respect in interpersonal treatment during organizational procedures).

A comprehensive meta-analysis by Cohen-Charash and Spector (2001) examined how each type of justice relates to key work outcomes. Their findings revealed that job satisfaction was positively associated with all three justice dimensions and that the magnitude of these relationships was similar across distributive, procedural, and interactional justice. This is a nuanced and important finding: it means that employees' overall satisfaction with their work is influenced not only by whether they receive fair outcomes but equally by whether they perceive the decision-making processes as equitable and whether they feel treated with dignity by supervisors and organizational representatives. A clinical supervisor who gives a trainee a fair evaluation score (distributive justice) but delivers the feedback dismissively and without explanation (low interactional justice) may still undermine the trainee's satisfaction — because all three channels matter.

Justice Type Focus Example Relationship to Satisfaction
Distributive Fairness of outcomes (pay, promotions) Receiving a raise proportional to contributions Positive
Procedural Fairness of decision-making processes Having voice in how performance evaluations are conducted Positive (similar magnitude)
Interactional Fairness in interpersonal treatment Supervisor explains decisions respectfully and transparently Positive (similar magnitude)

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, recognize that while OCBs are most strongly predicted by procedural justice specifically, job satisfaction itself is predicted equally by all three justice types. This distinction matters: the antecedents of satisfaction and the antecedents of citizenship behavior overlap but are not identical in their justice-related pathways. Organizations seeking to enhance satisfaction should attend to fairness comprehensively — ensuring equitable outcomes, transparent processes, and respectful interpersonal treatment.

To appreciate why justice perceptions matter so deeply, it helps to examine each form more closely and understand their distinct mechanisms. Research consistently demonstrates that perceptions of organizational justice are among the strongest predictors of OCBs. Organizational justice encompasses several related but distinct constructs that address different aspects of fairness in the workplace. Imagine a community mental health center where layoffs are announced: some clinicians will focus on whether the right people were let go (distributive), others on whether the process followed clear criteria (procedural), and still others on whether the director explained the decision with compassion (interactional). Each lens captures a different dimension of fairness.

Types of Organizational Justice

Distributive justice concerns the perceived fairness of how outcomes — such as pay raises, bonuses, promotions, and workload assignments — are allocated among employees. It draws heavily on Adams' equity theory, in which individuals evaluate whether the ratio of their inputs to outcomes is comparable to that of relevant others. A survey item asking an employee to rate whether a recent decision about raises and bonuses was fair in its allocation of rewards is assessing distributive justice specifically.

Procedural justice refers to the perceived fairness of the processes and procedures used to make decisions. Key criteria include consistency, bias suppression, accuracy of information, correctability, representativeness, and ethicality (Leventhal, 1980).

Interactional justice addresses the quality of interpersonal treatment received during the implementation of procedures. It is sometimes further divided into interpersonal justice (dignity and respect) and informational justice (adequacy of explanations).

Justice Type Core Question Example
Distributive "Were the outcomes fair?" Rating whether bonus allocations reflect actual contributions
Procedural "Were the decision-making methods fair?" Rating whether promotion criteria were applied consistently
Interactional "Was I treated with respect and given adequate explanation?" Rating whether a supervisor explained the rationale behind a layoff decision

For the EPPP, note that procedural justice tends to be the strongest predictor of OCBs directed toward the organization (OCB-O), while distributive and interactional justice more strongly predict attitudes toward specific decisions and supervisors. All three forms of justice contribute to job satisfaction, trust, and organizational commitment. This makes intuitive sense: when you believe the system itself is fair, you invest in the system; when you believe a particular outcome or person was fair, your loyalty flows toward that specific target.

Organ's Five Dimensions of OCB

Organ didn't treat citizenship behavior as a single monolithic concept. Instead, he identified five distinct dimensions, each capturing a different way employees contribute beyond formal expectations. Understanding these dimensions helps clarify why OCB is not simply "being nice" — it encompasses a range of behaviors from helping individuals to stewarding the organization as a whole.

  1. Altruism: Helping specific individuals with organizationally relevant tasks or problems (e.g., voluntarily assisting a coworker who is behind on their workload)
  2. Conscientiousness: Going well beyond minimum role requirements in areas such as attendance, punctuality, housekeeping, and use of time (also called generalized compliance)
  3. Sportsmanship: Willingness to tolerate less-than-ideal circumstances without complaining, grievances, or petty resentments -- "rolling with the punches"
  4. Courtesy: Taking actions to prevent problems with others by providing advance notice, reminders, or consultation before acting (e.g., alerting a colleague about a decision that may affect them)
  5. Civic virtue: Responsible, constructive involvement in the political process and governance of the organization (e.g., attending meetings, keeping up with organizational issues, voicing concerns)

A quick clinical example brings these to life: Consider a psychology training clinic. The intern who stays to help a peer rehearse a diagnostic interview is showing altruism. The postdoc who consistently arrives early to prepare the group therapy room demonstrates conscientiousness. The staff psychologist who doesn't complain when the holiday schedule is less than ideal models sportsmanship. The supervisor who gives a trainee advance notice about upcoming policy changes practices courtesy. And the faculty member who attends governance meetings to advocate for better supervision ratios displays civic virtue. Together, these behaviors create a training environment where people thrive — even though none of them appear in anyone's formal job description.

Research consistently shows that OCBs are positively predicted by affective commitment, job satisfaction, perceptions of fairness (organizational justice), and transformational leadership. Notice the thread connecting these concepts: employees who feel emotionally bonded to their organization (affective commitment, discussed in the previous section), who are satisfied with their work, and who perceive their workplace as fair are the ones most likely to go above and beyond. OCBs contribute to organizational effectiveness by enhancing coworker productivity, reducing the need for maintenance functions, and improving coordination. In aggregate, these small voluntary acts compound — research suggests they account for meaningful variance in unit-level performance, customer satisfaction, and even profitability.

EPPP Key Concept

OCBs are important because they represent performance that is not captured by traditional job performance measures. For the EPPP, know Organ's five dimensions and that OCBs are most strongly predicted by job satisfaction and organizational justice (particularly procedural justice). Also note the distinction between OCB-I (behaviors directed toward individuals, like altruism and courtesy) and OCB-O (behaviors directed toward the organization, like conscientiousness, sportsmanship, and civic virtue). A helpful mnemonic: OCB-I involves helping people (Individuals), while OCB-O involves supporting the system (Organization). When an exam question describes an employee voluntarily mentoring a new hire, that's OCB-I; when it describes someone attending optional committee meetings, that's OCB-O.

Among the forms of organizational justice, procedural justice is particularly relevant to OCB prediction. Procedural justice refers to the perceived fairness of the methods, mechanisms, and processes used to arrive at organizational decisions — such as how raises, bonuses, and promotions are determined. For example, a survey item asking employees to rate "how fair the processes are that determine employee compensation and advancement" directly taps procedural justice perceptions. This is distinct from distributive justice, which concerns perceived fairness of actual outcomes (e.g., whether the raise amount itself is fair), and interactional justice, which concerns the quality of interpersonal treatment received during the decision-making process. Research demonstrates that procedural justice is especially predictive of OCB-O (organization-directed citizenship behaviors), while interactional justice tends to be more predictive of OCB-I (individual-directed behaviors). When employees believe that decision-making processes are transparent, consistent, and unbiased, they are more likely to reciprocate with discretionary extra-role contributions.

Burnout

Burnout is a psychological syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Christina Maslach's model (Maslach & Jackson, 1981) is the most widely cited framework.

Maslach's Three Dimensions of Burnout

  • Emotional exhaustion: Feeling drained, depleted of emotional resources, and overwhelmed by work demands. This is considered the core and most prominent dimension.
  • Depersonalization (cynicism): Developing negative, callous, or detached attitudes toward clients, patients, or coworkers. In human service professions, this manifests as treating people as objects.
  • Reduced personal accomplishment (inefficacy): Feeling incompetent, unproductive, and lacking achievement in one's work with people.

Burnout is measured by the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), which has versions for human services, educators, and general occupations. Risk factors include high workload, lack of control, insufficient reward, breakdown of community, absence of fairness, and value conflicts (Maslach & Leiter, 2008).

EPPP Key Concept

Burnout is particularly prevalent among helping professionals (therapists, physicians, nurses, social workers, teachers). For the EPPP, know that emotional exhaustion is the most central component and develops first. Depersonalization is considered a coping response to exhaustion, and reduced personal accomplishment develops as a consequence. Burnout is distinct from depression, though they share features -- burnout is work-specific, while depression is more pervasive.

Occupational Stress Models

EPPP Test-Taking Strategy: Kobasa and Hardiness

On the EPPP, questions about hardiness typically provide the name Kobasa and ask what concept she is associated with, or they describe a scenario involving successful stress coping and ask which construct applies. The critical association to memorize is: Kobasa = hardiness = three C's (control, commitment, challenge). If a question stem mentions an individual's capacity to cope effectively with stress and lists answer options including hardiness (or psychological hardiness), this is almost certainly the correct choice. Even if the exact phrasing varies — for instance, "psychological hardiness" rather than simply "hardiness" — select the option that most closely aligns with what you know about Kobasa's framework. Phrases referencing resilience under stress, viewing change as opportunity, or maintaining engagement despite adversity all serve as retrieval cues pointing toward this construct. When in doubt, choose the answer that best matches the core idea of thriving under stressful conditions.

Kobasa's Hardiness

Suzanne Kobasa (1979, 1982) introduced the concept of psychological hardiness to explain why some individuals remain healthy and resilient under high stress while others become ill. Studying executives facing significant life stressors, Kobasa found that those who stayed healthy shared a constellation of personality characteristics she termed hardiness. This construct functions as a stress buffer, moderating the relationship between stressful events and health outcomes.

The Three C's of Hardiness

Control: The belief that one can influence the course of events in one's life, rather than feeling helpless or powerless in the face of external forces.

Commitment: A sense of deep involvement in and purpose derived from life activities — work, family, relationships, and personal interests — rather than alienation and withdrawal.

Challenge: The tendency to view change and new experiences as opportunities for growth and learning rather than as threats to security. Hardy individuals embrace rather than resist transitions.

Individuals who score high on all three components are considered hardy and demonstrate greater capacity to cope with stress successfully. Hardy individuals tend to appraise potentially stressful situations as less threatening, use more active and problem-focused coping strategies, and maintain better physical and psychological health under duress. Hardiness has been studied extensively in occupational contexts, including among military personnel, first responders, nurses, and corporate employees.

Clinical Note

Hardiness is conceptually related to but distinct from other resilience-related constructs. Unlike locus of control (which addresses only perceptions of control) or self-efficacy (which is task-specific), hardiness integrates three dispositional orientations that collectively shape how individuals engage with stressful circumstances. For the EPPP, firmly associate Kobasa with hardiness and the three C's: control, commitment, and challenge.

Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome

Before examining workplace-specific stress models, it is essential to understand the foundational biological stress framework developed by Hans Selye. The General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) describes the body's physiological response pattern when exposed to prolonged stressors. Selye (1956) observed that regardless of the specific stressor — whether physical, psychological, or environmental — the body follows a predictable three-stage response sequence.

Three Stages of the General Adaptation Syndrome

1. Alarm Reaction: The body detects a threat and activates the sympathetic nervous system, triggering the "fight-or-flight" response. Cortisol and adrenaline are released, heart rate increases, and resources are mobilized. Resistance to the stressor is initially below normal.

2. Resistance (Adaptation): If the stressor persists, the body attempts to adapt and restore homeostasis. Physiological arousal remains elevated but becomes more focused. The individual appears to be coping effectively, though bodily resources are being steadily consumed.

3. Exhaustion: When the stressor continues beyond the body's capacity to adapt, physiological resources become depleted. The individual becomes vulnerable to illness, organ damage, and psychological breakdown. Prolonged exhaustion can contribute to cardiovascular disease, immune suppression, and other serious health consequences.

Selye's model provides the biological underpinning for understanding why chronic occupational stress — such as sustained high workload with insufficient recovery — can lead to serious health outcomes. The exhaustion stage bears conceptual similarity to the emotional exhaustion dimension of Maslach's burnout model, though Selye's framework addresses general physiological deterioration rather than work-specific psychological responses. For the EPPP, recognize that Selye's GAS is a nonspecific response model — the same three-stage pattern occurs regardless of the nature of the stressor.

Type A Behavior Pattern and Health Outcomes

The Type A behavior pattern (TABP), originally described by cardiologists Meyer Friedman and Ray Rosenman in the 1950s, is characterized by a cluster of traits including time urgency, competitiveness, impatience, and hostility. Initial research from the Western Collaborative Group Study suggested that the overall Type A pattern doubled the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD). However, subsequent large-scale studies, including the Multiple Risk Factor Intervention Trial (MRFIT), failed to replicate this broad association, prompting researchers to examine which specific components of the pattern were most pathogenic.

This line of investigation converged on a clear finding: among all the constituent features of the Type A pattern, hostility — encompassing cynical mistrust of others, frequent anger, and aggressive interpersonal behavior — has emerged as the toxic core most reliably associated with cardiovascular disease and other adverse health outcomes. Hostile individuals display greater cardiovascular reactivity to stress (exaggerated blood pressure and heart rate responses), higher levels of circulating catecholamines, and more pronounced inflammatory markers. Over time, these repeated physiological surges contribute to atherosclerosis and elevated CHD risk.

Type A Component Description Link to CHD
Time urgency Chronic sense of time pressure; impatience with delays Weak or inconsistent
Competitiveness Strong achievement drive; need to outperform others Weak or inconsistent
Hostility Cynicism, frequent anger, antagonistic interpersonal style Strongest and most consistent predictor

Example

A manager exhibits classic Type A characteristics: she works long hours, schedules meetings back-to-back, and is intensely driven. However, she is generally warm and trusting toward colleagues. In contrast, her equally driven counterpart frequently expresses contempt toward subordinates, assumes others are incompetent, and reacts with anger when challenged. Research suggests the second manager faces substantially greater cardiovascular risk — not because of the shared competitiveness and time pressure, but because of the hostile interpersonal orientation that distinguishes his behavioral profile.

Physiological Effects of Chronic Occupational Stress

Prolonged exposure to occupational stressors activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in sustained release of glucocorticoids — primarily cortisol in humans. While acute cortisol elevation serves adaptive functions (mobilizing energy, sharpening attention), chronically elevated cortisol levels produce a range of harmful physiological consequences. One of the most well-documented effects involves the hippocampus, a brain structure essential for the consolidation of new memories and spatial navigation. The hippocampus has a high density of glucocorticoid receptors, making it particularly vulnerable to prolonged cortisol exposure.

Research by Sapolsky and others has demonstrated that chronic cortisol elevation leads to dendritic atrophy, inhibition of neurogenesis, and ultimately neuronal loss in the hippocampus. These structural changes manifest behaviorally as impairments in declarative memory, difficulties with new learning, and reduced cognitive flexibility. Neuroimaging studies of individuals experiencing chronic work-related stress have shown reduced hippocampal volume compared to low-stress controls. This neurotoxic cascade helps explain the cognitive complaints — forgetfulness, difficulty concentrating, mental "fog" — frequently reported by workers experiencing sustained occupational strain or burnout.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember the specific link: chronic cortisol elevation → hippocampal neuron loss → memory impairment. This connection illustrates how workplace stress produces not just psychological distress but measurable neurobiological damage. It also underscores why stress management interventions are critical from both an organizational and a health psychology perspective.

Karasek's Job Demand-Control Model

Robert Karasek (1979) proposed that occupational stress results from the interaction of two factors: job demands and job control (decision latitude).

Four Job Types in the Demand-Control Model

Low DemandsHigh Demands
High ControlLow-strain (relaxed)Active (challenging but manageable)
Low ControlPassive (boring, de-motivating)High-strain (most stressful)

The high-strain condition (high demands, low control) is most detrimental to health and well-being. Later versions added social support as a third dimension -- iso-strain (high demands, low control, low social support) is the most harmful combination.

Effort-Reward Imbalance Model (Siegrist)

Johannes Siegrist (1996) proposed that stress results from an imbalance between effort expended at work and rewards received (salary, esteem, career opportunities, job security). High effort combined with low reward produces distress, particularly for individuals high in overcommitment (excessive striving and need for approval).

Work-Life Balance

Work-family conflict occurs when demands from work and family domains are mutually incompatible. Two directions of conflict are recognized:

  • Work-to-family conflict: Work demands interfere with family responsibilities
  • Family-to-work conflict: Family demands interfere with work responsibilities

Both directions are associated with decreased satisfaction, increased stress, and poorer mental health. Organizational interventions include flexible work arrangements, family leave policies, telework options, and supportive supervision.
