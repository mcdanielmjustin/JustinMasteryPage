Introduction to Job Satisfaction and Occupational Stress

Walk into any workplace and ask people how they feel about their jobs, and you will hear a remarkable range of responses -- from passionate engagement to quiet resignation to simmering resentment. A clinical psychologist at a community mental health center who finds deep meaning in her work with underserved clients but dreads the administrative burden of managed care paperwork is simultaneously satisfied and dissatisfied, depending on which facet of her job you ask about. Meanwhile, her colleague down the hall, who earns the same salary and carries a similar caseload, reports being thoroughly content. What accounts for these differences? And more importantly, what consequences do they carry for the individuals, their clients, and the organizations they serve?

Job satisfaction and occupational stress are among the most widely studied outcomes in I/O psychology, and for good reason: they influence employee well-being, performance, absenteeism, and turnover in measurable ways that ripple through every level of an organization. The EPPP tests knowledge of the major theoretical frameworks, the construct of organizational commitment, and the burnout syndrome. Understanding these constructs is essential not only for exam preparation but for any psychologist who consults with organizations, supervises clinical staff, or designs workplace interventions.

Job Satisfaction and Health Outcomes

Beyond its effects on turnover and performance, job satisfaction has meaningful implications for employee health. A large-scale meta-analysis by Faragher, Cass, and Cooper (2005) systematically reviewed the accumulated research on the association between job satisfaction and health. Their analysis confirmed that satisfaction at work is significantly related to both mental health and physical health outcomes. Critically, the relationship was substantially stronger for mental health variables — including depression, anxiety, self-esteem, and general psychological well-being — than for physical health indicators such as cardiovascular problems or somatic complaints.

These findings underscore that chronic dissatisfaction with one's work is not merely an attitudinal inconvenience; it constitutes a genuine risk factor for psychological distress. Low job satisfaction was most strongly linked to burnout, lowered self-esteem, and elevated rates of depression and anxiety. The physical health associations, while statistically significant, were more modest in magnitude, suggesting that the pathway from dissatisfaction to physical illness may be partially mediated through psychological mechanisms such as stress, negative affect, and health behaviors.

Example

A clinical psychologist working in a managed care setting reports persistent dissatisfaction with excessive caseloads and limited autonomy. Over time, she develops symptoms of depression and anxiety that interfere with her personal life. Although she has not yet developed cardiovascular or immune-related problems, the Faragher et al. findings predict that her mental health is at considerably greater immediate risk than her physical health. Organizational interventions targeting her satisfaction — such as caseload adjustments or increased decision-making latitude — could serve a protective mental health function.

A longstanding question in I/O psychology concerns the direction and strength of the relationship between job satisfaction and job performance. Early research suggested the correlation was modest (around .17), leading some scholars to question whether "a happy worker is a productive worker." However, more recent meta-analyses by Judge, Thoresen, Bono, and Patton (2001) found a corrected correlation of approximately .30, indicating a meaningful association. Critically, the strength of this relationship depends on situational moderators — most notably, the degree to which performance is contingently rewarded.

When pay, bonuses, promotions, and other reinforcers are directly tied to individual productivity, both satisfaction and performance tend to be higher, and the correlation between them is strengthened. This finding aligns with reinforcement theory and expectancy theory: employees who see a clear link between their effort, their output, and the rewards they receive develop more positive attitudes toward their work and are motivated to sustain high performance levels. In contrast, when rewards are distributed uniformly regardless of individual contribution, the satisfaction-performance relationship weakens because high performers may feel inequitably treated while low performers receive the same outcomes.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that the satisfaction-performance link is not simply a matter of "happy workers work harder." The relationship is strongest in environments where contingent reinforcement systems are in place — that is, where valued outcomes like compensation and recognition are closely coupled with measured productivity. This means organizational design and reward structures serve as key moderators of the satisfaction-performance relationship.

Job Satisfaction

The health and performance findings above establish why satisfaction matters; the question now is what shapes it. Job satisfaction is an evaluative judgment reflecting both cognitive and affective responses to job experiences, assessed as overall (global) satisfaction or satisfaction with specific facets -- pay, supervision, coworkers, promotion, and the work itself. Three theoretical perspectives compete to explain its origins: dispositional, value-based, and job-design approaches. The organizational justice framework in the visual aid below cuts across all three.

Three Types of Organizational Justice
Distributive Justice

Fairness of outcomes (pay, promotions, workload). Based on Adams' equity theory. Predicts satisfaction with specific decisions.

Procedural Justice

Fairness of decision-making processes. Strongest predictor of OCB-O. Criteria: consistency, bias suppression, accuracy, correctability.

Organizational Justice — All three equally predict job satisfaction (Cohen-Charash & Spector, 2001)
Interactional Justice

Fairness of interpersonal treatment. Includes interpersonal justice (respect) and informational justice (adequate explanation).

Key EPPP Distinction

OCBs most strongly predicted by procedural justice. Job satisfaction predicted equally by all three types.

Dispositional Approach

The dispositional approach suggests that job satisfaction is partly determined by stable personality traits. Research shows that satisfaction levels remain moderately consistent across jobs and over time, suggesting a dispositional component.

  • Positive affectivity (PA): Individuals high in PA tend to experience more positive emotions and greater satisfaction across situations
  • Negative affectivity (NA): Individuals high in NA tend to experience more negative emotions and report lower satisfaction
  • Core self-evaluations (CSE): Judge et al. (1997) identified a higher-order trait comprising self-esteem, generalized self-efficacy, locus of control, and emotional stability that predicts job satisfaction

Locke's Value-Percept Theory

Edwin Locke (1976) proposed that job satisfaction results from the perception that one's job fulfills important work values. Satisfaction = (Vwant - Vhave) x Vimportance. Greater discrepancy between what is wanted and what is received leads to dissatisfaction, but only if the dimension is important to the individual.

Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model

The Job Characteristics Model (Hackman & Oldham, 1976) identifies five core job dimensions that produce critical psychological states leading to satisfaction and motivation:

Five Core Job Characteristics

  1. Skill variety: The degree to which a job requires different skills and talents
  2. Task identity: The degree to which a job involves completing a whole, identifiable piece of work
  3. Task significance: The degree to which the job has a meaningful impact on others
  4. Autonomy: The degree of freedom and independence in scheduling and procedures
  5. Feedback: The degree to which the job itself provides clear information about performance

The Motivating Potential Score (MPS) = [(Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance) / 3] x Autonomy x Feedback

Autonomy and feedback are multiplied because if either is zero, motivating potential is zero regardless of other dimensions.

EPPP Key Concept

The Job Characteristics Model includes a moderator: Growth Need Strength (GNS). The model predicts that the relationship between job characteristics and outcomes is strongest for individuals with high GNS -- those who have a strong desire for personal growth, learning, and challenge. Individuals with low GNS may not respond positively to enriched jobs.

Organizational Commitment

The Hackman-Oldham model above explains how job characteristics drive satisfaction; commitment addresses a related but distinct question: why do employees stay? Satisfaction can fluctuate day to day, yet some employees remain loyal for decades while others leave at the first opportunity. Organizational commitment -- the psychological bond tying an individual to an organization -- explains this stickiness. Meyer and Allen's (1991) three-component model, compared in the visual aid below, distinguishes three qualitatively different reasons for staying.

Three Components of Organizational Commitment

ComponentBasisReason for Staying
Affective commitmentEmotional attachment and identification"I stay because I want to"

Research consistently shows that affective commitment has the strongest positive relationship with desirable outcomes (job performance, organizational citizenship behavior, attendance) and the strongest negative relationship with turnover. Continuance commitment has weaker or even negative relationships with performance, as employees may stay but not be highly motivated.

Example: Continuance Commitment

A psychologist who has accumulated a generous pension, built a strong referral network, and lives close to her clinic may feel "locked in" despite dissatisfaction. Her years of investment and the perceived difficulty of starting over create high continuance commitment. She stays but may be disengaged -- illustrating why continuance commitment predicts retention but not performance.

Organizational Citizenship Behavior (OCB)

Commitment explains why employees stay; organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) explains why some go above and beyond once they do. The psychologist who stays late to help a colleague prepare for a case presentation, the intake coordinator who proactively reorganizes scheduling to cut patient wait times -- no one asked, no bonus is attached, yet these voluntary acts are the invisible glue that holds organizations together. Dennis Organ (1988) defined OCBs as discretionary behaviors not directly recognized by the formal reward system that, in the aggregate, promote effective organizational functioning. They connect satisfaction, commitment, and justice perceptions into a unified behavioral outcome -- and, as the justice visual aid from the previous section showed, procedural justice is their strongest predictor.

Organizational Justice and Job Satisfaction

What compels someone to engage in these generous extra-role behaviors? One of the most powerful drivers is the perception that their workplace is fair. The relationship between OCBs and organizational justice highlights the broader role that fairness perceptions play in workplace attitudes and behavior. When employees feel treated fairly, they reciprocate with citizenship behaviors; when they sense injustice, they withdraw not just effort but goodwill. Organizational justice is typically conceptualized in three forms: distributive justice (perceived fairness of outcomes and resource allocation), procedural justice (perceived fairness of the processes used to determine outcomes), and interactional justice (perceived fairness and respect in interpersonal treatment during organizational procedures).

A comprehensive meta-analysis by Cohen-Charash and Spector (2001) examined how each type of justice relates to key work outcomes. Their findings revealed that job satisfaction was positively associated with all three justice dimensions and that the magnitude of these relationships was similar across distributive, procedural, and interactional justice. This is a nuanced and important finding: it means that employees' overall satisfaction with their work is influenced not only by whether they receive fair outcomes but equally by whether they perceive the decision-making processes as equitable and whether they feel treated with dignity by supervisors and organizational representatives. A clinical supervisor who gives a trainee a fair evaluation score (distributive justice) but delivers the feedback dismissively and without explanation (low interactional justice) may still undermine the trainee's satisfaction — because all three channels matter.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, recognize that while OCBs are most strongly predicted by procedural justice specifically, job satisfaction itself is predicted equally by all three justice types. This distinction matters: the antecedents of satisfaction and the antecedents of citizenship behavior overlap but are not identical in their justice-related pathways. Organizations seeking to enhance satisfaction should attend to fairness comprehensively — ensuring equitable outcomes, transparent processes, and respectful interpersonal treatment.

To appreciate why justice perceptions matter so deeply, it helps to examine each form more closely and understand their distinct mechanisms. Research consistently demonstrates that perceptions of organizational justice are among the strongest predictors of OCBs. Organizational justice encompasses several related but distinct constructs that address different aspects of fairness in the workplace. Imagine a community mental health center where layoffs are announced: some clinicians will focus on whether the right people were let go (distributive), others on whether the process followed clear criteria (procedural), and still others on whether the director explained the decision with compassion (interactional). Each lens captures a different dimension of fairness.

Types of Organizational Justice

Distributive justice concerns the perceived fairness of how outcomes — such as pay raises, bonuses, promotions, and workload assignments — are allocated among employees. It draws heavily on Adams' equity theory, in which individuals evaluate whether the ratio of their inputs to outcomes is comparable to that of relevant others. A survey item asking an employee to rate whether a recent decision about raises and bonuses was fair in its allocation of rewards is assessing distributive justice specifically.

Procedural justice refers to the perceived fairness of the processes and procedures used to make decisions. Key criteria include consistency, bias suppression, accuracy of information, correctability, representativeness, and ethicality (Leventhal, 1980).

Interactional justice addresses the quality of interpersonal treatment received during the implementation of procedures. It is sometimes further divided into interpersonal justice (dignity and respect) and informational justice (adequacy of explanations).

Justice Type Core Question Example
Distributive "Were the outcomes fair?" Rating whether bonus allocations reflect actual contributions
Procedural "Were the decision-making methods fair?" Rating whether promotion criteria were applied consistently
Interactional "Was I treated with respect and given adequate explanation?" Rating whether a supervisor explained the rationale behind a layoff decision

For the EPPP, note that procedural justice tends to be the strongest predictor of OCBs directed toward the organization (OCB-O), while distributive and interactional justice more strongly predict attitudes toward specific decisions and supervisors. All three forms of justice contribute to job satisfaction, trust, and organizational commitment. This makes intuitive sense: when you believe the system itself is fair, you invest in the system; when you believe a particular outcome or person was fair, your loyalty flows toward that specific target.

Organ's Five Dimensions of OCB

Organ didn't treat citizenship behavior as a single monolithic concept. Instead, he identified five distinct dimensions, each capturing a different way employees contribute beyond formal expectations. Understanding these dimensions helps clarify why OCB is not simply "being nice" — it encompasses a range of behaviors from helping individuals to stewarding the organization as a whole.

  1. Altruism: Helping specific individuals with organizationally relevant tasks or problems (e.g., voluntarily assisting a coworker who is behind on their workload)
  2. Conscientiousness: Going well beyond minimum role requirements in areas such as attendance, punctuality, housekeeping, and use of time (also called generalized compliance)
  3. Sportsmanship: Willingness to tolerate less-than-ideal circumstances without complaining, grievances, or petty resentments -- "rolling with the punches"
  4. Courtesy: Taking actions to prevent problems with others by providing advance notice, reminders, or consultation before acting (e.g., alerting a colleague about a decision that may affect them)
  5. Civic virtue: Responsible, constructive involvement in the political process and governance of the organization (e.g., attending meetings, keeping up with organizational issues, voicing concerns)

A quick clinical example brings these to life: Consider a psychology training clinic. The intern who stays to help a peer rehearse a diagnostic interview is showing altruism. The postdoc who consistently arrives early to prepare the group therapy room demonstrates conscientiousness. The staff psychologist who doesn't complain when the holiday schedule is less than ideal models sportsmanship. The supervisor who gives a trainee advance notice about upcoming policy changes practices courtesy. And the faculty member who attends governance meetings to advocate for better supervision ratios displays civic virtue. Together, these behaviors create a training environment where people thrive — even though none of them appear in anyone's formal job description.

Research consistently shows that OCBs are positively predicted by affective commitment, job satisfaction, perceptions of fairness (organizational justice), and transformational leadership. Notice the thread connecting these concepts: employees who feel emotionally bonded to their organization (affective commitment, discussed in the previous section), who are satisfied with their work, and who perceive their workplace as fair are the ones most likely to go above and beyond. OCBs contribute to organizational effectiveness by enhancing coworker productivity, reducing the need for maintenance functions, and improving coordination. In aggregate, these small voluntary acts compound — research suggests they account for meaningful variance in unit-level performance, customer satisfaction, and even profitability.

EPPP Key Concept

OCBs are important because they represent performance that is not captured by traditional job performance measures. For the EPPP, know Organ's five dimensions and that OCBs are most strongly predicted by job satisfaction and organizational justice (particularly procedural justice). Also note the distinction between OCB-I (behaviors directed toward individuals, like altruism and courtesy) and OCB-O (behaviors directed toward the organization, like conscientiousness, sportsmanship, and civic virtue). A helpful mnemonic: OCB-I involves helping people (Individuals), while OCB-O involves supporting the system (Organization). When an exam question describes an employee voluntarily mentoring a new hire, that's OCB-I; when it describes someone attending optional committee meetings, that's OCB-O.
