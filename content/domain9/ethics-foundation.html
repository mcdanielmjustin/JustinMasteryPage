Introduction to the APA Ethics Code

A psychologist receives an urgent call from a former client's parent, asking the psychologist to release therapy records to an attorney involved in a custody dispute. The psychologist hesitates — she wants to help, but releasing the records without the client's consent could violate confidentiality, and the parent's request raises questions about who the actual client is and whose interests are being served. In this moment, she needs more than good intentions; she needs a structured ethical framework that distinguishes aspirational ideals from enforceable standards and provides clear guidance for navigating competing obligations. That framework is the APA Ethics Code.

The Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (commonly called the APA Ethics Code) was most recently revised in 2017 and serves as the primary ethical guide for psychologists in the United States. It governs the professional, scientific, and educational activities of psychologists and applies to all APA members regardless of their role. The Ethics Code is structured to include both broad aspirational goals and specific enforceable rules. Understanding this dual nature is critical for the EPPP, as exam questions frequently test whether a given statement is aspirational or enforceable.

Key Distinction

Aspirational principles (General Principles A-E) describe the highest ideals of the profession but are not enforceable. Enforceable standards (Standards 1-10) set minimum behavioral expectations and can be used as the basis for ethics complaints and disciplinary action.

Structure of the Ethics Code

Now that we've distinguished between aspirational principles and enforceable standards, let's zoom out and look at how the entire Ethics Code is organized — because understanding the architecture of the Code helps you navigate it efficiently, both in practice and on the EPPP. Think of the Code as a building: the Preamble lays the foundation and explains why the building exists, the General Principles describe the aspirational vision for what the building should look like at its best, the Ethical Standards provide the load-bearing walls that must not be compromised, and the Introduction and Applicability section tells you who must follow the building codes and under what circumstances. Each component serves a distinct purpose, and exam questions frequently test whether you know which part does what.

The APA Ethics Code consists of four major components, each playing a specific role in guiding professional conduct:

  1. Preamble -- States the purpose and scope of the Ethics Code. It establishes that psychologists commit to increasing knowledge, improving conditions, and helping the public develop informed judgments about behavior.
  2. General Principles (A through E) -- Aspirational goals that guide psychologists toward the highest ideals. They are not enforceable but provide a framework for ethical reasoning.
  3. Ethical Standards (1 through 10) -- Enforceable rules that set mandatory obligations. Violations can result in sanctions, license revocation, or expulsion from APA.
  4. Introduction and Applicability -- Clarifies how the Code applies, its relationship to law, and issues of jurisdiction.

Notice the critical distinction embedded in this structure: the General Principles set the ceiling — the highest ideals a psychologist should strive for — while the Ethical Standards set the floor — the minimum behaviors that are required. A psychologist who violates an Ethical Standard can face formal disciplinary action, but a psychologist who falls short of a General Principle cannot be sanctioned on that basis alone. This distinction is one of the most frequently tested concepts on the EPPP, so it is worth anchoring firmly in your understanding before moving forward.

The Preamble

Imagine you are opening a professional guidebook for the first time. Before you encounter any rules or aspirational goals, you want to understand the purpose behind the document — who it's for, what it's trying to accomplish, and why it matters. That is exactly the function of the Preamble. It describes the mission of psychology and the purpose of the Ethics Code, setting the stage for everything that follows. The Preamble acknowledges that psychologists occupy an extraordinarily wide range of roles — as researchers, educators, diagnosticians, therapists, supervisors, consultants, administrators, social interventionists, and expert witnesses — and that the ethical obligations captured in the Code apply to all of these roles.

An important nuance is that the Preamble stresses that the Code applies to these roles only in the context of scientific, educational, or professional activities. This means that the APA Ethics Code does not govern every aspect of a psychologist's personal life, but rather the professional activities that define their work. For example, a psychologist's behavior at a family reunion is generally outside the scope of the Code, but their behavior at a professional conference or in a therapy session is squarely within it. Understanding this boundary of applicability can help you reason through tricky exam scenarios where the line between personal and professional behavior is blurred.

General Principles (Aspirational)

Picture a newly licensed psychologist sitting down to decide whether to accept a complex forensic case that falls at the edge of her competence. She knows what the enforceable standards require, but she also wants to go beyond the minimum — she wants to embody the best of what the profession stands for. The five General Principles are the compass she turns to. They guide psychologists toward ethical ideals — the kind of practice that not only avoids harm but actively promotes human welfare, trust, honesty, fairness, and dignity. For the EPPP, memorize each principle and know that they are aspirational, not enforceable. No psychologist can be sanctioned solely for failing to meet a General Principle, but these principles provide the moral reasoning framework that often informs interpretation of the enforceable standards.

Each of the five principles captures a distinct dimension of ethical excellence. A helpful mnemonic is B-FI-JR — Beneficence, Fidelity, Integrity, Justice, Respect — though the official ordering uses the letters A through E. Let's look at each one in detail, along with the core ideas the EPPP expects you to recognize:

PrincipleCore FocusKey Idea
A: Beneficence and NonmaleficenceDo good; do no harmPsychologists strive to benefit those with whom they work and guard against factors that might lead to misuse of influence. They manage personal, financial, social, organizational, or political factors that could lead to harm.
B: Fidelity and ResponsibilityTrust, professional responsibilityPsychologists establish relationships of trust, are aware of their responsibilities to society and the communities they serve, and consult with colleagues to prevent unethical conduct. They donate a portion of professional time for little or no compensation (pro bono).
C: IntegrityHonesty, truthfulnessPsychologists seek to promote accuracy, honesty, and truthfulness. They do not steal, cheat, or engage in fraud, subterfuge, or intentional misrepresentation of fact. They keep promises and avoid unwise or unclear commitments.
D: JusticeFairness, equal accessPsychologists recognize that fairness and justice entitle all persons to access the contributions of psychology and to equal quality in processes, procedures, and services. They exercise reasonable judgment and take precautions to ensure bias does not lead to unjust practices.
E: Respect for People's Rights and DignityAutonomy, privacy, diversityPsychologists respect the dignity and worth of all people and the rights of individuals to privacy, confidentiality, and self-determination. They are aware of cultural, individual, and role differences including those based on age, gender, race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, sexual orientation, disability, language, and socioeconomic status.

To bring these principles to life, consider a clinical scenario: a psychologist working in a rural community discovers that her only colleague in town has been practicing while impaired due to substance use. Principle A (Beneficence and Nonmaleficence) compels her to consider the potential harm to clients. Principle B (Fidelity and Responsibility) reminds her of her obligation to address unethical conduct among colleagues — not to look the other way. Principle C (Integrity) requires her to be honest in any reporting or consultation she undertakes. Principle D (Justice) asks her to consider whether vulnerable populations in the community are being disproportionately affected. And Principle E (Respect for People's Rights and Dignity) requires her to handle the situation in a way that respects the dignity of both the impaired colleague and the affected clients. In this single scenario, all five principles are at play — which illustrates why understanding them as an interconnected framework, rather than isolated definitions, is so valuable.

EPPP Tip

A common EPPP question format presents a scenario and asks which General Principle applies. Remember: Principle B (Fidelity and Responsibility) is the one that mentions pro bono work and concern for colleagues' ethical behavior. Principle D (Justice) specifically addresses fairness and bias. When two principles seem to apply, focus on which principle most directly addresses the specific ethical concern described in the question stem — the EPPP rewards precision in matching principles to scenarios.

Ethical Standards Overview (Standards 1-10)

Now that we've explored the aspirational General Principles — the moral compass of the profession — it's time to turn to the part of the Ethics Code that has real teeth. Imagine a psychologist who maintains a sexual relationship with a current therapy client, or a researcher who fabricates data. These aren't just violations of lofty ideals; they are breaches of specific, enforceable rules that can result in formal sanctions, loss of licensure, or expulsion from the APA. The ten Ethical Standards are where the Ethics Code shifts from aspiration to obligation, spelling out precisely what psychologists must and must not do in their professional conduct.

Unlike the General Principles (which cannot be the basis for an ethics complaint), the Ethical Standards are the enforceable component of the Ethics Code. Each standard contains multiple subsections addressing specific behaviors and obligations across the full range of professional activities — from how you obtain informed consent, to when you may break confidentiality, to how authorship credit should be assigned on a publication. On the EPPP, you will encounter scenarios that require you to identify which specific standard applies, so familiarity with the scope of each one is essential. Think of the ten standards as ten rooms in a house: each room covers a distinct domain of practice, and knowing which room you're in helps you quickly locate the relevant rule.

The table below provides a high-level map of all ten standards. As you review it, notice how the standards are organized roughly by context — beginning with how to handle ethical dilemmas themselves (Standard 1), moving through the foundations of competent and respectful practice (Standards 2–4), and then addressing specific professional activities such as advertising, record keeping, teaching, research, assessment, and therapy (Standards 5–10). Each of these standards will receive detailed coverage in the sections that follow, but this overview will serve as your orienting framework — the table of contents for the enforceable ethical obligations that govern every facet of psychological practice.

When a test question describes a clinical or professional scenario, mentally scan this list to identify which domain is implicated — then recall the specific obligations within that standard. Detailed Coverage of Each Standard

Now that you have a panoramic view of the ten Ethical Standards and their enforceable role in the Ethics Code, you may be wondering: how deeply should I understand each one? The answer — especially for the EPPP — is thoroughly. Each standard governs a distinct domain of professional conduct, from how you handle ethical dilemmas to how you conduct therapy, and each carries its own nuances, gray areas, and clinical implications that deserve careful exploration. Ethics Decision-Making Models

The Ethics Code alone does not resolve every ethical dilemma. Several decision-making models provide structured approaches for navigating complex situations.

Koocher and Keith-Spiegel's Model

  1. Determine whether the matter is an ethical one
  2. Consult the Ethics Code and relevant guidelines
  3. Consider applicable laws and regulations
  4. Consult with colleagues or ethics committees
  5. Evaluate the rights, responsibilities, and welfare of all affected parties
  6. Generate alternative solutions
  7. Enumerate the consequences of each decision
  8. Make the decision
  9. Implement the decision and follow up

Canadian Code Model (Sinclair et al.)

The Canadian Psychological Association's four-principle model ranks ethical principles hierarchically when they conflict:

  1. Respect for the Dignity of Persons (highest priority)
  2. Responsible Caring
  3. Integrity in Relationships
  4. Responsibility to Society (lowest priority)
Key Difference

Unlike the APA Ethics Code (which does not rank its General Principles), the Canadian Code explicitly prioritizes principles so that when two principles conflict, the higher-ranked principle takes precedence.

The Role of Consultation

Across all decision-making models, consultation with knowledgeable colleagues is strongly emphasized. When facing an ethical dilemma, psychologists should document the steps taken, the reasoning behind their decision, and any consultations obtained. This documentation serves as evidence of the psychologist's good-faith effort to act ethically.

Virtue Ethics Approach

Some ethicists (e.g., Meara, Schmidt, & Day, 1996) argue that ethical codes focused solely on rules are insufficient. A virtue ethics approach asks not just "what should I do?" but "what kind of person should I be?" The virtues of prudence, integrity, respectfulness, and benevolence complement the enforceable standards of the Ethics Code.

Applicability and Enforcement

Having explored how psychologists navigate ethical dilemmas through structured decision-making models and virtue-based reflection, a practical question naturally follows: To whom does the Ethics Code actually apply, and what happens when its standards collide with the law? These are not abstract concerns — they surface regularly on the EPPP and in real-world professional life. Consider a psychologist who maintains a private social media account where they post controversial personal opinions. Does the Ethics Code govern that behavior? Or imagine a clinician practicing in a state whose mandatory reporting laws seem to conflict with ethical confidentiality standards. Which authority prevails? The answers hinge on understanding the scope and enforcement principles embedded in the Code itself, and mastering these nuances is essential for both the licensing exam and competent professional practice.

Several important points about the scope and enforcement of the Ethics Code frequently appear on the EPPP, and each one carries implications that extend far beyond test preparation into the daily realities of psychological practice:

  • The Ethics Code applies to APA members and to psychologists licensed under state laws that adopt the Code, covering professional, scientific, and educational activities.
  • The Code does not apply to psychologists' purely private conduct that is not connected to their professional roles.
  • Lack of awareness or misunderstanding of an Ethical Standard is not a defense to a charge of unethical conduct.
  • The Ethics Code is not intended to be a basis of civil liability; whether a psychologist has violated the Ethics Code does not itself determine legal liability.
  • The modifiers "reasonably," "appropriate," and "potentially" in the Standards allow for professional judgment but require that the psychologist's actions be justifiable given the circumstances.
  • When the Ethics Code establishes a higher standard than the law, psychologists must meet the ethical standard. When the law establishes a higher standard, psychologists must comply with the law.

Let's unpack several of these points, because EPPP questions often test subtle distinctions within them. The scope of the Code is deliberately broad — it covers not just therapy but also research, teaching, supervision, forensic work, consulting, and organizational roles. However, the boundary is drawn at professional activity. A psychologist's weekend hobby of competitive chess or their personal religious practices fall outside the Code's reach, unless those activities become intertwined with their professional role in a way that could cause harm to those they serve.

The "ignorance is no excuse" principle deserves special emphasis. A newly licensed psychologist who has never read Standard 3.05 on multiple relationships cannot cite unfamiliarity as a defense if an ethics complaint is filed. This principle places an affirmative obligation on every psychologist to know the Code thoroughly — a responsibility that begins in graduate training and continues throughout one's career. For EPPP purposes, remember that this applies equally to seasoned practitioners and recent graduates.

The distinction between ethical violations and legal liability is another frequently tested concept. An ethics board might find that a psychologist violated a standard, but that finding does not automatically mean the psychologist is liable in a civil lawsuit. Conversely, a court might find legal liability even when no ethical violation occurred. These are parallel but independent systems of accountability, and the EPPP expects you to understand that they operate on different tracks.

The use of qualifying language — words like "reasonably", "appropriate," and "potentially" — is intentional and clinically significant. These modifiers acknowledge that psychology is practiced in complex, often ambiguous situations where rigid rules cannot anticipate every scenario. However, this flexibility is not a blank check. A psychologist invoking professional judgment must be prepared to demonstrate that their decisions were thoughtful, informed by the available evidence, and defensible to a reasonable peer. Think of it as guided discretion rather than unchecked autonomy.

Perhaps the most practically consequential rule involves the interplay between ethical standards and legal requirements. The governing principle follows a "higher standard" rule: whichever authority — law or ethics — sets the more protective or stringent standard is the one the psychologist must follow. This creates a dynamic hierarchy that shifts depending on the specific situation, the jurisdiction, and the ethical standard at issue. When a genuine conflict exists that cannot be resolved by following the higher standard, psychologists are expected to make known their commitment to the Ethics Code and take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict in a manner consistent with the General Principles.

Example

A state law requires psychologists to report suspected child abuse. Even if a psychologist feels that reporting might damage the therapeutic relationship, the legal mandate overrides the ethical preference for maintaining confidentiality. Conversely, if the Ethics Code requires informed consent in a situation where local law is silent, the psychologist should still obtain informed consent. In both cases, the "higher standard" principle is at work — whichever authority provides greater protection to the public is the one the psychologist must honor. A useful test-taking heuristic: when an EPPP question presents a conflict between ethics and law, first determine which standard is more protective, and then select the answer that follows that higher standard.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • General Principles (A-E) are aspirational and not enforceable; Ethical Standards (1-10) are enforceable.
  • Principle A = Beneficence/Nonmaleficence; B = Fidelity/Responsibility; C = Integrity; D = Justice; E = Respect for Rights and Dignity.
  • When ethics conflict with law, clarify the conflict, advocate for the Ethics Code, and resolve consistent with the Code (Standard 1.02).
  • First response to learning of a colleague's ethical violation is usually informal resolution (Standard 1.04), unless it would violate confidentiality or the violation is severe.
  • The Canadian Code explicitly ranks its principles; the APA Code does not.
  • Consultation and documentation are essential components of ethical decision-making.
  • Virtue ethics complements rule-based codes by focusing on professional character.

Overview: Resolving Ethical Issues

Imagine this scenario: A psychologist working in a correctional facility is ordered by the warden to share confidential therapy notes for an upcoming parole hearing — without the client's consent. The psychologist knows that breaching confidentiality without proper authorization violates the APA Ethics Code, yet the institutional authority is pressing hard, and state regulations seem ambiguous on the matter. What should the psychologist do? Where does one even begin to navigate such a conflict? The answer lies in the very first standard of the APA Ethics Code, which was designed precisely for moments like these — moments when ethical principles collide with real-world pressures, legal mandates, or organizational demands.

Standard 1 of the APA Ethics Code addresses what psychologists should do when they encounter ethical problems, conflicts between ethics and law or organizational demands, or knowledge of others' ethical violations. Rather than offering a single rigid rule, this standard provides a structured framework — a decision-making roadmap — for working through the kinds of ethical dilemmas that inevitably arise across every domain of professional practice, from clinical work and research to consultation and forensic evaluation. It is foundational in the truest sense: without a clear process for resolving ethical tensions, every other standard in the Code would lack the mechanism for real-world application.

Think of Standard 1 as the "meta-standard" — it doesn't tell you what specific ethical behavior looks like in therapy, assessment, or research (those details come in later standards), but it tells you what to do when you're stuck, conflicted, or confronted with someone else's misconduct. It answers the question that every early-career psychologist eventually faces: "I know something isn't right here — now what?" The steps outlined in this standard include clarifying the conflict, attempting informal resolution when appropriate, consulting with colleagues or ethics committees, and — when necessary — taking more formal action such as filing an ethics complaint.

Consider another common scenario: A graduate student observes her clinical supervisor making dismissive, culturally insensitive remarks to a client during a therapy session. The student feels deeply uncomfortable but is uncertain whether this rises to the level of an ethical violation — and she worries about the power differential involved in confronting her supervisor. Standard 1 speaks directly to situations like these, offering guidance on when and how to address perceived violations by others while also recognizing the complexities of authority, loyalty, and professional risk. It acknowledges that resolving ethical issues is rarely a clean, mechanical process; it requires judgment, courage, and a willingness to engage with ambiguity.

Standard 1 is frequently tested on the EPPP because ethical conflicts are inherent in professional psychology, and knowing the proper steps for resolving them is essential. Exam questions often present nuanced vignettes in which law, organizational policy, and ethical obligations pull in different directions — and the correct answer hinges on understanding the resolution process that Standard 1 lays out. Mastering this standard doesn't just prepare you for the exam; it prepares you for the daily realities of ethical practice, where the "right thing to do" is sometimes unclear and the path forward must be carefully reasoned rather than simply memorized.

Standard 1: Resolving Ethical Issues

Imagine you're a psychologist working in a busy hospital system. One morning, you discover that an administrator has been selectively quoting your psychological evaluation to justify denying a patient's disability claim — twisting your clinical conclusions beyond recognition. That same week, a colleague confides that another psychologist on staff has been engaging in a dual relationship with a current client. And to complicate matters further, your supervisor asks you to release raw test data in a way that conflicts with APA ethical guidelines but aligns with a new state regulation. What do you do first? Who do you tell? What steps must you take, and in what order?

These are exactly the kinds of dilemmas that Standard 1 of the APA Ethics Code was designed to address. Standard 1 provides psychologists with a structured framework for navigating ethical problems, resolving conflicts between ethics and law or organizational demands, and responding appropriately when they learn of ethical violations committed by colleagues. It is, in many ways, the "meta-standard" — the standard about what to do when standards collide with reality. Understanding its provisions deeply is essential not only for the EPPP but for every day of professional practice.

1.01 Misuse of Psychologists' Work

Psychologists invest tremendous care in producing accurate assessments, research findings, and clinical reports. But once that work leaves their hands, others may distort it — sometimes carelessly, sometimes deliberately. A forensic evaluation might be quoted out of context in a custody hearing. A research finding might be cherry-picked by a media outlet to support a sensational headline. When psychologists learn that their work is being misused or misrepresented by others, Standard 1.01 requires them to take reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse or misrepresentation. "Reasonable steps" is key language here: the standard doesn't demand that psychologists achieve a perfect correction, but it does require active effort — contacting the misrepresenting party, issuing a clarifying statement, or reaching out to the audience that received the distorted information.

Consider a school psychologist who discovers that a principal has been using her psychoeducational report to justify placing a student in a more restrictive environment than the report actually recommended. Under Standard 1.01, the psychologist cannot simply shrug and move on. She is ethically obligated to take constructive action — perhaps requesting a meeting with the IEP team to clarify her findings, or providing a written addendum that accurately reflects her conclusions. The obligation is to act, not merely to feel troubled.

1.02 Conflicts Between Ethics and Law, Regulations, or Other Governing Legal Authority

One of the most challenging situations a psychologist can face is discovering that what the law demands and what the Ethics Code requires are pulling in opposite directions. Standard 1.02 provides a clear, multi-step process for these moments. When psychologists' ethical responsibilities conflict with law, regulations, or other governing legal authority, they must first clarify the nature of the conflict — articulating precisely which ethical standard is in tension with which legal requirement. Next, they make known their commitment to the Ethics Code to the relevant parties. Finally, they take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict in a manner consistent with the General Principles and Ethical Standards of the APA code. Critically, under no circumstances does this standard permit a psychologist to violate fundamental human rights, regardless of what a law or regulation may appear to demand.

This standard recognizes a difficult truth: psychologists do not operate in a vacuum. They practice within legal systems, governmental regulations, and institutional hierarchies that sometimes impose requirements at odds with ethical practice. The standard does not naively insist that ethics always trump law — but it does insist that psychologists engage the conflict transparently, advocate for ethical practice, and never become passive instruments of policies that violate human rights.

Example

A psychologist working in a correctional facility is ordered by administration to provide confidential therapy records to correctional officers. This conflicts with Standard 4 (Confidentiality). Under 1.02, the psychologist should clarify the conflict, advocate for confidentiality, and try to resolve the situation -- but cannot simply ignore the legal authority without taking constructive action.

1.03 Conflicts Between Ethics and Organizational Demands

Closely related to Standard 1.02, but distinct in important ways, Standard 1.03 addresses what happens when the conflict is not with the law but with an organization. Perhaps a managed care company demands that a psychologist limit treatment to four sessions regardless of clinical need, or a university department pressures a psychologist to alter research findings to secure continued funding. When the demands of an organization with which psychologists are affiliated conflict with the Ethics Code, psychologists must clarify the nature of the conflict, make known their commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict — the same three-step process as in 1.02.

However, Standard 1.03 includes an important escape valve: if the conflict is truly unresolvable despite good-faith efforts, the psychologist may adhere to the requirements of law, regulations, or other governing legal authority. Notice the careful language here — the psychologist may defer to legal authority, not simply to organizational preference. An organization's internal policy does not carry the same weight as law. This distinction matters on the EPPP: if an organization demands something unethical and there is no legal mandate backing the demand, the psychologist cannot simply comply and claim the conflict was unresolvable. Genuine, documented efforts at resolution are expected before any deference occurs.

1.04 Informal Resolution of Ethical Violations

Learning that a colleague may be violating the Ethics Code puts a psychologist in an uncomfortable but important position. The instinct might be to report the behavior immediately — or conversely, to look the other way. Standard 1.04 charts a middle course that respects collegiality while protecting the public. When psychologists believe there has been an ethical violation by another psychologist, they attempt to resolve the issue by bringing it to the attention of that individual, if an informal resolution appears appropriate and the intervention does not violate any confidentiality rights.

Think of it as the professional equivalent of a conversation before a confrontation. If you notice that a colleague has been inadvertently revealing client names in the break room, the ethical path typically begins with a private, respectful conversation with that colleague — not with an immediate complaint to the licensing board. This approach preserves professional relationships, allows for the possibility that the violation was unintentional, and often produces faster correction than formal proceedings. However, informal resolution has clear boundaries: it is not appropriate when the violation is serious enough to cause substantial harm (such as a sexual relationship with a client), and it must never be pursued if doing so would breach someone's confidentiality.

EPPP Tip

On the EPPP, when asked "what should a psychologist do first?" upon learning a colleague may be acting unethically, the answer is usually to attempt informal resolution (1.04) -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution (e.g., sexual contact with a client).

1.05 Reporting Ethical Violations

When informal resolution is not appropriate — either because the violation is too serious or because an informal attempt has already failed — Standard 1.05 provides the next step. If an apparent ethical violation has substantially harmed or is likely to substantially harm a person or organization and is not appropriate for informal resolution, psychologists take further action such as referral to state or national ethics committees or licensing boards. This is the formal reporting pathway, reserved for situations where the stakes are high and collegial conversation is insufficient or inappropriate.

However, Standard 1.05 includes two important exceptions. First, it does not apply when reporting the violation would itself violate someone's confidentiality rights — for instance, if you learned about a colleague's misconduct through a therapy session with the colleague's client. Second, it does not apply when psychologists have been retained specifically to review the work of another psychologist whose professional conduct is already in question — a carve-out designed to protect the integrity of expert review processes. Understanding these exceptions is critical for EPPP preparation, as test items often present scenarios designed to test whether you know when reporting is required versus when it would be ethically inappropriate.

1.06 Cooperating with Ethics Committees

Once an ethics investigation is underway, the process depends on the cooperation of the professionals involved. Standard 1.06 establishes that psychologists cooperate in ethics investigations, proceedings, and resulting requirements of the APA or any affiliated state psychological association to which they belong. This is not a polite suggestion — failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation. The rationale is straightforward: the self-regulatory system that protects both the public and the profession can only function if psychologists participate in it honestly and fully. A psychologist who stonewalls an ethics committee undermines the entire framework of professional accountability, and the Ethics Code treats that obstruction with corresponding seriousness.

1.07 Improper Complaints

The ethics complaint system is a powerful tool — and like any powerful tool, it can be weaponized. Standard 1.07 guards against this possibility by prohibiting psychologists from filing or encouraging the filing of ethics complaints that are made with reckless disregard for or willful ignorance of facts that would disprove the allegation. In practice, this means that a psychologist who files a retaliatory complaint against a colleague — knowing full well the allegation is baseless — is committing an ethical violation. The standard protects the integrity of the complaint process and shields innocent psychologists from malicious or careless accusations by their peers.

Note the precise language: the standard targets "reckless disregard" and "willful ignorance," not honest mistakes. A psychologist who files a complaint in good faith based on available information, only to have the complaint later dismissed, has not violated Standard 1.07. The standard addresses intent and negligence, not outcomes.

1.08 Unfair Discrimination Against Complainants and Respondents

Filing or being the subject of an ethics complaint is stressful enough without facing professional retaliation on top of it. Standard 1.08 addresses this concern directly: psychologists do not deny persons employment, advancement, admissions, or other benefits solely because they have made or been the subject of an ethics complaint. This protection runs in both directions — it shields both the person who filed the complaint and the person accused. A training director, for example, cannot refuse to admit an otherwise qualified applicant simply because that applicant once filed an ethics complaint against a former supervisor, nor can a department chair refuse to promote a colleague solely because a complaint was once filed against them.

However, the standard includes an important qualifier: it does not preclude taking action based on the outcome of ethics proceedings. If a formal investigation results in a finding of misconduct and specific sanctions, those outcomes can legitimately factor into employment and advancement decisions. The distinction is between the mere existence of a complaint — which should not be held against anyone — and the substantiated results of a completed investigation, which may be appropriately considered. This nuance is worth internalizing for both the EPPP and for the ethical practice it tests.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • When ethics conflict with law, psychologists must clarify the conflict, make known their commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve it (Standard 1.02). This never permits violating human rights.
  • The first response to learning of a colleague's ethical violation is usually informal resolution (Standard 1.04) -- bringing it to the colleague's attention -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution.
  • When informal resolution is not appropriate, the psychologist should take further action such as referral to an ethics committee or licensing board (Standard 1.05).
  • Cooperating with ethics investigations is mandatory; failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation (Standard 1.06).
  • Filing an ethics complaint with reckless disregard for the facts is prohibited (Standard 1.07).
  • Psychologists may not retaliate against those who file ethics complaints (Standard 1.08).
  • If a psychologist's work is being misused or misrepresented, they must take reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse (Standard 1.01).
