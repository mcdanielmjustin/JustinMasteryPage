Introduction to the APA Ethics Code

A psychologist receives an urgent call from a former client's parent, asking the psychologist to release therapy records to an attorney involved in a custody dispute. The psychologist hesitates — she wants to help, but releasing the records without the client's consent could violate confidentiality, and the parent's request raises questions about who the actual client is and whose interests are being served. In this moment, she needs more than good intentions; she needs a structured ethical framework that distinguishes aspirational ideals from enforceable standards and provides clear guidance for navigating competing obligations. That framework is the APA Ethics Code.

The Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (commonly called the APA Ethics Code) was most recently revised in 2017 and serves as the primary ethical guide for psychologists in the United States. It governs the professional, scientific, and educational activities of psychologists and applies to all APA members regardless of their role. The Ethics Code is structured to include both broad aspirational goals and specific enforceable rules. Understanding this dual nature is critical for the EPPP, as exam questions frequently test whether a given statement is aspirational or enforceable.

Key Distinction

Aspirational principles (General Principles A-E) describe the highest ideals of the profession but are not enforceable. Enforceable standards (Standards 1-10) set minimum behavioral expectations and can be used as the basis for ethics complaints and disciplinary action.

Structure of the Ethics Code

Now that we've distinguished between aspirational principles and enforceable standards, let's zoom out and look at how the entire Ethics Code is organized — because understanding the architecture of the Code helps you navigate it efficiently, both in practice and on the EPPP. Think of the Code as a building: the Preamble lays the foundation and explains why the building exists, the General Principles describe the aspirational vision for what the building should look like at its best, the Ethical Standards provide the load-bearing walls that must not be compromised, and the Introduction and Applicability section tells you who must follow the building codes and under what circumstances. Each component serves a distinct purpose, and exam questions frequently test whether you know which part does what.

The APA Ethics Code consists of four major components, each playing a specific role in guiding professional conduct:

  1. Preamble -- States the purpose and scope of the Ethics Code. It establishes that psychologists commit to increasing knowledge, improving conditions, and helping the public develop informed judgments about behavior.
  2. General Principles (A through E) -- Aspirational goals that guide psychologists toward the highest ideals. They are not enforceable but provide a framework for ethical reasoning.
  3. Ethical Standards (1 through 10) -- Enforceable rules that set mandatory obligations. Violations can result in sanctions, license revocation, or expulsion from APA.
  4. Introduction and Applicability -- Clarifies how the Code applies, its relationship to law, and issues of jurisdiction.

Notice the critical distinction embedded in this structure: the General Principles set the ceiling — the highest ideals a psychologist should strive for — while the Ethical Standards set the floor — the minimum behaviors that are required. A psychologist who violates an Ethical Standard can face formal disciplinary action, but a psychologist who falls short of a General Principle cannot be sanctioned on that basis alone. This distinction is one of the most frequently tested concepts on the EPPP, so it is worth anchoring firmly in your understanding before moving forward.

Preamble & Introduction
Purpose, scope, and applicability of the Code

The Preamble

The Preamble describes the mission of psychology and the purpose of the Ethics Code. It acknowledges the breadth of psychological roles — researcher, educator, therapist, supervisor, consultant, forensic expert — and that ethical obligations apply to all of them.

A critical nuance: the Code applies only in the context of scientific, educational, or professional activities. A psychologist's behavior at a family reunion is generally outside its scope, but behavior at a professional conference or in session is squarely within it. This boundary matters for EPPP scenarios where personal and professional conduct blur.

General Principles (Aspirational)

The five General Principles guide psychologists toward ethical ideals — practice that not only avoids harm but actively promotes human welfare, trust, honesty, fairness, and dignity. For the EPPP, know that they are aspirational, not enforceable — no psychologist can be sanctioned solely for falling short of a General Principle, but these principles inform interpretation of the enforceable standards. A helpful mnemonic: B-FI-JR (Beneficence, Fidelity, Integrity, Justice, Respect).

PrincipleCore FocusKey Idea
A: Beneficence and NonmaleficenceDo good; do no harmPsychologists strive to benefit those with whom they work and guard against factors that might lead to misuse of influence. They manage personal, financial, social, organizational, or political factors that could lead to harm.
B: Fidelity and ResponsibilityTrust, professional responsibilityPsychologists establish relationships of trust, are aware of their responsibilities to society and the communities they serve, and consult with colleagues to prevent unethical conduct. They donate a portion of professional time for little or no compensation (pro bono).
C: IntegrityHonesty, truthfulnessPsychologists seek to promote accuracy, honesty, and truthfulness. They do not steal, cheat, or engage in fraud, subterfuge, or intentional misrepresentation of fact. They keep promises and avoid unwise or unclear commitments.
D: JusticeFairness, equal accessPsychologists recognize that fairness and justice entitle all persons to access the contributions of psychology and to equal quality in processes, procedures, and services. They exercise reasonable judgment and take precautions to ensure bias does not lead to unjust practices.
E: Respect for People's Rights and DignityAutonomy, privacy, diversityPsychologists respect the dignity and worth of all people and the rights of individuals to privacy, confidentiality, and self-determination. They are aware of cultural, individual, and role differences including those based on age, gender, race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, sexual orientation, disability, language, and socioeconomic status.

To bring these principles to life, consider a clinical scenario: a psychologist working in a rural community discovers that her only colleague in town has been practicing while impaired due to substance use. Principle A (Beneficence and Nonmaleficence) compels her to consider the potential harm to clients. Principle B (Fidelity and Responsibility) reminds her of her obligation to address unethical conduct among colleagues — not to look the other way. Principle C (Integrity) requires her to be honest in any reporting or consultation she undertakes. Principle D (Justice) asks her to consider whether vulnerable populations in the community are being disproportionately affected. And Principle E (Respect for People's Rights and Dignity) requires her to handle the situation in a way that respects the dignity of both the impaired colleague and the affected clients. In this single scenario, all five principles are at play — which illustrates why understanding them as an interconnected framework, rather than isolated definitions, is so valuable.

EPPP Tip

A common EPPP question format presents a scenario and asks which General Principle applies. Remember: Principle B (Fidelity and Responsibility) is the one that mentions pro bono work and concern for colleagues' ethical behavior. Principle D (Justice) specifically addresses fairness and bias. When two principles seem to apply, focus on which principle most directly addresses the specific ethical concern described in the question stem — the EPPP rewards precision in matching principles to scenarios.

Ethical Standards Overview (Standards 1-10)

Now that we've explored the aspirational General Principles — the moral compass of the profession — it's time to turn to the part of the Ethics Code that has real teeth. Imagine a psychologist who maintains a sexual relationship with a current therapy client, or a researcher who fabricates data. These aren't just violations of lofty ideals; they are breaches of specific, enforceable rules that can result in formal sanctions, loss of licensure, or expulsion from the APA. The ten Ethical Standards are where the Ethics Code shifts from aspiration to obligation, spelling out precisely what psychologists must and must not do in their professional conduct.

Unlike the General Principles (which cannot be the basis for an ethics complaint), the Ethical Standards are the enforceable component of the Ethics Code. Each standard contains multiple subsections addressing specific behaviors and obligations across the full range of professional activities — from how you obtain informed consent, to when you may break confidentiality, to how authorship credit should be assigned on a publication. On the EPPP, you will encounter scenarios that require you to identify which specific standard applies, so familiarity with the scope of each one is essential. Think of the ten standards as ten rooms in a house: each room covers a distinct domain of practice, and knowing which room you're in helps you quickly locate the relevant rule.

The table below provides a high-level map of all ten standards. As you review it, notice how the standards are organized roughly by context — beginning with how to handle ethical dilemmas themselves (Standard 1), moving through the foundations of competent and respectful practice (Standards 2–4), and then addressing specific professional activities such as advertising, record keeping, teaching, research, assessment, and therapy (Standards 5–10). Each of these standards will receive detailed coverage in the sections that follow, but this overview will serve as your orienting framework — the table of contents for the enforceable ethical obligations that govern every facet of psychological practice.

Detailed Coverage of Each Standard

Now that you have a panoramic view of the ten Ethical Standards and their enforceable role in the Ethics Code, you may be wondering: how deeply should I understand each one? The answer — especially for the EPPP — is thoroughly. Each standard governs a distinct domain of professional conduct, from how you handle ethical dilemmas to how you conduct therapy, and each carries its own nuances, gray areas, and clinical implications that deserve careful exploration. Rather than compressing all of that complexity into a single chapter, this textbook dedicates an entire chapter to each standard so you can engage with the material at the depth the exam and your future practice demand.

The next chapter begins with Standard 1 (Resolving Ethical Issues), which fittingly addresses what psychologists should do when they encounter or suspect ethical violations — a natural starting point for any clinician building an ethical decision-making framework. From there, Standards 2 through 8 each receive dedicated chapters in Part A of this domain, walking you through competence, human relations, privacy and confidentiality, advertising, record keeping, education and training, and research. Standards 9 (Assessment) and 10 (Therapy) — the two standards you will encounter most frequently in day-to-day clinical work — also receive their own separate, in-depth coverage. Think of the chapters ahead as a guided tour through each standard, complete with clinical scenarios, common exam pitfalls, and the kind of concrete examples that transform abstract rules into intuitive professional habits.

Ethics Decision-Making Models

The Ethics Code alone does not resolve every ethical dilemma. Several decision-making models provide structured approaches for navigating complex situations.

Koocher and Keith-Spiegel's Model

  1. Determine whether the matter is an ethical one
  2. Consult the Ethics Code and relevant guidelines
  3. Consider applicable laws and regulations
  4. Consult with colleagues or ethics committees
  5. Evaluate the rights, responsibilities, and welfare of all affected parties
  6. Generate alternative solutions
  7. Enumerate the consequences of each decision
  8. Make the decision
  9. Implement the decision and follow up

Canadian Code Model (Sinclair et al.)

The Canadian Psychological Association's four-principle model ranks ethical principles hierarchically when they conflict:

  1. Respect for the Dignity of Persons (highest priority)
  2. Responsible Caring
  3. Integrity in Relationships
  4. Responsibility to Society (lowest priority)
Key Difference

Unlike the APA Ethics Code (which does not rank its General Principles), the Canadian Code explicitly prioritizes principles so that when two principles conflict, the higher-ranked principle takes precedence.

The Role of Consultation

Across all decision-making models, consultation with knowledgeable colleagues is strongly emphasized. When facing an ethical dilemma, psychologists should document the steps taken, the reasoning behind their decision, and any consultations obtained. This documentation serves as evidence of the psychologist's good-faith effort to act ethically.

Virtue Ethics Approach

Some ethicists (e.g., Meara, Schmidt, & Day, 1996) argue that ethical codes focused solely on rules are insufficient. A virtue ethics approach asks not just "what should I do?" but "what kind of person should I be?" The virtues of prudence, integrity, respectfulness, and benevolence complement the enforceable standards of the Ethics Code.

Applicability and Enforcement

To whom does the Ethics Code apply, and what happens when standards collide with law? A psychologist with controversial personal social media posts, or a clinician whose state's mandatory reporting laws seem to conflict with ethical confidentiality standards, both need clear answers. Key scope and enforcement points frequently tested on the EPPP:

  • The Ethics Code applies to APA members and to psychologists licensed under state laws that adopt the Code, covering professional, scientific, and educational activities.
  • The Code does not apply to psychologists' purely private conduct that is not connected to their professional roles.
  • Lack of awareness or misunderstanding of an Ethical Standard is not a defense to a charge of unethical conduct.
  • The Ethics Code is not intended to be a basis of civil liability; whether a psychologist has violated the Ethics Code does not itself determine legal liability.
  • The modifiers "reasonably," "appropriate," and "potentially" in the Standards allow for professional judgment but require that the psychologist's actions be justifiable given the circumstances.
  • When the Ethics Code establishes a higher standard than the law, psychologists must meet the ethical standard. When the law establishes a higher standard, psychologists must comply with the law.

The "ignorance is no excuse" principle deserves emphasis: a psychologist who has never read Standard 3.05 on multiple relationships cannot cite unfamiliarity as a defense. This affirmative obligation to know the Code begins in training and continues throughout one's career.

Ethical violations and legal liability operate on parallel but independent tracks. An ethics board finding does not automatically create civil liability; a court finding of liability does not require an ethical violation. The EPPP expects you to understand this separation.

Qualifying language — "reasonably", "appropriate," "potentially" — acknowledges that rigid rules cannot anticipate every situation. However, this flexibility is guided discretion, not a blank check: psychologists must be prepared to demonstrate that their decisions were thoughtful and defensible to a reasonable peer.

The most practically consequential rule is the "higher standard" principle: whichever authority — law or ethics — sets the more protective standard is the one the psychologist must follow. When genuine conflict remains, psychologists make known their commitment to the Ethics Code and take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict consistent with the General Principles.

Example

A state law requires psychologists to report suspected child abuse. Even if a psychologist feels that reporting might damage the therapeutic relationship, the legal mandate overrides the ethical preference for maintaining confidentiality. Conversely, if the Ethics Code requires informed consent in a situation where local law is silent, the psychologist should still obtain informed consent. In both cases, the "higher standard" principle is at work — whichever authority provides greater protection to the public is the one the psychologist must honor. A useful test-taking heuristic: when an EPPP question presents a conflict between ethics and law, first determine which standard is more protective, and then select the answer that follows that higher standard.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • General Principles (A-E) are aspirational and not enforceable; Ethical Standards (1-10) are enforceable.
  • Principle A = Beneficence/Nonmaleficence; B = Fidelity/Responsibility; C = Integrity; D = Justice; E = Respect for Rights and Dignity.
  • When ethics conflict with law, clarify the conflict, advocate for the Ethics Code, and resolve consistent with the Code (Standard 1.02).
  • First response to learning of a colleague's ethical violation is usually informal resolution (Standard 1.04), unless it would violate confidentiality or the violation is severe.
  • The Canadian Code explicitly ranks its principles; the APA Code does not.
  • Consultation and documentation are essential components of ethical decision-making.
  • Virtue ethics complements rule-based codes by focusing on professional character.

Standard 1: Resolving Ethical Issues

A psychologist in a hospital discovers that an administrator has been selectively quoting her evaluation to deny a patient's disability claim. That same week, she learns a colleague has a dual relationship with a current client. Her supervisor asks her to release raw test data in a way that conflicts with APA guidelines but aligns with a new state regulation. What does she do first? This is Standard 1's domain — the "meta-standard" that tells psychologists what to do when ethical principles collide with real-world pressures.

Standard 1 provides a structured framework for navigating ethical problems, resolving conflicts between ethics and law, and responding to colleagues' violations. It answers the question every early-career psychologist faces: "I know something isn't right here — now what?" The decision path moves from identifying the conflict, through informal resolution, to formal reporting when necessary. The following visual aid maps this progression.

1.01 Misuse of Psychologists' Work

When psychologists learn their work is being misused or misrepresented — a forensic evaluation quoted out of context, a research finding cherry-picked for a sensational headline — Standard 1.01 requires reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse. "Reasonable steps" means active effort: contacting the misrepresenting party, issuing a clarifying statement, or reaching out to affected audiences. A school psychologist who discovers a principal is using her report to justify a more restrictive placement than recommended must take constructive action, not merely feel troubled.

1.02 Conflicts Between Ethics and Law, Regulations, or Other Governing Legal Authority

When ethical responsibilities conflict with law or regulations, Standard 1.02 prescribes a three-step process: (1) clarify the nature of the conflict, (2) make known your commitment to the Ethics Code, and (3) take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict consistent with the General Principles. Critically, this standard never permits violating fundamental human rights, regardless of what a law may demand. Psychologists must engage the conflict transparently and advocate for ethical practice — never becoming passive instruments of policies that violate human rights.

Example

A psychologist working in a correctional facility is ordered by administration to provide confidential therapy records to correctional officers. This conflicts with Standard 4 (Confidentiality). Under 1.02, the psychologist should clarify the conflict, advocate for confidentiality, and try to resolve the situation -- but cannot simply ignore the legal authority without taking constructive action.

1.03 Conflicts Between Ethics and Organizational Demands

Standard 1.03 addresses conflicts with organizations rather than law — a managed care company limiting treatment to four sessions regardless of clinical need, or a university pressuring altered research findings. The same three-step process from 1.02 applies. However, if truly unresolvable, the psychologist may defer to legal authority — not organizational preference. An organization's internal policy does not carry the same weight as law: if no legal mandate backs the demand, the psychologist cannot simply comply. Genuine, documented resolution efforts are expected first.

1.04 Informal Resolution of Ethical Violations

When psychologists believe a colleague has committed an ethical violation, they attempt informal resolution first — bringing it to the colleague's attention directly, provided informal resolution appears appropriate and would not violate confidentiality rights. A colleague inadvertently revealing client names in the break room warrants a private conversation, not an immediate licensing board complaint. This approach preserves relationships and allows for the possibility the violation was unintentional. However, informal resolution is inappropriate when the violation could cause substantial harm (such as sexual contact with a client) or when addressing it would breach confidentiality.

EPPP Tip

On the EPPP, when asked "what should a psychologist do first?" upon learning a colleague may be acting unethically, the answer is usually to attempt informal resolution (1.04) -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution (e.g., sexual contact with a client).

1.05 Reporting Ethical Violations

When informal resolution is inappropriate or has failed, Standard 1.05 requires further action — referral to ethics committees or licensing boards — if the violation has substantially harmed or is likely to substantially harm a person or organization. Two important exceptions: (1) reporting is not required when it would itself violate someone's confidentiality rights, and (2) it does not apply when a psychologist has been retained to review another psychologist's work already under question. EPPP items frequently test these boundaries.

1.06 Cooperating with Ethics Committees

Once an ethics investigation is underway, the process depends on the cooperation of the professionals involved. Standard 1.06 establishes that psychologists cooperate in ethics investigations, proceedings, and resulting requirements of the APA or any affiliated state psychological association to which they belong. This is not a polite suggestion — failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation. The rationale is straightforward: the self-regulatory system that protects both the public and the profession can only function if psychologists participate in it honestly and fully. A psychologist who stonewalls an ethics committee undermines the entire framework of professional accountability, and the Ethics Code treats that obstruction with corresponding seriousness.

1.07 Improper Complaints

The ethics complaint system is a powerful tool — and like any powerful tool, it can be weaponized. Standard 1.07 guards against this possibility by prohibiting psychologists from filing or encouraging the filing of ethics complaints that are made with reckless disregard for or willful ignorance of facts that would disprove the allegation. In practice, this means that a psychologist who files a retaliatory complaint against a colleague — knowing full well the allegation is baseless — is committing an ethical violation. The standard protects the integrity of the complaint process and shields innocent psychologists from malicious or careless accusations by their peers.

Note the precise language: the standard targets "reckless disregard" and "willful ignorance," not honest mistakes. A psychologist who files a complaint in good faith based on available information, only to have the complaint later dismissed, has not violated Standard 1.07. The standard addresses intent and negligence, not outcomes.

1.08 Unfair Discrimination Against Complainants and Respondents

Filing or being the subject of an ethics complaint is stressful enough without facing professional retaliation on top of it. Standard 1.08 addresses this concern directly: psychologists do not deny persons employment, advancement, admissions, or other benefits solely because they have made or been the subject of an ethics complaint. This protection runs in both directions — it shields both the person who filed the complaint and the person accused. A training director, for example, cannot refuse to admit an otherwise qualified applicant simply because that applicant once filed an ethics complaint against a former supervisor, nor can a department chair refuse to promote a colleague solely because a complaint was once filed against them.

However, the standard includes an important qualifier: it does not preclude taking action based on the outcome of ethics proceedings. If a formal investigation results in a finding of misconduct and specific sanctions, those outcomes can legitimately factor into employment and advancement decisions. The distinction is between the mere existence of a complaint — which should not be held against anyone — and the substantiated results of a completed investigation, which may be appropriately considered. This nuance is worth internalizing for both the EPPP and for the ethical practice it tests.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • When ethics conflict with law, psychologists must clarify the conflict, make known their commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve it (Standard 1.02). This never permits violating human rights.
  • The first response to learning of a colleague's ethical violation is usually informal resolution (Standard 1.04) -- bringing it to the colleague's attention -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution.
  • When informal resolution is not appropriate, the psychologist should take further action such as referral to an ethics committee or licensing board (Standard 1.05).
  • Cooperating with ethics investigations is mandatory; failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation (Standard 1.06).
  • Filing an ethics complaint with reckless disregard for the facts is prohibited (Standard 1.07).
  • Psychologists may not retaliate against those who file ethics complaints (Standard 1.08).
  • If a psychologist's work is being misused or misrepresented, they must take reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse (Standard 1.01).
