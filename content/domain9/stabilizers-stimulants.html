Mood Stabilizers

A 29-year-old graphic designer is admitted to the inpatient psychiatry unit after a week-long manic episode during which she maxed out three credit cards, slept fewer than two hours a night, and began writing what she described as a "revolutionary" novel at a frenetic pace. Her speech is rapid and pressured, she jumps between grandiose plans, and she insists nothing is wrong. Her treatment team starts lithium — and over the next ten days, the mania gradually recedes. But managing lithium, they know, will demand a level of pharmacological vigilance unlike any other psychiatric medication: narrow therapeutic margins, mandatory blood monitoring, and a list of interactions that can turn a stable patient into an emergency in a matter of days.

Mood stabilizers are used primarily for bipolar disorder to treat acute mania, prevent mood episodes, and stabilize mood fluctuations. Lithium is the classic mood stabilizer, but several anticonvulsant medications also serve this function. Understanding the unique monitoring requirements and risks associated with each agent is essential for the EPPP.

While lithium has historically been the primary pharmacological intervention for acute mania in bipolar disorder, carbamazepine and valproic acid (valproate) have emerged as well-established alternatives that are now considered first-line options for managing manic episodes. Both agents were originally developed as anticonvulsant (antiepileptic) medications, and their mood-stabilizing properties were discovered through clinical observation and subsequent controlled trials. Current treatment guidelines recognize all three agents—lithium, valproate, and carbamazepine—as appropriate first-line choices for acute mania, with the selection among them guided by the patient's symptom profile, episode subtype, medical comorbidities, and tolerability considerations. Valproate may be preferred for mixed episodes and rapid cycling, while carbamazepine offers particular advantages for patients with comorbid pain syndromes or those who have not responded adequately to lithium or valproate.

Lithium (Eskalith, Lithobid)

Lithium remains the gold standard treatment for bipolar disorder, particularly for classic (euphoric) mania. It is also the only mood stabilizer with strong evidence for reducing suicidality.

  • Mechanism: Not fully understood; may affect second messenger systems (inositol phosphate pathway), modulate neurotransmitter release, and influence gene expression
  • Therapeutic level: 0.6-1.2 mEq/L (narrow therapeutic index -- levels must be monitored regularly)
  • Toxicity: Levels above 1.5 mEq/L can be dangerous; above 2.0 mEq/L is a medical emergency

Signs of Lithium Toxicity

  • Mild (1.5-2.0): Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, tremor, drowsiness, muscle weakness
  • Moderate (2.0-2.5): Confusion, ataxia, coarse tremor, fasciculations, dysarthria
  • Severe (>2.5): Seizures, renal failure, coma, death

Side Effects and Monitoring

  • Renal: Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (polyuria, polydipsia); long-term kidney damage possible
  • Thyroid: Hypothyroidism (20-30% of patients); monitor TSH regularly
  • Cardiac: Benign T-wave flattening; rare cardiac conduction issues
  • Tremor: Fine hand tremor (common); managed with beta-blockers
  • Weight gain
  • Teratogenicity: Ebstein's anomaly (cardiac malformation in the fetus); contraindicated in first trimester of pregnancy
  • Dehydration, NSAIDs, and low-sodium diets can increase lithium levels to toxic ranges
Lithium Monitoring

Baseline labs before starting lithium include: renal function (BUN, creatinine), thyroid function (TSH), CBC, EKG, and pregnancy test. Lithium levels are drawn 12 hours after the last dose (trough level). Regular monitoring of renal and thyroid function is required throughout treatment.

Valproate / Valproic Acid (Depakote, Depakene)

  • Mechanism: Increases GABA levels; blocks voltage-gated sodium channels
  • Uses: Bipolar disorder (especially mixed episodes and rapid cycling), seizure disorders, migraine prophylaxis
  • Side effects: GI disturbances, weight gain, tremor, hair loss (alopecia), sedation
  • Serious risks: Hepatotoxicity (liver failure, especially in children under 2); pancreatitis; neural tube defects in pregnancy (spina bifida -- Category X, contraindicated in pregnancy)
  • Monitoring: Liver function tests, CBC (thrombocytopenia risk), valproate levels

Carbamazepine (Tegretol)

  • Mechanism: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels
  • Uses: Bipolar disorder, seizure disorders, trigeminal neuralgia
  • Side effects: Dizziness, drowsiness, ataxia, diplopia, nausea
  • Serious risks: Aplastic anemia and agranulocytosis (requires CBC monitoring); Stevens-Johnson syndrome (severe skin reaction); hepatotoxicity; neural tube defects in pregnancy
  • Drug interactions: Potent inducer of CYP3A4 enzymes; reduces effectiveness of oral contraceptives and many other medications; also induces its own metabolism (autoinduction)

Lamotrigine (Lamictal)

  • Mechanism: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels; modulates glutamate release
  • Uses: Bipolar disorder maintenance (especially effective for preventing bipolar depression); seizure disorders
  • Advantages: Does not cause weight gain; may improve cognition; effective for bipolar depression
  • Serious risk: Stevens-Johnson syndrome (potentially fatal skin reaction); risk is minimized by very slow dose titration; risk increases with rapid titration or concurrent valproate use
EPPP Tip

Key associations: Lithium = thyroid and kidney monitoring + Ebstein's anomaly. Valproate = hepatotoxicity + neural tube defects + contraindicated in pregnancy. Carbamazepine = aplastic anemia + Stevens-Johnson syndrome + enzyme induction. Lamotrigine = Stevens-Johnson syndrome + slow titration required + best for bipolar depression prevention.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Lithium has a narrow therapeutic index (0.6-1.2 mEq/L); requires monitoring of renal and thyroid function; causes Ebstein's anomaly in pregnancy.
  • Valproate causes neural tube defects (Category X in pregnancy) and hepatotoxicity.
  • Carbamazepine causes aplastic anemia, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and induces CYP enzymes (many drug interactions, including autoinduction).
  • Lamotrigine must be titrated slowly to prevent Stevens-Johnson syndrome; most effective mood stabilizer for bipolar depression prevention.
  • Lithium is the only mood stabilizer with strong evidence for reducing suicidality.
  • Dehydration, NSAIDs, and low-sodium diets can increase lithium to toxic levels.

Psychostimulants

Having examined mood stabilizers — medications that dampen excessive neural activity to control mania and prevent mood cycling — we now turn to a pharmacological class that operates in the opposite direction. Stimulant medications increase catecholamine activity in the brain, specifically targeting the dopamine and norepinephrine systems that govern attention, executive function, and impulse control. While this might seem counterintuitive for treating a disorder characterized by hyperactivity, the paradox resolves when we understand that ADHD involves underactivation of the prefrontal cortex — the very brain region responsible for behavioral inhibition and sustained attention.

Stimulant medications are the first-line pharmacological treatment for Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). They work by increasing dopamine and norepinephrine activity in the prefrontal cortex, effectively correcting the executive function deficit that underlies the disorder's core symptoms.

Side Effects of Stimulants

A clinically important question is whether prescribing stimulant medications to children with ADHD affects their likelihood of developing a substance use disorder (SUD) later in life. The research on this topic has yielded mixed findings. One influential meta-analysis concluded that treating childhood ADHD with stimulant drugs neither raised nor lowered the risk of subsequent SUD development, suggesting a neutral long-term effect. However, more recent research has introduced greater nuance. A subsequent study found that the age at which treatment begins and the initial dosage may be critical moderating factors: children with ADHD who started stimulant pharmacotherapy at a younger age and at higher doses showed a reduced likelihood of later developing an SUD compared to those who began treatment when they were older and at lower doses. This pattern may reflect the benefits of early, adequate intervention in normalizing reward circuitry and reducing the impulsivity and self-medication behaviors that can serve as pathways to substance misuse. Clinicians should be aware that while stimulant treatment does not appear to create addiction risk in appropriately diagnosed children, the timing and adequacy of treatment initiation may carry protective significance.

Example

A child diagnosed with ADHD at age 6 who begins treatment with an adequate dose of methylphenidate may be at lower risk for adolescent substance misuse than a peer who is not diagnosed until age 12 and starts on a minimal dose. This finding supports the clinical rationale for early identification and appropriately dosed pharmacological intervention.

  • Decreased appetite and weight loss
  • Insomnia (especially if taken late in the day)
  • Increased heart rate and blood pressure
  • Irritability, anxiety, or mood lability
  • Growth suppression in children (controversial; may slow growth velocity but generally does not affect final adult height)
  • Potential for abuse and dependence (Schedule II controlled substances)
  • Rebound effects when medication wears off

Atomoxetine (Strattera)

Atomoxetine is a non-stimulant medication for ADHD:

  • Mechanism: Selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (NRI)
  • Advantages: Not a controlled substance; no abuse potential; useful for patients with comorbid substance use disorders or anxiety; continuous 24-hour coverage
  • Disadvantages: Takes 4-6 weeks for full effect; less effective than stimulants for many patients; FDA black box warning for suicidal ideation in children and adolescents
  • Side effects: GI upset, decreased appetite, dizziness, mood changes, potential hepatotoxicity (rare)

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

Having explored the mechanisms and clinical applications of psychostimulants and non-stimulant alternatives like atomoxetine, it's time to consolidate the high-yield concepts most likely to appear on the EPPP. This section brings together several important findings that bridge psychopharmacology with broader clinical knowledge — including a surprising serotonin pattern in autism spectrum disorder, the off-label use of antipsychotics for tic disorders, and a cautionary finding about stimulant misuse in college populations. Each of these topics represents a point where pharmacological reasoning intersects with clinical judgment, making them prime material for exam questions.

One of the most counterintuitive findings in biological psychiatry involves serotonin and autism. You might expect that a neurotransmitter abnormality would go in one direction — either too much or too little. But in autism spectrum disorder, the picture is more complex: serotonin levels are simultaneously low in the brain and high in the blood. This paradox has important implications for understanding both the neurobiology and the pharmacological treatment of ASD, and it's the kind of detail the EPPP may test.

Serotonin Dysregulation in Autism Spectrum Disorder

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has been associated with a distinctive pattern of serotonin abnormality: individuals with ASD tend to show low serotonin levels in the brain alongside elevated serotonin levels in the blood (a phenomenon sometimes called hyperserotonemia). Peripheral hyperserotonemia—meaning high platelet serotonin concentrations—is one of the most consistently replicated biomarker findings in autism research, observed in approximately 25–30% of individuals with ASD. The simultaneous central serotonin deficit is thought to contribute to the behavioral rigidity, repetitive behaviors, and social communication difficulties characteristic of the disorder. This paradoxical pattern reflects the complexity of serotonergic regulation across different bodily compartments, as brain serotonin synthesis and peripheral serotonin storage operate through partially independent mechanisms. The clinical relevance of this finding is reflected in the use of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) to target repetitive behaviors and anxiety in some individuals with ASD, though treatment response is variable.

Why does this paradox exist? The key lies in understanding that the brain and the rest of the body manage serotonin through partially independent systems. Most of the body's serotonin — roughly 95% — is produced in the gut and stored in blood platelets, which actively take up serotonin from the bloodstream using the same transporter (SERT) that neurons use in the brain. Genetic or functional variations in SERT activity, as well as differences in serotonin synthesis pathways, may produce elevated peripheral levels while leaving central serotonin depleted. Think of it as two separate reservoirs fed by different streams: one can overflow while the other runs dry. For exam purposes, remember the mnemonic "Brain Low, Blood High" when thinking about the serotonergic profile of ASD.

When most students think of antipsychotic medications, they think of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. But the EPPP also expects you to know that these dopamine-blocking agents serve important roles outside of psychosis — including one you might not initially expect: the suppression of tics. Understanding why antipsychotics work for tics reinforces a broader principle about how pharmacological mechanisms can be repurposed across seemingly different conditions when the underlying neurotransmitter pathology overlaps.

Antipsychotics and Tic Suppression

Tic disorders, including Tourette's disorder, involve involuntary motor or vocal behaviors that are associated with dysregulation of dopaminergic pathways in the basal ganglia. Because excessive dopamine activity in these brain regions contributes to tic generation, antipsychotic medications that block dopamine receptors have proven effective for tic suppression. Traditional (first-generation) antipsychotics such as haloperidol and pimozide have long been used for this purpose, and certain second-generation antipsychotics (e.g., risperidone, aripiprazole) have also demonstrated efficacy. This application of dopamine-reducing agents illustrates how the same neurotransmitter system implicated in psychotic disorders can be therapeutically targeted for movement-related conditions. For the EPPP, it is useful to recognize that antipsychotic drugs serve clinical functions beyond the management of psychosis, including the treatment of tics through their dopamine-antagonist properties in specific neural circuits.

Consider a 10-year-old boy brought to clinic with frequent eye-blinking and throat-clearing tics that have persisted for over a year, along with occasional coprolalia. His pediatrician has tried behavioral interventions, but the tics are causing significant social distress and academic disruption. A child psychiatrist adds a low dose of risperidone, and within several weeks the tic frequency decreases substantially. The rationale is straightforward: excessive dopamine in the basal ganglia's motor circuits drives tic generation, and blocking D2 receptors in those circuits reduces the involuntary movements. This same mechanism — dopamine antagonism — is what makes antipsychotics effective for the positive symptoms of schizophrenia, but the neural circuit being targeted is different. This cross-application of pharmacological mechanisms is exactly the type of integrative thinking the EPPP rewards.

Returning to stimulants, there's a finding about their use in non-ADHD populations that is both clinically important and highly testable. On college campuses across the country, students without ADHD routinely obtain stimulant medications — often from peers with prescriptions — believing these drugs will sharpen their focus and boost their grades. The subjective experience seems to confirm this belief: users feel more alert, more concentrated, more "locked in." But what does the research actually show about academic outcomes? The answer may surprise both students and examinees alike.

Clinical Note: Stimulant Use in Non-ADHD Populations

Misuse of prescription stimulants among college students who do not have ADHD has become a significant concern. Research on this topic reveals a notable dissociation between subjective and objective outcomes: students without ADHD who take methylphenidate or similar stimulant medications often report enhanced concentration and improved mood. However, these perceived cognitive benefits do not translate into measurable academic gains. Studies have found that stimulant use among non-ADHD college students has no positive effect on grade point average (GPA). This discrepancy likely reflects the fact that stimulants produce a subjective sense of heightened focus and arousal that users interpret as improved performance, while actual learning and retention remain unchanged. The finding underscores the distinction between pharmacological effects on alertness and mood versus genuine enhancement of complex academic functioning. For clinicians, this pattern is relevant when counseling patients or conducting assessments, as it highlights both the motivations behind non-prescribed stimulant use and the lack of evidence supporting cognitive enhancement in neurotypical individuals.

This dissociation between feeling smarter and actually performing better is a critical distinction for clinical psychologists to understand. When a college student tells you, "Adderall really helps me study," they are accurately reporting their subjective experience — stimulants genuinely increase arousal, wakefulness, and the subjective sense of concentration. But this pharmacological effect on alertness does not automatically translate into deeper encoding, better retrieval, or improved complex reasoning. In individuals with ADHD, stimulants correct an underlying deficit in dopaminergic and noradrenergic signaling that was genuinely impairing executive function. In neurotypical individuals, the same drugs push an already-functioning system into overdrive without producing meaningful cognitive enhancement. For the EPPP, remember: stimulants in non-ADHD populations improve subjective experience but not objective academic performance.

To wrap up this chapter's core concepts, the following summary captures the essential pharmacological facts about ADHD medications that you should be prepared to recognize and apply on exam day. These points integrate the mechanisms, side effects, and clinical considerations covered throughout the chapter.

  • Stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamines) are the first-line pharmacological treatment for ADHD and increase dopamine and norepinephrine activity.
  • Atomoxetine is a non-stimulant alternative (selective NRI) with no abuse potential; takes 4-6 weeks for full effect.
  • Stimulants are Schedule II controlled substances with potential for abuse and dependence.
  • Common side effects include decreased appetite, insomnia, and increased heart rate/blood pressure.
  • Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) is a prodrug formulation that reduces abuse potential.
  • Growth suppression in children is possible but generally does not affect final adult height.
