How Drugs Work — Pharmacokinetics, Pharmacodynamics & Drug Interactions

Fundamental Content
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Introduction

A 58-year-old woman with treatment-resistant depression arrives at her psychiatrist's office reporting persistent drowsiness and confusion after starting a new antidepressant. Her medication list reveals she is also taking a potent CYP450 inhibitor prescribed by her cardiologist — a drug interaction that has effectively doubled the antidepressant's plasma concentration. Within minutes, the psychiatrist identifies the culprit: a pharmacokinetic interaction that no amount of clinical intuition alone could have predicted. Scenarios like this illustrate why understanding the science behind drug behavior in the body is not an academic luxury but a clinical necessity.

Before studying individual medication classes, it is essential to understand the foundational principles that govern how drugs work in the body. This chapter covers pharmacokinetics (what the body does to the drug), pharmacodynamics (what the drug does to the body), and the critical drug interaction mechanisms that shape prescribing decisions every day. These concepts underpin every medication chapter that follows and are frequently tested on the EPPP — particularly questions involving drug-drug interactions, therapeutic index calculations, and the CYP450 enzyme system.

Pharmacokinetics (ADME)

Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics describes "what the body does to the drug" -- the processes of Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion (ADME) that determine a drug's concentration at its site of action over time.

Absorption

Absorption is the process by which a drug enters the bloodstream from its site of administration. For oral medications (the most common route for psychotropics), the drug must dissolve in the GI tract, cross the intestinal epithelium, and enter the portal circulation.

  • Oral bioavailability -- the fraction of an orally administered drug that reaches systemic circulation in unchanged form. This is always less than 100% for oral drugs due to incomplete absorption and first-pass metabolism.
  • First-pass metabolism -- after oral absorption, drugs pass through the liver via the portal vein before reaching systemic circulation. The liver may metabolize a significant portion of the drug on this "first pass," substantially reducing the amount of active drug that reaches the brain. Example: some drugs (e.g., buspirone) have very low oral bioavailability due to extensive first-pass metabolism.
  • Factors affecting absorption include: drug formulation, gastric pH, food in the stomach, GI motility, and drug-drug interactions at the gut level.

Distribution

Once absorbed, the drug is distributed throughout the body via the bloodstream. Several factors determine where the drug goes and how much reaches the brain:

  • Protein binding -- many drugs bind to plasma proteins (primarily albumin). Only the unbound (free) fraction is pharmacologically active, able to cross membranes, and available for metabolism. Drugs with high protein binding (e.g., >95%) may be displaced by other protein-bound drugs, leading to transient increases in the free fraction and potentially toxic effects.
  • Blood-brain barrier (BBB) -- a selective, semi-permeable barrier formed by tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells. Psychotropic medications must cross the BBB to exert their CNS effects. Lipophilic (fat-soluble), small, and uncharged molecules cross more readily. The BBB is the primary reason that many peripherally active drugs do not cause CNS side effects.
  • Volume of distribution (Vd) -- a theoretical measure of the total body space available to a drug. A large Vd indicates the drug distributes extensively into tissues (e.g., is highly lipophilic); a small Vd indicates it stays mostly in the plasma. Highly lipophilic psychotropics (e.g., diazepam) have large volumes of distribution and may accumulate in adipose tissue.

Metabolism (Biotransformation)

Drug metabolism, primarily occurring in the liver, converts drugs into more water-soluble compounds that can be excreted. Metabolism occurs in two phases:

  • Phase I reactions -- oxidation, reduction, and hydrolysis reactions that introduce or expose a functional group on the drug molecule. The most important Phase I enzymes are the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) family. Phase I reactions may produce active metabolites (e.g., fluoxetine is metabolized to the active metabolite norfluoxetine).
  • Phase II reactions -- conjugation reactions (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation) that attach a large polar group to the drug or its Phase I metabolite, making it highly water-soluble and readily excreted. Phase II metabolites are almost always pharmacologically inactive.
EPPP Tip

Some drugs (called prodrugs) are inactive when administered and must be metabolized to their active form. Others produce active metabolites that extend the drug's duration of action (e.g., fluoxetine's metabolite norfluoxetine has a half-life of 4-16 days). The distinction between Phase I and Phase II metabolism is commonly tested.

Excretion (Elimination)

Excretion is the removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body. The two primary routes are:

  • Renal excretion -- the kidneys filter water-soluble metabolites into the urine. This is the primary route for most drug metabolites. Impaired renal function can lead to drug accumulation and toxicity. Lithium, which is not metabolized but excreted entirely by the kidneys, is a critical example -- renal impairment, dehydration, or sodium depletion can cause dangerous lithium accumulation.
  • Hepatic (biliary) excretion -- some drugs are excreted into bile and eliminated in the feces. Some undergo enterohepatic recirculation, where the drug is reabsorbed from the intestines, effectively prolonging its duration of action.

Half-Life and Steady State

Now that we understand how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted, a natural question arises: how long does a drug actually stay active in the body? The answer lies in a single pharmacokinetic parameter that influences nearly every prescribing decision — from how often a patient takes a pill to how long a clinician must wait before switching medications.

Half-Life (t1/2)

Half-life is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by 50%. It is the single most important pharmacokinetic parameter for understanding dosing frequency, time to steady state, and duration of drug effect after discontinuation.

Key Principles

  • Steady state is reached after approximately 5 half-lives of continuous dosing. At steady state, the amount of drug being administered equals the amount being eliminated, and plasma levels plateau.
  • Similarly, after discontinuation, it takes approximately 5 half-lives for a drug to be essentially eliminated from the body (~97% eliminated).
  • Half-life determines dosing frequency: drugs with short half-lives (e.g., venlafaxine, ~5 hours) often need twice-daily or extended-release dosing, while drugs with long half-lives (e.g., fluoxetine, ~2-6 days) may be given once daily.
  • Washout period -- the time needed for a drug to be cleared from the body before starting a new medication (to avoid interactions). This is directly related to half-life. Example: the 5-week washout required when switching from fluoxetine to an MAOI reflects fluoxetine's extremely long half-life (plus its active metabolite norfluoxetine).

The CYP450 Enzyme System

Half-life and steady state tell us how long a drug persists in the body, but they do not explain why two patients on the same medication can have dramatically different blood levels — or why adding a second drug can suddenly make the first one toxic. The answer to both questions lies in the liver's primary metabolic machinery: the cytochrome P450 enzyme system. Understanding this system is arguably the single most important pharmacological concept for predicting drug-drug interactions, and it is one of the most commonly tested topics on the EPPP.

Cytochrome P450 (CYP450)

The CYP450 system is a superfamily of liver enzymes responsible for the Phase I metabolism of approximately 75% of all clinically used drugs. Understanding CYP450 isoenzymes is essential for predicting drug-drug interactions -- one of the most commonly tested pharmacology topics on the EPPP.

CYP450 Inhibitors vs. Inducers: Clinical Impact
CYP450 Inhibitor
  • Slows metabolism of substrate
  • Substrate levels increase
  • Risk: toxicity
  • Examples: fluoxetine, paroxetine (CYP2D6); fluvoxamine (CYP1A2, CYP3A4); ketoconazole, grapefruit juice (CYP3A4)
  • Clinical case: fluoxetine + haloperidol → raised haloperidol → EPS risk
  • Onset: rapid (within days)
CYP450 Inducer
  • Speeds up metabolism of substrate
  • Substrate levels decrease
  • Risk: therapeutic failure
  • Examples: carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John's Wort (CYP3A4); smoking/tobacco (CYP1A2)
  • Clinical case: carbamazepine + oral contraceptives → contraceptive failure
  • Onset: gradual (days to weeks)

EPPP Tip

When a CYP450 inhibitor is co-administered with a substrate of that enzyme, the substrate's metabolism is slowed, leading to increased plasma levels and potentially toxicity. When an inducer is added, the substrate is metabolized faster, leading to decreased plasma levels and potentially therapeutic failure. Example: adding fluoxetine (CYP2D6 inhibitor) to a patient on haloperidol (CYP2D6 substrate) will raise haloperidol levels, increasing the risk of EPS and other side effects.

Pharmacogenomics: Poor vs. Extensive Metabolizers

Genetic polymorphisms in CYP450 enzymes result in significant variability in how individuals metabolize drugs. This field is known as pharmacogenomics.

  • Poor metabolizers (PMs) -- have reduced or absent enzyme activity. Drugs metabolized by that enzyme accumulate to higher levels, increasing the risk of side effects and toxicity at standard doses. Example: CYP2D6 poor metabolizers may experience toxic effects from standard doses of codeine (though paradoxically, they may get less analgesic effect from codeine, since codeine requires CYP2D6 conversion to morphine).
  • Extensive metabolizers (EMs) -- have normal enzyme activity and respond predictably to standard doses.
  • Ultrarapid metabolizers (UMs) -- have excess enzyme activity due to gene duplication. They metabolize drugs too quickly, resulting in subtherapeutic levels at standard doses. Ultrarapid CYP2D6 metabolizers may convert codeine to morphine too rapidly, leading to opioid toxicity.
Example: Pharmacogenomic Testing

The FDA has recommended pharmacogenomic testing for certain drug-gene pairs. For example, CYP2D6 genotyping is recommended before prescribing codeine (risk of ultra-rapid metabolism causing morphine toxicity) and CYP2C19 testing can guide citalopram dosing (poor metabolizers require lower doses).

Pharmacodynamics

So far, we have focused on what the body does to the drug — how it is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and eliminated. Now we shift perspective to ask the complementary question: what does the drug do to the body? This is the domain of pharmacodynamics, and it is where the clinical action truly begins — because understanding how drugs interact with their molecular targets explains both their therapeutic effects and their side effects.

Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics describes "what the drug does to the body" -- how a drug produces its effects by interacting with molecular targets (typically receptors) in the body.

Drug-Receptor Interactions

Most psychotropic medications exert their effects by binding to specific receptors -- protein molecules on cell surfaces or within cells that normally respond to endogenous neurotransmitters or hormones. Key concepts include:

  • Agonist -- a drug that binds to a receptor and activates it, producing a biological response. It mimics the effect of the endogenous neurotransmitter. Example: benzodiazepines are agonists at the GABA-A receptor (they enhance GABA's effect at the receptor).
  • Antagonist -- a drug that binds to a receptor and blocks it, preventing activation by the endogenous neurotransmitter. It has affinity for the receptor but no intrinsic activity (efficacy). Example: haloperidol is a dopamine D2 receptor antagonist.
  • Partial agonist -- a drug that binds to and activates a receptor but produces only a submaximal response, even at full receptor occupancy. Partial agonists can act as functional agonists in low-neurotransmitter states and as functional antagonists in high-neurotransmitter states, providing a "stabilizing" effect. Example: aripiprazole is a partial agonist at the D2 receptor; buspirone is a partial agonist at 5-HT1A.
  • Inverse agonist -- a drug that binds to a receptor and produces an effect opposite to that of an agonist. This is distinct from an antagonist, which simply blocks receptor activation. Some researchers consider certain beta-carbolines to be inverse agonists at the benzodiazepine site on GABA-A receptors (they reduce GABA activity below baseline).
Drug-Receptor Agonism Spectrum
Inverse Agonist
Binds receptor; produces effect opposite to agonist; reduces activity below baseline (e.g., some beta-carbolines at GABA-A)
Antagonist
Binds receptor; blocks activation; zero intrinsic activity; no effect alone (e.g., haloperidol at D2)
Partial Agonist
Binds and activates; submaximal response; stabilizing effect — agonist in low-NT states, antagonist in high-NT states (e.g., aripiprazole at D2; buspirone at 5-HT1A)
Full Agonist
Binds and fully activates receptor; maximal response; mimics endogenous neurotransmitter (e.g., benzodiazepines at GABA-A)

Affinity and Efficacy

  • Affinity -- how strongly a drug binds to its receptor. High affinity means the drug binds tightly and at low concentrations. Both agonists and antagonists have affinity.
  • Efficacy (intrinsic activity) -- the ability of a drug to activate the receptor once bound. Full agonists have high efficacy; partial agonists have intermediate efficacy; antagonists have zero efficacy.

Dose-Response Relationships

The relationship between drug dose and the magnitude of effect is described by the dose-response curve. Key parameters include:

  • ED50 -- the dose that produces a therapeutic effect in 50% of the population (or 50% of the maximal effect in a single individual).
  • LD50 -- the dose that is lethal to 50% of the population (determined in animal studies).
  • Therapeutic index (TI) -- the ratio of LD50 to ED50 (TI = LD50 / ED50). A higher therapeutic index means a wider margin of safety.

Potency vs. Efficacy

Potency vs. Efficacy

Potency refers to the amount of drug needed to produce a given effect (related to ED50) -- a more potent drug requires a lower dose. Efficacy refers to the maximum effect a drug can produce regardless of dose. A drug can be highly potent but have low maximum efficacy (and vice versa). For clinical purposes, efficacy is generally more important than potency -- a drug that requires a higher dose but achieves a better outcome is preferable to one that works at a lower dose but has a lower ceiling of effect.

Example: Potency vs. Efficacy in Antipsychotics

Haloperidol is a high-potency antipsychotic (effective at low doses, e.g., 2-10 mg), while chlorpromazine is a low-potency antipsychotic (requires higher doses, e.g., 200-800 mg). However, at their respective optimal doses, both achieve similar efficacy in treating psychotic symptoms. The key difference lies in their side effect profiles: high-potency agents cause more EPS; low-potency agents cause more sedation, orthostatic hypotension, and anticholinergic effects.

Therapeutic Index

The concepts of dose-response relationships and potency naturally lead to a practical clinical question: how safe is this drug? A medication that produces its desired effect at 10 mg but becomes lethal at 15 mg demands a very different level of clinical vigilance than one where the effective dose is 10 mg and the lethal dose is 1,000 mg. The therapeutic index (TI) is a critical concept for understanding medication safety. It quantifies the margin between a drug's effective dose and its toxic or lethal dose.

Narrow Therapeutic Index Drugs

Drugs with a narrow TI have a small margin between the therapeutic and toxic dose. These drugs require careful dosing, blood level monitoring, and vigilance for drug interactions.

Wide Therapeutic Index Drugs

Drugs with a wide TI have a large margin of safety. Routine blood level monitoring is generally not necessary.

  • SSRIs -- wide therapeutic index; difficult to cause lethal overdose with SSRIs alone. This is a major advantage over TCAs and MAOIs, particularly in patients with suicidal ideation.
  • Most benzodiazepines (when taken alone) -- wide TI, but significantly narrowed when combined with other CNS depressants (alcohol, opioids).
EPPP Tip

The EPPP frequently tests the clinical monitoring requirements for narrow therapeutic index drugs, especially lithium. Know the therapeutic range, signs of toxicity, and factors that alter lithium levels (dehydration, NSAIDs, diuretics, sodium restriction). Lithium toxicity presents as coarse tremor, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, and can progress to seizures, coma, and death.

Drug-Drug Interactions

Armed with an understanding of both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, we can now tackle what may be the most clinically consequential topic in psychopharmacology: drug-drug interactions. When a patient takes more than one medication — and most psychiatric patients do — the drugs do not exist in isolation. They compete for the same metabolic enzymes, bind to overlapping receptor sites, and sometimes amplify or cancel each other's effects in ways that can be therapeutic, dangerous, or both. Drug-drug interactions are a major clinical concern and a frequently tested EPPP topic. Interactions can be classified into two broad categories:

Pharmacokinetic Interactions

These interactions alter the ADME of one drug by another, changing the drug's plasma concentration:

  • CYP450 inhibition -- one drug inhibits the metabolism of another, causing the second drug to accumulate (increased levels, greater risk of toxicity). Example: fluvoxamine (CYP1A2 inhibitor) dramatically increases clozapine levels (CYP1A2 substrate), potentially causing toxicity.
  • CYP450 induction -- one drug induces (increases the expression of) CYP450 enzymes, causing a second drug to be metabolized faster (decreased levels, reduced efficacy). Example: carbamazepine (CYP3A4 inducer) reduces the effectiveness of oral contraceptives (CYP3A4 substrate).
  • Protein binding displacement -- one highly protein-bound drug displaces another from plasma proteins, transiently increasing the free fraction of the displaced drug.
  • Altered renal excretion -- drugs that affect renal function or urinary pH can alter the excretion rate of other drugs. Example: thiazide diuretics reduce lithium clearance, increasing lithium levels and toxicity risk.

Pharmacodynamic Interactions

These interactions occur when drugs act on the same or related receptor systems, altering the overall pharmacological effect without changing drug concentrations:

  • Additive effects -- the combined effect equals the sum of each drug's individual effect (1 + 1 = 2). Example: combining two CNS depressants (e.g., a benzodiazepine and alcohol) produces additive sedation.
  • Synergistic effects -- the combined effect is greater than the sum of individual effects (1 + 1 = 3). Example: combining an SSRI with an MAOI can produce synergistic serotonergic effects, potentially causing life-threatening serotonin syndrome.
  • Antagonistic effects -- one drug reduces or blocks the effect of another (1 + 1 = 0.5). Example: a dopamine agonist used for Parkinson's disease may counteract the dopamine-blocking effects of an antipsychotic.
EPPP Tip

Key drug-drug interactions to know: (1) SSRI + MAOI = serotonin syndrome (requires 2-week washout, 5 weeks for fluoxetine); (2) lithium + NSAIDs or diuretics = lithium toxicity; (3) carbamazepine + oral contraceptives = contraceptive failure; (4) fluvoxamine + clozapine = clozapine toxicity; (5) benzodiazepines + alcohol/opioids = respiratory depression. Psychologists must understand these interactions even though they typically do not prescribe, because they assess patient presentation and coordinate with prescribers.

Example: A Dangerous Combination in Clinical Practice

A 45-year-old man with bipolar disorder stabilized on lithium develops joint pain and begins taking ibuprofen (an NSAID) daily on his own. Over the following week, he becomes increasingly confused, develops a coarse tremor, and his speech becomes slurred. His wife brings him to the emergency department, where a lithium level drawn at 2.3 mEq/L confirms toxicity. The mechanism: ibuprofen reduced his renal clearance of lithium, causing dangerous accumulation. This case illustrates why psychologists must routinely ask about over-the-counter medications and coordinate with prescribers — even common pain relievers can turn a stable medication regimen into a medical emergency (Sadock et al., 2015).

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Pharmacokinetics (ADME) describes what the body does to the drug: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion.
  • First-pass metabolism in the liver reduces oral bioavailability; some drugs (e.g., buspirone) have very low bioavailability because of extensive first-pass effects.
  • Only the unbound (free) fraction of a protein-bound drug is pharmacologically active.
  • Steady state is reached after approximately 5 half-lives of continuous dosing; elimination after discontinuation also takes approximately 5 half-lives.
  • The CYP450 enzyme system metabolizes approximately 75% of clinically used drugs; know the major isoenzymes (CYP2D6, CYP3A4, CYP1A2, CYP2C19) and their key substrates, inhibitors, and inducers.
  • CYP450 inhibitors slow metabolism of substrates (increased levels, toxicity risk); inducers accelerate metabolism (decreased levels, therapeutic failure).
  • Pharmacogenomics: poor metabolizers accumulate drugs to toxic levels at standard doses; ultrarapid metabolizers clear drugs too quickly, resulting in subtherapeutic levels.
  • Agonists activate receptors; antagonists block them; partial agonists produce submaximal responses and can stabilize systems (e.g., aripiprazole at D2, buspirone at 5-HT1A).
  • Therapeutic index (TI) = LD50/ED50; narrow TI drugs (lithium, carbamazepine, valproate, clozapine) require blood level monitoring.
  • Key drug interactions: SSRI + MAOI = serotonin syndrome; lithium + NSAIDs/diuretics = lithium toxicity; carbamazepine + oral contraceptives = contraceptive failure; benzodiazepines + alcohol = respiratory depression.
