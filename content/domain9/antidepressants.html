Antidepressants

SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, serotonin syndrome
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Introduction to Antidepressant Medications

Antidepressants are among the most commonly prescribed psychotropic medications. They are used to treat major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, OCD, PTSD, chronic pain, and other conditions. Understanding the major classes of antidepressants -- their mechanisms, side effects, and clinical considerations -- is essential for the EPPP.

Antidepressants generally take 2-6 weeks to reach full therapeutic effect, although side effects may begin immediately. This delay is thought to relate to downstream neuroplastic changes rather than the acute increase in neurotransmitter availability.

Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)

When Prozac arrived on the U.S. market in 1988, it fundamentally changed the landscape of depression treatment. For the first time, clinicians had an antidepressant that was both broadly effective and far less dangerous in overdose than the tricyclic antidepressants that had dominated prescribing for decades. Within a few years, Prozac became one of the most widely prescribed medications in the world — and it opened the floodgates for an entire class of drugs that now forms the backbone of first-line pharmacotherapy for mood and anxiety disorders.

SSRIs are the most commonly prescribed class of antidepressants and are generally considered first-line treatment for depression and many anxiety disorders due to their relatively favorable side effect profile. Their popularity rests on a straightforward clinical calculus: they work well for a wide range of conditions, they are tolerated reasonably well by most patients, and they carry a considerably lower risk of lethality in overdose compared to older antidepressants like tricyclics and MAOIs. For the EPPP, you will need to know not only the general profile of this class but also the distinguishing features of individual SSRIs — because those distinctions regularly appear in exam questions.

Mechanism of Action

Recall from the preceding section that antidepressants generally take two to six weeks to reach full therapeutic effect, even though they begin altering neurotransmitter levels almost immediately. This paradox is especially relevant to SSRIs, because their acute mechanism of action is deceptively simple. SSRIs selectively block the reuptake of serotonin (5-HT) at the presynaptic neuron, increasing the availability of serotonin in the synaptic cleft. They have minimal effects on norepinephrine and dopamine reuptake, which is precisely what makes them "selective" — they target the serotonin transporter (SERT) with far greater affinity than other monoamine transporters.

Imagine a conversation in a noisy room. Normally, once serotonin delivers its message to the postsynaptic receptor, the presynaptic neuron vacuums it back up through the SERT, recycling it for future use. An SSRI essentially blocks that vacuum, leaving more serotonin lingering in the synaptic cleft to continue binding to postsynaptic receptors. The result is enhanced serotonergic neurotransmission — but the therapeutic benefit unfolds only after weeks of downstream changes, including receptor downregulation, enhanced neuroplasticity, and alterations in gene expression. This is why patients need to be counseled about the delay in therapeutic onset and encouraged to continue the medication even when they feel no immediate improvement.

The selectivity of SSRIs for serotonin is both a strength and a limitation. Because they largely leave norepinephrine and dopamine systems alone, they produce fewer anticholinergic, cardiovascular, and sedating side effects than tricyclics. However, this same selectivity means they may not be the best choice for patients whose depression is characterized by prominent fatigue, psychomotor slowing, or attentional deficits — symptoms that may respond better to agents with noradrenergic or dopaminergic activity, a point we will revisit in the next section on SNRIs.

Common SSRIs

Although all SSRIs share the same basic mechanism, individual agents differ in half-life, receptor selectivity, drug interaction profiles, and FDA-approved indications. These differences matter clinically — and they matter on the EPPP. The table below summarizes the six major SSRIs, but a few high-yield distinctions deserve special attention.

Fluoxetine deserves particular attention because its uniquely long half-life creates a kind of built-in taper. If a patient abruptly stops fluoxetine, the drug and its active metabolite norfluoxetine can linger in the body for weeks, dramatically reducing the risk of discontinuation syndrome. This also means fluoxetine is the SSRI most likely to cause problems with drug interactions when switching to another agent — especially an MAOI, where the standard washout period is five weeks rather than the typical two weeks required for other SSRIs.

Paroxetine stands at the opposite end of the spectrum. Its short half-life makes it the SSRI most prone to discontinuation syndrome — patients who miss even a dose or two may experience dizziness, irritability, nausea, and the distinctive "brain zaps" (brief, electric shock-like sensations) that are a hallmark of SSRI withdrawal. Paroxetine is also the most anticholinergic SSRI, meaning it is more likely to cause dry mouth, constipation, and sedation. Critically for clinical practice and the EPPP, paroxetine carries a Category D pregnancy rating due to an association with cardiac malformations in the first trimester, making it the one SSRI explicitly contraindicated during pregnancy.

Sertraline is often considered a versatile workhorse: it has a mild dopamine reuptake-inhibiting effect that may offer a subtle energizing quality, it is a first-line choice for PTSD, and its favorable cardiac safety profile makes it one of the preferred antidepressants for patients with heart disease. Citalopram is the most pharmacologically selective SSRI — meaning it binds almost exclusively to SERT with virtually no activity at other receptors — but this purity comes with a caveat: at doses above 40 mg/day, it carries a dose-dependent risk of QT prolongation, a cardiac conduction abnormality that can predispose to arrhythmias. Escitalopram, the purified S-enantiomer of citalopram, retains the selectivity of its parent compound while offering potentially fewer side effects and drug interactions at lower doses.

Common Side Effects of SSRIs

One of the most important clinical conversations a provider will have with a patient starting an SSRI involves setting realistic expectations about side effects. Many side effects appear within the first week — well before the therapeutic benefit kicks in — which can be discouraging and lead to premature discontinuation. Understanding the side effect profile is not only essential for patient education but also a frequent topic on the EPPP.

  • Sexual dysfunction (decreased libido, anorgasmia, erectile dysfunction) -- most common reason for discontinuation
  • Gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, diarrhea)
  • Headache, insomnia, or drowsiness
  • Weight gain (especially paroxetine) or weight loss
  • Increased anxiety initially (may worsen before improving)
  • Discontinuation syndrome with abrupt cessation (dizziness, nausea, "brain zaps," irritability)

Sexual dysfunction warrants special emphasis because it is both the most common reason patients discontinue SSRIs and the side effect most likely to go unreported unless clinicians ask about it directly. Rates vary across studies, but estimates suggest that 30–70% of patients on SSRIs experience some form of sexual side effect, including decreased desire, difficulty achieving orgasm, or erectile dysfunction. For the exam, remember that sexual dysfunction is a class-wide effect of SSRIs, though some agents (particularly paroxetine) may be worse offenders than others.

The phenomenon of increased anxiety during the first one to two weeks of SSRI treatment is another high-yield clinical point. Patients beginning treatment for panic disorder or generalized anxiety may feel paradoxically more anxious before they feel better, which is why many prescribers initiate SSRIs at half the usual starting dose in anxious patients and slowly titrate upward. Counseling patients that this initial activation is temporary and expected can be the difference between a patient who stays on the medication long enough to benefit and one who abandons it prematurely.

Finally, discontinuation syndrome is distinct from drug addiction or withdrawal in the traditional sense — SSRIs are not considered addictive — but it can be highly uncomfortable and clinically significant. The mnemonic "FINISH" (Flu-like symptoms, Insomnia, Nausea, Imbalance, Sensory disturbances, Hyperarousal) can help you remember the constellation of symptoms. Short-half-life SSRIs like paroxetine carry the highest risk, while long-half-life SSRIs like fluoxetine carry the lowest. The clinical takeaway — and the exam-ready answer — is that SSRIs should always be tapered gradually rather than stopped abruptly.

Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors (SNRIs)

Having explored how SSRIs work by selectively targeting serotonin reuptake, a natural question arises: what happens when a patient's depression doesn't respond to boosting serotonin alone? Consider a 42-year-old woman with major depressive disorder who has tried two different SSRIs over the past year with only partial improvement in her mood — and who also reports chronic lower back pain and persistent fatigue. Her clinician might reason that engaging a second neurotransmitter system could address what serotonin modulation alone could not. This is precisely the clinical logic behind the serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors.

SNRIs block the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, providing a dual mechanism that may be beneficial for patients who do not respond to SSRIs alone. By inhibiting the reuptake of norepinephrine in addition to serotonin, SNRIs can target a broader range of depressive symptoms — particularly the fatigue, poor concentration, and psychomotor slowing that are sometimes associated with norepinephrine deficits. Equally important for exam preparation is recognizing that this dual mechanism extends the clinical utility of SNRIs well beyond mood disorders: they are also used for chronic pain conditions, fibromyalgia, and neuropathic pain. The norepinephrine component is thought to play a key role in modulating descending pain inhibitory pathways in the spinal cord, which is why a patient being treated for diabetic neuropathy might receive the same medication class as someone with generalized anxiety disorder.

Understanding the dual-action mechanism also helps explain the side-effect profile of SNRIs. Because they affect norepinephrine — a catecholamine involved in sympathetic nervous system activation — SNRIs carry cardiovascular side effects that SSRIs generally do not. This distinction between SSRI and SNRI side-effect profiles is a commonly tested concept and one that has real clinical consequences for patient safety.

Common SNRIs

The four SNRIs currently available differ meaningfully in their relative selectivity for serotonin versus norepinephrine, their approved indications, and their side-effect considerations. The table below summarizes these distinctions — pay particular attention to venlafaxine's dose-dependent mechanism and duloxetine's broad range of FDA-approved indications, as both are high-yield for the EPPP.

Generic NameBrand NameNotable Features
VenlafaxineEffexorAt low doses, primarily serotonergic; norepinephrine effects emerge at higher doses; may increase blood pressure; significant discontinuation syndrome
DuloxetineCymbaltaFDA-approved for MDD, GAD, fibromyalgia, diabetic neuropathic pain, and chronic musculoskeletal pain; hepatotoxicity risk (avoid in liver disease)
DesvenlafaxinePristiqActive metabolite of venlafaxine; fewer drug interactions; simpler dosing
LevomilnacipranFetzimaGreater selectivity for norepinephrine than serotonin; may be more activating

A few points from this table deserve emphasis. Venlafaxine is sometimes described as behaving like an SSRI at low doses, only "recruiting" norepinephrine reuptake inhibition as the dose is increased — a dose-dependent pharmacological profile that makes it unique among the SNRIs. This means a patient on a low dose of venlafaxine who reports no improvement may benefit from a dosage increase that engages the norepinephrine system. Desvenlafaxine is the active metabolite of venlafaxine, which means it bypasses the hepatic metabolism step required by its parent compound, resulting in fewer drug interactions and more predictable pharmacokinetics. Meanwhile, levomilnacipran stands apart from its class by having greater selectivity for norepinephrine over serotonin — essentially the reverse of what most SNRIs emphasize — which may make it a more activating choice and potentially more helpful for patients whose depression is characterized by fatigue and low motivation.

Duloxetine deserves special attention because of its remarkably broad FDA-approved indication list. Imagine a single patient with comorbid major depression, generalized anxiety, and fibromyalgia — duloxetine could theoretically address all three conditions with one medication. However, clinicians must be aware of its hepatotoxicity risk, which makes it contraindicated in patients with substantial liver disease or heavy alcohol use. This is a clinically significant caution that differentiates duloxetine from other SNRIs and is worth remembering for both practice and the exam.

Side Effects of SNRIs

Because SNRIs share serotonin reuptake inhibition with SSRIs, they also share many of the same side effects — including sexual dysfunction, nausea, and headache. However, the addition of norepinephrine reuptake inhibition introduces a set of side effects that are relatively specific to this class and important to recognize clinically. Think of the norepinephrine-related effects as reflecting increased sympathetic activation: the body's "fight or flight" system is being gently dialed up.

  • Similar to SSRIs (sexual dysfunction, nausea, headache)
  • Elevated blood pressure (especially venlafaxine at higher doses) -- blood pressure monitoring recommended
  • Increased sweating
  • Discontinuation syndrome (particularly severe with venlafaxine due to its short half-life)

The elevated blood pressure risk associated with venlafaxine is clinically significant and a frequent exam topic. Because norepinephrine plays a direct role in vasoconstriction and cardiac output, higher doses of venlafaxine can produce sustained hypertension in some patients. This means that regular blood pressure monitoring is not just recommended — it is standard of care. A test question might present a patient on high-dose venlafaxine whose blood pressure has crept upward, asking you to identify the likely medication-related cause.

Perhaps the most clinically dramatic side effect in this class is discontinuation syndrome, which is particularly severe with venlafaxine due to its short half-life. Patients who abruptly stop venlafaxine may experience dizziness, nausea, irritability, sensory disturbances often described as "brain zaps" or electrical shock sensations, and flu-like symptoms — sometimes within hours of a missed dose. This is not a sign of psychological dependence; it reflects the nervous system's abrupt loss of neurotransmitter modulation. For this reason, all SNRIs — and venlafaxine in particular — should be tapered gradually rather than discontinued abruptly. Understanding this phenomenon is essential for the psychologist who is collaborating with a prescriber and helping a patient understand what they are experiencing during medication changes.

Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

TCAs were the first widely used antidepressants but have largely been replaced by SSRIs and SNRIs as first-line treatments due to their significant side effect profile and lethality in overdose. They remain useful for treatment-resistant depression and certain other conditions.

Mechanism of Action

TCAs block the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine but also block histamine (H1), acetylcholine (muscarinic), and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, which produces many of their side effects.

Common TCAs

ClomipramineAnafranilMost serotonergic TCA; first-line pharmacotherapy for OCD
DesipramineNorpraminMost noradrenergic TCA; least sedating; less anticholinergic

Side Effects of TCAs

  • Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision, tachycardia) -- remember "can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't sh*t"
  • Sedation (due to histamine blockade)
  • Orthostatic hypotension (due to alpha-1 blockade)
  • Weight gain
  • Cardiac toxicity -- fatal in overdose due to cardiac arrhythmias (QT prolongation, heart block). This is why TCAs are dangerous in suicidal patients.
  • Lowered seizure threshold
EPPP Tip

TCAs are lethal in overdose due to cardiac arrhythmias. This is a critical clinical consideration and a frequently tested EPPP fact. For this reason, TCAs are prescribed cautiously (if at all) to patients with suicidal ideation. A one-week supply of TCAs can be lethal in overdose.

Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors (MAOIs)

MAOIs are the oldest class of antidepressants. They are now typically reserved for treatment-resistant depression and atypical depression because of their potentially dangerous drug and food interactions.

Mechanism of Action

MAOIs inhibit the enzyme monoamine oxidase, which normally breaks down serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine. By inhibiting this enzyme, MAOIs increase the availability of all three monoamines.

  • MAO-A preferentially metabolizes serotonin and norepinephrine
  • MAO-B preferentially metabolizes dopamine (selegiline, used in Parkinson's disease, is selective for MAO-B at low doses)

Common MAOIs

  • Phenelzine (Nardil) -- irreversible, nonselective
  • Tranylcypromine (Parnate) -- irreversible, nonselective
  • Selegiline (Emsam) -- available as a transdermal patch; at low doses, selective for MAO-B; dietary restrictions may not be required at lowest patch dose

The Tyramine Crisis (Hypertensive Crisis)

Tyramine Crisis

Patients taking MAOIs must follow strict dietary restrictions to avoid tyramine-containing foods. Tyramine is normally broken down by MAO in the gut. When MAO is inhibited, tyramine accumulates and causes the release of large amounts of norepinephrine, leading to a potentially fatal hypertensive crisis (severe headache, rapid heart rate, stiff neck, nausea, stroke, death).

Foods to avoid while taking MAOIs include:

  • Aged cheeses (cheddar, Swiss, parmesan, blue cheese)
  • Cured or smoked meats (salami, pepperoni, smoked fish)
  • Fermented foods (sauerkraut, kimchi, soy sauce)
  • Draft beer, red wine
  • Broad (fava) beans
  • Certain dietary supplements and over-the-counter cold medications containing pseudoephedrine or phenylephrine

Drug Interactions with MAOIs

MAOIs have dangerous interactions with many medications, including SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, meperidine (Demerol), dextromethorphan, and sympathomimetic drugs. A washout period of at least 2 weeks is required when switching between MAOIs and serotonergic medications (5 weeks for fluoxetine due to its long half-life).

Atypical Antidepressants

Having explored the major classes of antidepressants — SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, and MAOIs — you might assume that these categories cover the full pharmacological landscape for treating depression. But the brain's complexity has inspired medications that break the mold entirely, targeting neurotransmitter systems and receptor mechanisms that don't fit neatly into any of those established categories. These are the atypical antidepressants, a diverse group of medications united not by a shared mechanism but by their refusal to be classified elsewhere. Understanding these agents is clinically essential because they are often the medications patients turn to when first-line treatments fail, when side effect profiles of conventional antidepressants prove intolerable, or when the clinical picture demands something different — like rapid relief from acute suicidal ideation.

What makes this group particularly interesting for the practicing psychologist is that each atypical antidepressant has a distinctive pharmacological fingerprint: one targets the glutamate system and can lift depression within hours, another boosts norepinephrine and dopamine without touching serotonin at all, and still another is prescribed more often as a sleep aid than as an antidepressant. Knowing these distinctions helps psychologists understand why a psychiatrist might choose one agent over another, anticipate side effects that could affect therapy engagement, and recognize when a patient's experience in session might be medication-related.

Esketamine (Spravato)

Imagine a patient who has been struggling with severe depression for years — she has tried three different SSRIs, an SNRI, and augmentation with an atypical antipsychotic, yet nothing has meaningfully lifted the darkness. For patients like her, the approval of esketamine in 2019 represented something genuinely new: a fundamentally different approach to treating depression. Esketamine is the S-enantiomer of ketamine, an NMDA (N-methyl-D-aspartate) glutamate receptor antagonist. While every antidepressant we have discussed so far works by modulating monoamine neurotransmitter systems — serotonin, norepinephrine, or dopamine — esketamine acts on the glutamatergic system, the brain's primary excitatory neurotransmitter network. This mechanistic difference is not merely academic: it translates into a clinical timeline that would have seemed impossible a generation ago. Rather than the weeks-long wait typical of conventional antidepressants, esketamine can produce meaningful antidepressant effects within hours to days.

Esketamine is administered as a nasal spray and is specifically indicated for treatment-resistant depression (TRD), which is formally defined as depression that has failed to respond to at least two adequate antidepressant trials in the current episode. This is an important threshold to remember — the medication is not a first- or second-line treatment but rather a tool reserved for patients whose illness has already proven refractory to standard approaches. It is always used as an adjunct to an oral antidepressant rather than as monotherapy, meaning the patient continues a conventional medication alongside the esketamine treatments.

Because of concerns about dissociative effects, sedation, abuse potential, and transient increases in blood pressure, esketamine is available only through a restricted distribution system called the REMS (Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy) program. In practice, this means a patient cannot simply pick up a prescription at the pharmacy and use it at home. Instead, each dose must be administered in a certified healthcare setting — typically a psychiatrist's office or specialized clinic — and the patient must be observed for at least two hours following administration. Common adverse effects include dissociation, dizziness, nausea, sedation, vertigo, and a feeling of disconnection from reality. These effects are typically transient and resolve within the monitoring period, but they can be unsettling for patients who are unprepared for them.

Clinical Note

Esketamine's rapid onset of action makes it a particularly valuable option for patients in acute depressive crises, including those with suicidal ideation. In 2020, the FDA expanded its approval to include depressive symptoms in adults with major depressive disorder accompanied by acute suicidal thinking or behavior. This expanded indication is especially relevant for psychologists working in crisis settings, where knowing that a rapid-acting pharmacological intervention exists can inform safety planning and referral decisions. Psychologists should be aware that patients receiving esketamine may experience perceptual disturbances — sometimes described as feeling "floaty," detached, or as though the world looks different — and should not drive or operate machinery until the following day after a restful sleep. If a patient reports unusual perceptual experiences following a treatment session, it is important to contextualize these as expected medication effects rather than symptoms of a new psychiatric condition.

Bupropion (Wellbutrin)

Consider a patient who responds well to antidepressant medication in terms of mood but finds the sexual side effects — diminished libido, difficulty reaching orgasm — so distressing that he stops taking the medication entirely. Or picture a woman who is trying to quit smoking while also managing a depressive episode. In both scenarios, bupropion (marketed as Wellbutrin for depression and Zyban for smoking cessation) stands out as a uniquely suited option, precisely because of what it does not do: it does not affect serotonin. This is the defining characteristic that sets bupropion apart from nearly every other antidepressant on the market.

  • Mechanism: Norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor (NDRI); does NOT affect serotonin
  • Uses: Depression, smoking cessation (marketed as Zyban), ADHD (off-label)
  • Advantages: No sexual dysfunction (may actually improve libido); no weight gain (may cause weight loss); activating rather than sedating
  • Risks: Lowers seizure threshold (contraindicated in patients with seizure disorders, eating disorders, or those undergoing abrupt alcohol withdrawal); may increase anxiety

The seizure risk associated with bupropion is one of its most clinically important features and a frequent exam topic. Its contraindication in eating disorders — particularly bulimia nervosa and anorexia nervosa — stems from the fact that electrolyte imbalances common in these conditions already lower the seizure threshold, and bupropion further compounds that risk. Similarly, abrupt alcohol withdrawal can provoke seizures, making bupropion a poor choice during active detoxification. The activating profile of bupropion (boosting dopamine and norepinephrine) means it can feel stimulating — helpful for patients with fatigue and low motivation, but potentially problematic for patients already experiencing significant anxiety or agitation.

Mirtazapine (Remeron)

Now consider a very different clinical picture: an elderly patient with depression who has lost 15 pounds because she has no appetite, lies awake most of the night, and feels nauseated much of the time. For this patient, the side effect profile of mirtazapine (Remeron) is not a drawback — it is the treatment plan. Mirtazapine's most prominent effects — sedation, increased appetite, and weight gain — are precisely what certain patients need, making it a case study in how pharmacological "side effects" can become therapeutic advantages when matched to the right clinical presentation.

  • Mechanism: Noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressant (NaSSA); blocks alpha-2 autoreceptors (increasing NE and 5-HT release) and 5-HT2 and 5-HT3 receptors; potent H1 antagonist
  • Uses: Depression, especially with insomnia, poor appetite, or weight loss
  • Side effects: Significant sedation and weight gain (due to H1 blockade); increased appetite; useful when these "side effects" are actually therapeutic goals
  • Advantages: Minimal sexual dysfunction; helpful for nausea (5-HT3 blockade)

The mechanism of mirtazapine is more complex than most antidepressants and worth understanding. By blocking alpha-2 autoreceptors — the receptors that normally serve as a "brake" on norepinephrine and serotonin release — mirtazapine effectively takes the foot off the brake, allowing more of both neurotransmitters to flow. Its blockade of 5-HT3 receptors explains its anti-nausea properties (the same receptor targeted by ondansetron, a dedicated anti-nausea medication), and its potent antagonism of histamine H1 receptors accounts for the pronounced sedation and appetite stimulation. Because it causes minimal sexual dysfunction, mirtazapine is sometimes combined with an SSRI or SNRI — a combination colloquially known as "California rocket fuel" — to leverage complementary mechanisms while counteracting the sexual side effects of the serotonergic agent.

Trazodone (Desyrel)

If you ask most clinicians what trazodone is used for, the first answer will almost certainly be "sleep" — even though it is technically classified as an antidepressant. Trazodone occupies an unusual niche in psychopharmacology: while it does have genuine antidepressant properties at higher doses, its sedating effects are so prominent that it is far more commonly prescribed at low doses as a sleep aid than as a treatment for depression. This makes it one of the most frequently encountered medications in clinical practice, since insomnia is a near-universal companion of depression and anxiety disorders.

  • Mechanism: Serotonin antagonist and reuptake inhibitor (SARI)
  • Uses: Primarily used as a sleep aid at low doses; antidepressant at higher doses (rarely used this way due to sedation)
  • Side effects: Sedation, orthostatic hypotension, priapism (rare but serious -- prolonged painful erection requiring emergency treatment)

The side effect of priapism — a rare but potentially serious condition involving a prolonged, painful erection — is a high-yield exam fact for trazodone. Although uncommon (occurring in roughly 1 in 6,000 male patients), priapism constitutes a medical emergency because it can lead to permanent tissue damage if not treated promptly. Patients prescribed trazodone should be warned about this risk and instructed to seek emergency medical attention if an erection persists for more than four hours. Orthostatic hypotension — a drop in blood pressure upon standing that can cause dizziness or fainting — is another clinically relevant concern, particularly for elderly patients who may be at increased risk of falls.

Serotonin Syndrome

St. John's Wort (Hypericum perforatum)

St. John's Wort is a herbal supplement widely used as an over-the-counter remedy for depression, particularly in Europe. Research evidence suggests that for mild to moderate depression, St. John's Wort extract demonstrates comparable efficacy to SSRIs in producing therapeutic response, while being associated with fewer adverse effects such as sexual dysfunction, gastrointestinal distress, and sedation. However, its efficacy for severe major depression is not well supported. The active compounds — primarily hypericin and hyperforin — are believed to inhibit the reuptake of serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine, though the precise mechanism remains under investigation.

Despite its favorable side effect profile, St. John's Wort poses substantial risks through drug interactions. It is a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes (particularly CYP3A4) and the P-glycoprotein transport system, which can significantly reduce the plasma levels and effectiveness of numerous medications, including oral contraceptives, anticoagulants (warfarin), immunosuppressants (cyclosporine), antiretrovirals, and certain cardiac drugs. Of particular relevance to this chapter, combining St. John's Wort with serotonergic medications such as SSRIs, SNRIs, or MAOIs can precipitate serotonin syndrome. Patients should be specifically asked about St. John's Wort use, as many individuals do not spontaneously report herbal supplements when listing their medications.

Example

A 32-year-old woman presents with worsening depressive symptoms despite taking sertraline 100 mg daily. Upon careful inquiry, she reveals she recently began taking St. John's Wort on the recommendation of a friend. She reports new symptoms of agitation, tremor, and excessive sweating. Her clinician recognizes these as potential early signs of serotonin syndrome resulting from the combination of two serotonergic agents and immediately advises discontinuation of the herbal supplement.

Serotonin Syndrome

Serotonin syndrome is a potentially life-threatening condition caused by excessive serotonergic activity, typically resulting from combining two or more serotonergic medications (e.g., SSRI + MAOI, SSRI + tramadol, SSRI + St. John's Wort). It requires immediate medical attention.

Clinical Triad of Serotonin Syndrome

  1. Cognitive/behavioral changes: Agitation, confusion, hypomania, restlessness
  2. Autonomic instability: Hyperthermia, tachycardia, diaphoresis (sweating), diarrhea, fluctuating blood pressure
  3. Neuromuscular abnormalities: Tremor, myoclonus (muscle jerking), hyperreflexia, rigidity, clonus

Treatment: Discontinue all serotonergic agents; supportive care; cyproheptadine (a serotonin antagonist) may be used in severe cases; benzodiazepines for agitation.

The FDA Black Box Warning

Having just explored serotonin syndrome — a potentially life-threatening consequence of serotonergic excess — we turn now to another critical safety concern that every psychologist must understand: the relationship between antidepressants and suicidality in young people. This topic sits at the intersection of psychopharmacology and ethics, and it is one of the most frequently tested areas on the EPPP. Understanding the nuances of this warning can mean the difference between a correct and incorrect answer on exam day — and, far more importantly, between safe and unsafe clinical practice.

Imagine you are supervising a trainee who has just learned that a 16-year-old client was started on sertraline one week ago. The trainee asks, "Should we be worried about the medication making things worse?" This is exactly the kind of question that the FDA's most serious regulatory action on antidepressants was designed to address. In 2004, after reviewing data from multiple clinical trials, the FDA issued a black box warning — the strongest warning the agency can place on a prescription medication — for all antidepressants. The warning alerts prescribers and patients to an increased risk of suicidal thinking and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults (up to age 24) during the initial weeks of treatment. The name "black box" comes from the literal black border that surrounds the warning text on the medication's packaging, signaling that this is not routine precautionary language but a serious, evidence-based safety concern.

Several key details about this warning are essential for clinical practice and for the EPPP. First, the warning applies to all antidepressants — not just SSRIs. This is a common misconception. Whether the medication is an SSRI, SNRI, tricyclic, or atypical antidepressant, the black box warning applies universally across the class. Second, the risk appears to be highest during the first one to two months of treatment, or following any dose change. This is the critical window when vigilance must be greatest. Third, the proposed mechanism is both clinically intuitive and somewhat paradoxical: antidepressants may initially restore energy and psychomotor activation before they meaningfully improve mood. A patient who was previously too lethargic and hopeless to act on suicidal thoughts may suddenly have the energy to do so — while still experiencing the despair that drives the ideation. This temporal mismatch between restored motivation and still-depressed mood creates a dangerous window of vulnerability.

Perhaps the most important nuance — and one that the EPPP frequently tests — is what the warning does not say. The black box warning does NOT state that antidepressants should not be prescribed to young people. Rather, it emphasizes that close monitoring is essential during the initial treatment period, especially in younger patients. The clinical consensus remains that for most children and adolescents with moderate to severe depression, the benefits of antidepressant treatment generally outweigh the risks — provided that careful, frequent follow-up occurs. Ironically, some research has suggested that the black box warning itself may have had unintended consequences: after its release, antidepressant prescriptions for young people declined significantly, and some epidemiological data indicated a concurrent rise in youth suicide rates, raising concern that undertreating depression may pose a greater risk than the medications themselves.

  • The warning applies to all antidepressants, not just SSRIs
  • The risk appears to be highest during the first 1-2 months of treatment or after dose changes
  • The mechanism is thought to involve an initial increase in energy and motivation before mood fully improves, potentially enabling a suicidal individual to act on preexisting ideation
  • Close monitoring is recommended during the initial period of treatment, especially in young patients
  • The warning does NOT state that antidepressants should not be prescribed to young people; the benefits generally outweigh the risks, but careful monitoring is essential

For psychologists who do not prescribe, understanding the black box warning remains critical. You may be the provider who sees a young client most frequently, placing you in the best position to detect early warning signs of increased suicidality after a medication change. Knowing when the risk is highest — and communicating effectively with the prescribing physician — is a core competency that bridges pharmacological knowledge and ethical responsibility.

EPPP Tip

The black box warning is limited to ages 24 and under. For adults aged 25-64, antidepressants showed no increased suicidality, and for adults 65+, antidepressants appeared to reduce suicidal risk. The only SSRI FDA-approved for use in children with depression is fluoxetine (ages 8+). Remember these age cutoffs — they are high-yield distinctions that appear repeatedly on the exam. A helpful mnemonic: "24 and under, watch like thunder; 65 and older, depression's the smolder" — reminding you that the risk profile shifts across the lifespan.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • SSRIs are first-line treatment for depression and anxiety; primary side effects include sexual dysfunction and GI disturbance.
  • Fluoxetine has the longest half-life and is the only SSRI FDA-approved for pediatric depression.
  • SNRIs block both serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake; venlafaxine can raise blood pressure.
  • TCAs are lethal in overdose due to cardiac arrhythmias; cause anticholinergic side effects and sedation.
  • Clomipramine is the most serotonergic TCA and the first-line pharmacotherapy for OCD.
  • MAOIs require strict dietary restrictions to avoid tyramine-induced hypertensive crisis; require a 2-week washout before switching to serotonergic drugs.
  • Bupropion (NDRI) does not cause sexual dysfunction, aids in smoking cessation, but lowers seizure threshold.
  • Mirtazapine causes sedation and weight gain but no sexual dysfunction; useful when appetite stimulation and sleep are needed.
  • Serotonin syndrome results from combining serotonergic agents and presents with the triad of cognitive changes, autonomic instability, and neuromuscular hyperactivity.
  • The FDA black box warning applies to suicidality risk in patients aged 24 and under during initial antidepressant treatment.
