Overview of Professional Issues

Imagine you are a newly licensed psychologist who receives a frantic voicemail from a former client's attorney — a malpractice liability claim has been filed against you. Your heart races. You wonder: Did I document that risk assessment thoroughly enough? Was my informed consent process adequate? Did I consult with a colleague when the case became complicated? In that moment, every professional practice guideline you ever studied suddenly matters in the most personal way possible. The legal, regulatory, and ethical frameworks that govern psychology are not abstract rules — they are the very structures that protect both clients and practitioners when things go wrong.

This chapter explores the professional practice issues that define the boundaries, responsibilities, and evolving landscape of psychological practice. From the moment you sit for your licensure exam to the day you supervise your own trainees, these topics will shape virtually every clinical decision you make. We will examine how malpractice law creates accountability, how supervision models ensure competent training, how licensure and credentialing systems safeguard the public, and how emerging areas like prescriptive authority and telepsychology are reshaping what it means to be a psychologist in the twenty-first century. Forensic psychology — where clinical expertise meets the courtroom — rounds out the chapter with its own unique demands on professional judgment.

These topics appear on the EPPP not merely because they are important to know, but because they reflect the day-to-day reality of professional practice. A psychologist who understands dual relationships only in the abstract may not recognize one unfolding in their own caseload. A clinician unfamiliar with the legal standard for malpractice may fail to take the very steps that would have prevented a lawsuit. Throughout this chapter, we will ground each concept in realistic clinical scenarios so that these principles become part of your professional instinct — not just material to memorize, but wisdom to practice by. Let us begin where the stakes are highest: understanding when professional conduct crosses the line into liability.

Malpractice and Professional Liability

Imagine a psychologist who, during a crisis session, fails to assess a client's suicidal ideation despite clear warning signs — and the client later attempts suicide. The client's family files a lawsuit. Was the psychologist negligent? What standard is used to judge their conduct? And what must the family prove in court to prevail? These are the questions at the heart of malpractice law, and they sit squarely at the intersection of clinical practice and legal accountability. Understanding malpractice isn't just about passing an exam — it's about knowing the legal framework that shapes every clinical decision you make, from the moment a professional relationship begins to the way it ends.

Having surveyed the landscape of professional practice issues — from licensure to forensic roles — we turn now to one of the most consequential topics for any practicing psychologist: Malpractice. In legal terms, malpractice is a form of professional negligence in which a practitioner's conduct falls below the accepted standard of care, resulting in harm to the client. Unlike criminal proceedings, which are brought by the state and may result in imprisonment, malpractice claims against psychologists are civil actions — meaning they are initiated by the injured party (or their representative) and typically seek monetary damages. The standard against which a psychologist's behavior is measured is not perfection; rather, it is what a reasonably competent practitioner with similar training and in a similar situation would have done. This distinction matters enormously: a poor outcome alone does not constitute malpractice. The question is always whether the psychologist's professional conduct met the accepted standard of care.

The Four D's of Malpractice

When a malpractice lawsuit proceeds to court, the plaintiff — typically the client or the client's family — bears the burden of proof. They must demonstrate, by a preponderance of the evidence, that all four essential elements are present. These elements are commonly referred to as the "Four D's," and they provide a clear, memorable framework for understanding how negligence is legally established. Think of them as four links in a chain: if even one link is missing, the entire claim collapses.

The Four D's

1. Duty: A professional relationship existed, creating a duty of care. This duty typically arises the moment a psychologist agrees to see a client, though it can also emerge in supervisory or consultative relationships. 2. Dereliction: The psychologist breached (deviated from) the standard of care. For example, a psychologist who never conducts a suicide risk assessment despite a client reporting hopelessness and a plan has arguably deviated from what competent practitioners would do. 3. Damage: The client suffered actual harm or injury — emotional, physical, or financial. Without demonstrable harm, there is no actionable claim, even if the psychologist's conduct was substandard. 4. Direct causation: The breach of duty directly caused the damage. The plaintiff must show a clear causal link between the psychologist's negligence and the harm suffered, not merely that the two events occurred in sequence.

All four elements must be established for a malpractice claim to succeed. Consider this scenario: a psychologist uses an outdated assessment tool (arguably a dereliction of the standard of care), but the client suffers no adverse consequences as a result. Because there is no damage, there is no viable malpractice claim — even though the psychologist's practice was subpar. Conversely, a client may experience significant harm during treatment, but if the psychologist acted in accordance with the accepted standard of care, there is no dereliction, and the claim fails. This is why mastering the Four D's is essential — they clarify not only what constitutes malpractice, but also what does not.

Common Grounds for Malpractice Claims

While the Four D's provide the legal scaffolding for any malpractice action, it helps to know the specific types of professional conduct that most frequently give rise to these claims. Some categories, like sexual misconduct, are both the most ethically egregious and among the most commonly litigated. Others, like negligent supervision, underscore the legal principle that a psychologist's responsibility extends beyond their own direct clinical work. The table below summarizes the major categories of malpractice claims that psychologists should be aware of, along with their defining features.

Claim TypeDescription
Sexual misconductSexual relationships with clients; most common basis for ethics complaints and malpractice suits against psychologists
Breach of confidentialityUnauthorized disclosure of client information
Failure to warn/protectFailure to take reasonable steps when a client poses a danger to an identifiable third party (Tarasoff duty)
AbandonmentImproper termination of the therapeutic relationship without adequate notice or referral
Negligent diagnosis or treatmentFailure to properly assess, diagnose, or treat; use of inappropriate treatment methods
Failure to obtain informed consentTreating without adequate disclosure of risks, benefits, and alternatives
Negligent supervisionSupervisor liability for acts of supervisees (vicarious liability/respondeat superior)
DefamationFalse statements in reports or testimony that harm the client's reputation

Several of these categories deserve special attention. Abandonment, for instance, does not mean that a psychologist can never end treatment — rather, it means that termination must be handled responsibly, with adequate notice and appropriate referrals. A psychologist who abruptly stops seeing a high-risk client without arranging alternative care may face a credible abandonment claim. Similarly, failure to obtain informed consent is not merely a paperwork issue; it reflects the fundamental ethical principle that clients have the right to make autonomous decisions about their care, armed with adequate information about risks, benefits, and alternatives. Negligent supervision is particularly important for those who train or oversee others, because under the legal doctrine of respondeat superior ("let the master answer"), a supervisor can be held vicariously liable for the harmful actions of a supervisee — even if the supervisor was unaware of the specific conduct at the time.

EPPP Tip

The most common reason for ethics complaints against psychologists is sexual dual relationships. The most common reason for malpractice payouts varies by source but includes sexual misconduct, failure to warn/protect, and incorrect treatment. Know the Four D's cold -- they appear frequently on the EPPP. When you encounter a malpractice question on the exam, systematically check for the presence or absence of each "D" — this disciplined approach will help you avoid being misled by answer choices that describe poor practice but fail to meet all four legal requirements for a successful claim.

Supervision Models and Responsibilities

Imagine a newly licensed psychologist reflecting on the mentors who shaped her clinical skills — the supervisor who modeled how to sit with a client's silence, the one who challenged her case conceptualizations until they sharpened into something genuinely useful, and the one who caught a dangerous blind spot before it led to harm. Clinical supervision is more than a training requirement; it is the primary mechanism through which competence is transmitted across professional generations, and it carries some of the most significant ethical and legal responsibilities in all of professional psychology. Having just explored how malpractice liability operates when clinicians cause harm, we now turn to a context where liability can flow uphill — from supervisee to supervisor — making this topic essential for understanding the full landscape of professional accountability.

Supervision sits at the intersection of education, clinical practice, and gatekeeping. A supervisor simultaneously teaches skills, protects clients from harm, evaluates trainee fitness, and models the ethical commitments of the profession. Because supervisees are not yet fully autonomous practitioners, the supervisor bears responsibility not only for the quality of their own guidance but also for the clinical outcomes of the supervisee's work. This dual layer of responsibility — for what the supervisee does and for what the supervisor fails to do — makes supervision one of the most legally consequential roles a psychologist can hold. Understanding the models that guide how supervision is conducted, and the legal doctrines that govern who is responsible when things go wrong, is critical for both the EPPP and for competent professional practice.

Models of Supervision

Just as therapy can be conducted from multiple theoretical perspectives, supervision has evolved its own set of organizing frameworks. Some models import the lens of a particular therapeutic orientation into the supervisory relationship itself, while others step back to address the developmental trajectory of the supervisee or the flexible roles a supervisor must adopt. Knowing these models helps you recognize what each emphasizes — and anticipate how EPPP questions will distinguish among them.

ModelKey Features
Psychotherapy-based modelsApply a specific therapeutic orientation to supervision (e.g., psychodynamic supervision focuses on transference/countertransference in the supervisory relationship; CBT supervision uses skill-building and behavioral rehearsal)
Developmental models (e.g., Stoltenberg & Delworth's IDM)Supervisees progress through stages of increasing competence; supervisors adapt their style (from more directive to more consultative) as the supervisee develops. Three levels: beginner (high motivation, limited skills), intermediate (fluctuating motivation, growing awareness), advanced (stable motivation, strong skills)
Social role models (e.g., Bernard's Discrimination Model)Supervisor flexibly adopts different roles (teacher, counselor, consultant) across different focus areas (intervention, conceptualization, personalization) depending on the supervisee's needs
Competency-based supervisionFocuses on developing specific, measurable competencies; aligned with the competence constellation model

Consider how these models play out in real supervision sessions. In a psychotherapy-based model, a psychodynamically oriented supervisor might notice that a trainee consistently avoids discussing a particular client and explore whether parallel process — where dynamics in the therapy relationship are unconsciously replicated in supervision — is at work. A CBT-oriented supervisor, by contrast, might ask the trainee to role-play a cognitive restructuring intervention, then provide structured feedback on technique. The model of supervision determines what the supervisor pays attention to and what counts as learning.

Developmental models offer a particularly intuitive framework. Stoltenberg and Delworth's Integrated Developmental Model (IDM) describes three levels that most trainees will recognize from their own experience. At Level 1, the beginning trainee is often highly motivated and eager but lacks the clinical skills and self-awareness to work independently — they need structure, direct instruction, and frequent reassurance. At Level 2, the intermediate trainee starts developing genuine clinical awareness, but this growth often brings fluctuating confidence and motivation as the complexity of the work becomes more apparent. By Level 3, the advanced trainee demonstrates stable motivation, strong autonomous skills, and the capacity for genuine self-reflection. The supervisor's job is to match their supervisory style to the trainee's developmental level — providing more directive guidance early on and shifting toward a consultative stance as competence grows.

Social role models, most notably Bernard's Discrimination Model, take a different approach by focusing on what the supervisor does moment to moment rather than tracking developmental stages. In this model, the supervisor selects from three roles — teacher (providing didactic instruction), counselor (facilitating the supervisee's self-awareness), or consultant (encouraging the supervisee's own problem-solving) — depending on which of three focus areas is most salient: intervention skills, case conceptualization, or personalization (how the supervisee's own reactions affect their clinical work). This creates a flexible 3 × 3 matrix that allows the supervisor to shift approaches fluidly within a single supervision session. For the EPPP, remember that the Discrimination Model is defined by its emphasis on the supervisor's flexibility across roles and focus areas, distinguishing it from models that track stages or apply a single theoretical orientation.

Finally, competency-based supervision reflects the profession's broader movement toward identifying and measuring specific professional competencies — such as assessment, intervention, ethical practice, and cultural humility. Rather than assuming that accumulating clinical hours automatically produces competence, this approach explicitly defines the benchmarks a trainee must demonstrate, tracks progress toward them, and structures supervision around closing identified gaps. It aligns with the competence constellation model and represents the contemporary emphasis on accountability in training.

Legal and Ethical Responsibilities of Supervisors

Now that we have examined how supervision is structured, we need to address a sobering reality: when a supervisee causes harm, the supervisor may be the one held legally accountable. This principle fundamentally shapes how supervisors must approach their role — not just as mentors but as professionals who bear direct legal exposure for what happens in their supervisees' caseloads.

  • Vicarious liability (respondeat superior): Supervisors may be held legally responsible for the negligent acts of their supervisees. The supervisor has a duty to properly oversee the supervisee's work.
  • Direct liability: Supervisors can be held directly liable for their own negligent supervision (e.g., failing to review cases, providing inadequate guidance, failing to identify supervisee impairment).
  • Supervisors must establish clear expectations, provide regular feedback, document supervision sessions, and ensure supervisees practice within their competence.
  • Supervisors must not engage in sexual relationships with supervisees (Standard 7.07).
  • The informed consent process in therapy should include disclosure that the therapist is being supervised and the supervisor's name and role.

The distinction between vicarious liability and direct liability is critical for the EPPP and worth understanding clearly. Vicarious liability — captured by the legal doctrine of respondeat superior ("let the master answer") — means that a supervisor can be held responsible for the supervisee's negligent clinical acts simply by virtue of the supervisory relationship, even if the supervisor personally did nothing wrong beyond failing to catch the problem. Direct liability, by contrast, targets the supervisor's own behavior: Did they review cases adequately? Did they provide appropriate guidance? Did they recognize and address signs of supervisee impairment? A supervisor who rubber-stamps case notes without reading them, who cancels supervision sessions repeatedly, or who ignores red flags about a trainee's clinical performance is creating direct liability exposure — they are being negligent in their own professional duties.

These legal realities underscore why thorough documentation of supervision is not merely good practice — it is a professional safeguard. Supervisors should maintain records of topics discussed, directives given, concerns raised, and actions taken. Similarly, informed consent obligations extend into the supervisory context: clients have a right to know that their therapist is being supervised, who the supervisor is, and how information about their case will be shared within the supervisory relationship. Omitting this disclosure can constitute an ethical violation and weaken both the supervisee's and the supervisor's legal position if a complaint arises.

Example

A pre-doctoral intern provides therapy to a suicidal client but does not conduct a formal risk assessment or develop a safety plan. The supervisor does not review the case or ask about high-risk clients. If the client attempts suicide, the supervisor could be held liable under both vicarious liability (for the intern's negligence) and direct liability (for inadequate supervision).

This example illustrates why supervision is not a passive role. The supervisor in this scenario failed on two independent dimensions: the intern's clinical error creates vicarious liability exposure, and the supervisor's own failure to inquire about high-risk cases constitutes negligent supervision — a separate and independent basis for liability. For the EPPP, when you encounter a question about a supervisee's error, always evaluate both pathways: What did the supervisee do wrong, and what did the supervisor fail to do? Recognizing this dual analysis will help you select the most comprehensive and legally accurate answer.

Licensure and Certification

Imagine you've completed your doctoral degree, finished your internship, accumulated thousands of supervised hours — and a prospective client calls asking for help. Can you see them independently? The answer depends entirely on whether your state has granted you a license to practice. Without it, even the most well-trained psychologist cannot legally offer independent services. As the preceding section on supervision illustrated, the relationship between supervisors and trainees is governed by layers of legal accountability. But what transforms a trainee into an independent practitioner in the first place? That transformation is a legal one, and it hinges on the concepts of licensure, certification, and credentialing — distinctions that carry real consequences for professional practice and that appear frequently on the EPPP.

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they refer to fundamentally different processes with different legal implications. One is a legal requirement enforced by the state; another is a voluntary professional achievement. Confusing them can lead to misunderstandings about what a psychologist is authorized to do — and who has the power to discipline or restrict that authorization. Let's unpack each one carefully.

Key Definitions

TermDefinition
LicensureA state-granted legal authorization to practice psychology. Licensure is mandatory for independent practice and is governed by each state's psychology licensing board. It is a form of statutory law.
CertificationA voluntary credential awarded by a professional organization (not the state) indicating that a psychologist has met specific standards of training and competence in a specialty area. Example: Board certification by ABPP.
CredentialingA general term encompassing both licensure and certification, as well as institutional privileges (e.g., hospital credentialing).
ABPPThe American Board of Professional Psychology offers board certification in specialty areas (clinical, counseling, neuropsychology, forensic, etc.). ABPP certification is voluntary and indicates advanced competence.

Think of it this way: licensure is the gate that allows you into independent practice — no license, no practice. Certification, by contrast, is the badge that signals you've gone above and beyond within a particular specialty. A clinical psychologist might be fully licensed by the state of California and also hold ABPP board certification in clinical neuropsychology. The license gives them the legal right to practice; the ABPP certification tells peers, employers, and the public that they've demonstrated advanced competence in that specialty area. Credentialing is the broadest umbrella term — it covers licensure, certification, and even the institutional review process a hospital might conduct before granting a psychologist admitting privileges.

Typical Licensure Requirements

Although specific requirements vary from state to state, the pathway to licensure follows a broadly consistent trajectory across the United States. Each step is designed to ensure that independently practicing psychologists have the academic foundation, supervised clinical experience, and demonstrated competence necessary to protect the public. The typical requirements include the following:

  • Doctoral degree in psychology from an accredited program (APA or CPA accredited, or equivalent)
  • Supervised professional experience (typically 1-2 years postdoctoral)
  • Passing score on the EPPP (Examination for Professional Practice in Psychology)
  • Additional state-specific requirements (e.g., jurisprudence exam, oral examination, state-specific written exam)
  • Continuing education for license renewal

Notice that licensure is not a one-time achievement — it requires ongoing maintenance through continuing education, ensuring that practitioners stay current with evolving research, ethical standards, and clinical methods. A psychologist who earns a license in one state and later moves to another cannot simply begin practicing in the new jurisdiction; they must meet that state's specific requirements, which may include additional examinations or supervised hours. This state-by-state variation is an important point to remember: because licensure is governed by statutory law at the state level, there is no single national psychology license.

Consider a practical scenario: Dr. Martinez completes her doctorate at an APA-accredited program, finishes two years of postdoctoral supervision, passes the EPPP, and obtains her license in Texas. Five years later, she relocates to New York. Despite her years of experience and her Texas license, she must apply for licensure in New York and satisfy that state's specific requirements — which may include a jurisprudence exam covering New York's mental health laws — before she can see clients independently. This is a direct consequence of licensure being a state-level legal authorization rather than a national credential.

EPPP Tip

Licensure is mandatory and state-regulated; certification (e.g., ABPP) is voluntary and organization-based. Licensure laws define the scope of practice; certification indicates specialty competence within that scope. State licensing boards have the authority to discipline licensees, including suspending or revoking licenses. On exam questions, pay close attention to who has the authority to act: a professional organization like APA can sanction a member or revoke membership, but only a state licensing board can revoke a psychologist's legal right to practice. This distinction between organizational ethics enforcement and state regulatory power is a frequently tested concept.

Scope of Practice

Now that we've established how licensure grants psychologists the legal right to practice — and how licensing boards can discipline those who violate professional standards — a natural follow-up question emerges: what exactly does that license authorize you to do? The answer varies more than you might expect, and getting it wrong can carry serious consequences, including criminal charges for practicing without a license in a discipline you never intended to trespass into.

Consider this scenario: a licensed psychologist in one state routinely conducts neuropsychological assessments, provides psychotherapy, and consults with schools on behavioral interventions. She relocates to a neighboring state and assumes she can continue offering the same services. But her new state's practice act defines psychology more narrowly, excluding certain assessment activities that fall under a different profession's jurisdiction. Without realizing it, she may be practicing outside her legal boundaries — not because her competence changed, but because the law did.

This is the core issue behind scope of practice, which each state defines through its own statutes and regulations. Scope of practice describes the full range of professional activities that a licensed psychologist may legally perform within that jurisdiction. It functions as the legal boundary of your professional identity — everything inside it is permissible (assuming you have the competence), and everything outside it is off-limits, regardless of your training or expertise.

Several key realities shape how scope of practice operates in day-to-day professional life. First, scope varies significantly across states. Some states craft broad practice acts that define psychology expansively — for example, as "the assessment and treatment of mental and emotional disorders" — granting psychologists wide latitude in their professional activities. Other states draft far more narrow definitions that restrict which services psychologists may offer. A dramatic example of this variability involves prescriptive authority: a handful of states now permit psychologists with specialized training to prescribe psychotropic medications, while the vast majority do not. A psychologist who prescribes in one state could face criminal prosecution for the same act in another.

Second, and critically for exam preparation, practicing outside one's legally defined scope of practice can result in charges of practicing without a license. This is true even when the psychologist possesses genuine expertise in the activity. Competence and legal authorization are distinct concepts — you may be highly skilled at something your state's law does not permit you to do. The legal system does not recognize good intentions or advanced training as defenses against scope-of-practice violations.

Finally, scope of practice laws exist primarily as a consumer protection mechanism. By restricting professional activities to individuals who meet defined qualifications, these laws aim to shield the public from unqualified practitioners. This consumer-protection rationale — rather than professional turf protection — provides the legal and ethical justification for scope-of-practice regulation. When you encounter exam questions about why scope of practice laws exist, remember that the answer centers on protecting clients and the public, not on protecting professional guilds.

As you'll see in the next section on prescriptive authority, the boundaries of scope of practice are not static — they evolve as the profession advances, as new competencies develop, and as legislatures respond to changing healthcare needs. Understanding scope of practice as a living, jurisdiction-specific framework is essential for ethical and legally sound professional conduct.

Prescriptive Authority

As we discussed in the preceding section, scope of practice is not a fixed boundary — it shifts as professions evolve, as competencies expand, and as legislatures respond to real-world healthcare demands. Perhaps no issue illustrates this dynamic quality more dramatically than the ongoing movement to grant psychologists the legal authority to prescribe psychotropic medications. This is one of the most consequential and contested professional developments in modern psychology, and it shows up regularly on the EPPP because it sits at the intersection of ethics, law, training standards, and public health policy.

Consider a clinical psychologist working in a rural community mental health center. The nearest psychiatrist is 90 miles away with a six-month waitlist. The psychologist's patient — a 34-year-old veteran with worsening major depressive disorder — desperately needs a medication adjustment, but access barriers make timely psychiatric consultation nearly impossible. Scenarios like this one have driven the push for prescriptive authority, the legal right for specially trained psychologists to prescribe psychotropic medications. Advocates argue that expanding prescriptive privileges addresses critical gaps in mental healthcare access, particularly in underserved rural and military populations where psychiatrists are scarce.

The road to prescriptive authority began with a landmark initiative: the Department of Defense's Psychopharmacology Demonstration Project (PDP), conducted from 1991 to 1997. In this project, a small cohort of military psychologists received intensive training in psychopharmacology and were subsequently authorized to prescribe medications within military settings. The results were striking — independent evaluations concluded that these psychologists prescribed safely and effectively, with outcomes comparable to those of physician prescribers. The PDP provided the empirical foundation that proponents needed, demonstrating that the question was not whether psychologists could prescribe competently, but whether the profession and legal system would allow it.

Building on the PDP's success, several jurisdictions have since enacted legislation granting psychologists prescriptive authority. Currently, psychologists in a small but growing number of jurisdictions have obtained prescriptive authority, including New Mexico (the first state, in 2002), Louisiana, Illinois, Iowa, Idaho, and Colorado, as well as psychologists serving in the U.S. military and the Indian Health Service. Each jurisdiction's law differs in its specifics — for instance, some require a collaborative relationship with a physician, while others grant more independent prescribing privileges — but all share a common requirement: additional postdoctoral training in psychopharmacology, typically a master's-level program in clinical psychopharmacology that covers neuroscience, pharmacology, pathophysiology, and clinical management of medications.

At the national level, the American Psychological Association (APA) has adopted a formal policy supporting the pursuit of prescriptive authority for appropriately trained psychologists, framing it as both a logical extension of psychological expertise and a public health imperative. The APA's model legislation provides a template that state psychological associations can use when seeking to introduce prescriptive authority bills in their legislatures. This institutional support has been instrumental in advancing the movement state by state.

Not everyone agrees, however, and the opposition is worth understanding because it reflects genuine tensions about professional identity and patient safety. The most vocal resistance has come from organized medicine, particularly the American Medical Association (AMA) and the American Psychiatric Association, which argue that prescribing requires the breadth of medical training that physicians receive. Some critics within psychology itself have raised a different concern: that pursuing prescriptive authority fundamentally changes the profession's identity, pulling psychologists away from their distinctive strengths in psychotherapy, assessment, and behavioral science toward a more biomedical model. These internal debates raise important questions about what it means to be a psychologist and how the profession should evolve in a rapidly changing healthcare landscape.

  • Currently, psychologists in a small number of jurisdictions have obtained prescriptive authority (e.g., New Mexico, Louisiana, Illinois, Iowa, Idaho, Colorado, and the U.S. military and Indian Health Service)
  • Prescriptive authority typically requires additional postdoctoral training in psychopharmacology (a master's-level program in clinical psychopharmacology)
  • The APA has adopted a policy supporting the pursuit of prescriptive authority for appropriately trained psychologists
  • Opposition comes primarily from organized medicine (AMA) and some within psychology who argue it changes the profession's identity
  • The Department of Defense's Psychopharmacology Demonstration Project (PDP) demonstrated that appropriately trained psychologists could safely and effectively prescribe medications

For the EPPP, remember the key takeaways: prescriptive authority is jurisdiction-specific and requires specialized postdoctoral training beyond the doctoral degree; the PDP was the pivotal demonstration project that established the evidence base; and the debate around prescriptive authority exemplifies the broader tension between expanding access to care and maintaining traditional professional boundaries. As we turn next to telepsychology, we'll see another domain where evolving technology and changing healthcare needs are pushing the boundaries of how, where, and with whom psychologists practice.
