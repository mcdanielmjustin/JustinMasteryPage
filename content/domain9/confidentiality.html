Overview of Standard 4: Privacy and Confidentiality

During a therapy session, a 17-year-old client reveals that he has been having thoughts of harming a classmate who has been bullying him. He describes a specific plan and names the intended victim. He then looks at his psychologist and says, "You can't tell anyone — everything I say here is confidential, right?" In that moment, the psychologist faces the collision between two foundational ethical obligations: the duty to protect confidential communications and the duty to protect identifiable third parties from serious harm. How she navigates this situation depends on her understanding of Standard 4 — and the legal framework that surrounds it.

Standard 4 of the APA Ethics Code addresses one of the most fundamental principles of psychological practice: the obligation to protect confidential information obtained through professional work. Confidentiality is central to the therapeutic relationship, as clients must feel safe disclosing sensitive information to benefit from treatment. This standard is one of the most heavily tested on the EPPP, with questions frequently focusing on the limits of confidentiality, the duty to warn or protect, HIPAA regulations, and special populations such as minors.

Privacy vs. Confidentiality vs. Privilege

Privacy is the right of individuals to control access to information about themselves. Confidentiality is the ethical obligation of the psychologist not to disclose information obtained in a professional relationship. Privilege (or testimonial privilege) is a legal right that protects clients from having their confidential communications disclosed in legal proceedings without their consent. Privilege belongs to the client, not the psychologist.

Standard 4.01: Maintaining Confidentiality

A psychologist chats with a colleague at a coffee shop about a challenging case — a well-known local business owner struggling with substance use. She omits the client's name but shares enough details that the colleague quickly identifies who it is. No form was signed, no subpoena issued — yet a fundamental ethical boundary has been crossed. Standard 4.01 demands more than good intentions: it requires reasonable precautions, active vigilance, and a deeply internalized commitment to safeguarding client information.

Building on the privacy-confidentiality-privilege distinctions from the preceding section, Standard 4.01 operationalizes confidentiality. Psychologists take reasonable precautions to protect confidential information in any medium — "reasonable" shifting with context, technology, and setting. The obligation encompasses:

  • Information obtained in clinical practice, research, teaching, and consultation
  • Data stored electronically, on paper, or in any other format
  • Information shared orally in sessions, consultations, or supervisory contexts

The scope is expansive: a practicum role-play recording, a cloud-stored survey dataset, a client voicemail — all fall under Standard 4.01. The duty extends beyond active relationships: confidential information must be protected after the relationship ends and, in most jurisdictions, after the client has died. A psychologist who retires and donates filing cabinets to a thrift store without shredding contents has violated this standard. This connects to the professional executor concept — planning for record security in case of incapacity or death.

Confidentiality and Technology-Mediated Services

The growing use of videoconferencing platforms and other digital technologies for delivering psychological services introduces significant confidentiality risks that psychologists must carefully evaluate. When practitioners use consumer-grade communication tools—such as popular video-calling applications—to conduct therapy sessions over the Internet, the primary ethical concern involves the protection of client confidentiality. Many widely used platforms were not designed with healthcare privacy requirements in mind, and their terms of service may permit the parent company to record, store, or access transmitted data. Clients may be unaware that their therapy sessions could potentially be captured, retained, or disclosed by the technology provider.

Psychologists are ethically obligated to take reasonable steps to ensure that any technology used for service delivery provides adequate safeguards for confidential communications. This includes reviewing the platform's privacy policies, understanding its data storage and encryption practices, and determining whether the service complies with HIPAA requirements for electronic transmission of protected health information. Practitioners should use platforms specifically designed for telehealth that offer end-to-end encryption and execute a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with the technology vendor when required by HIPAA.

Clients should be informed during the consent process about the specific technology being used, any known limitations to confidentiality inherent in the platform, and practical steps they can take on their end to enhance privacy (e.g., using a private room, wearing headphones, avoiding shared or public devices).

Clinical Note

Consumer video-calling applications that do not offer HIPAA-compliant configurations pose risks because their corporate policies may allow session data to be recorded, analyzed, or released. Always select telehealth platforms that provide a signed BAA, utilize robust encryption, and are explicitly designed for healthcare communications. Inform clients about these considerations as part of the informed consent discussion.

Standard 4.02: Discussing the Limits of Confidentiality

Psychologists discuss with persons and organizations the relevant limits of confidentiality and the foreseeable uses of information generated through their psychological activities. This discussion should occur:

  • At the outset of the relationship (preferably during the informed consent process)
  • As new circumstances arise that may affect confidentiality
  • When the psychologist's role changes (e.g., from individual to couples therapy)

Confidentiality in Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs)

When psychologists provide services through an Employee Assistance Program (EAP), a unique confidentiality dynamic arises because the employer funds the program while the employee is the client. Employees may fear that their personal disclosures will be reported back to supervisors or human resources, which can significantly inhibit their willingness to seek help or speak openly in sessions. To address this concern, psychologists working within EAP settings have a responsibility to clearly inform employees at the outset of services that confidential information will not be shared with the employer, supervisors, or any other organizational personnel without the employee's explicit written authorization.

This assurance must be communicated directly and unambiguously, as many employees hold mistaken beliefs about the flow of information between EAP providers and their workplace. The psychologist should explain that the employer typically receives only aggregate, de-identified utilization data (e.g., the total number of employees who used the program) and does not receive information about which specific employees sought services or the nature of their presenting concerns. Exceptions to confidentiality—such as mandated reporting obligations or imminent danger situations—should be discussed in the same manner as in any other clinical setting.

Example

An employee is referred to the company's EAP after a conflict with a coworker. During the initial session, the employee asks, "Will my boss find out what I say here?" The psychologist should explain that no information from sessions will be disclosed to supervisors, managers, or human resources without the employee's signed consent. The psychologist should also clarify what limited, non-identifying data the EAP may share with the employer for program evaluation purposes.

Common Limits of Confidentiality

The following situations typically require or permit disclosure of otherwise confidential information:

  1. Danger to self -- When a client poses an imminent risk of suicide or serious self-harm
  2. Danger to others -- When a client poses a credible threat of violence toward an identifiable third party (Tarasoff duty)
  3. Child abuse or neglect -- All 50 states mandate reporting of suspected child abuse or neglect
  4. Elder abuse or abuse of dependent adults -- Most states mandate reporting
  5. Court orders -- A valid court order may require disclosure; a subpoena alone generally does not
  6. Client consent -- Clients may authorize disclosure through a signed release
  7. Insurance and third-party payors -- Information necessary for reimbursement
  8. Consultation -- Psychologists may disclose limited information for consultation purposes, typically without identifying the client
EPPP Tip

A common EPPP trap: A subpoena alone does NOT require disclosure of confidential records. The psychologist should consult an attorney, notify the client, and may assert privilege on the client's behalf. Only a court order (signed by a judge) compels disclosure. Know this distinction well.

The Tarasoff Duty: Duty to Warn and Protect

Perhaps the most dramatic confidentiality exception arises when a client threatens harm. A university student calmly describes his plan to kill a woman who rejected him — naming her, describing when and how. The therapist alerts campus police but does not contact the intended victim. Weeks later, the student kills her. This is not hypothetical: it is the case that produced Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California, one of the most consequential legal decisions in mental health practice and among the most heavily tested EPPP topics. Tarasoff I (1974) -- Duty to Warn

In the initial ruling, the family of Tatiana Tarasoff sued the University of California after a therapist at the university's counseling center failed to warn Tatiana that his client, Prosenjit Poddar, had expressed intent to kill her. The California Supreme Court initially ruled that therapists have a duty to warn identifiable potential victims when a client communicates a credible threat of violence. The court's now-famous language captured the principle starkly: the protective privilege of confidentiality ends where the public peril begins. Under this first ruling, the obligation was specific and narrow — the therapist needed to directly warn the person at risk.

Tarasoff II (1976) -- Duty to Protect

Two years later, the same case returned to the California Supreme Court for rehearing, and the justices recognized that a rigid "warn only" standard was both impractical and clinically limiting. On rehearing, the court expanded the ruling to establish a broader duty to protect, which may be discharged through various means, not solely by warning the potential victim. This was a pivotal shift: rather than prescribing a single action, the court acknowledged that clinicians are in the best position to determine which protective steps are appropriate given the circumstances. Protective actions include:

  • Warning the intended victim directly
  • Notifying law enforcement
  • Hospitalizing the client (voluntary or involuntary)
  • Increasing the frequency of therapy sessions
  • Modifying the treatment plan to reduce risk
  • Other reasonable steps to protect the identified victim

Notice the clinical flexibility built into this list. A therapist treating a client who makes a vague but concerning statement might first intensify sessions and adjust the treatment plan, while a therapist confronted with a specific, imminent threat might need to simultaneously call law enforcement and contact the intended victim. The standard is one of reasonableness — what would a prudent mental health professional do under similar circumstances? For the EPPP, remember that Tarasoff II's "duty to protect" is the broader, more current standard, and it superseded the narrower "duty to warn" from Tarasoff I.

Elements Required for Tarasoff Duty

Most jurisdictions require: (1) a special relationship exists between the therapist and the dangerous individual (i.e., a treatment relationship); (2) the client makes a credible, serious threat of physical violence; and (3) the threat is directed toward an identifiable potential victim.

These three elements serve as a practical threshold for clinicians. Without a treatment relationship, there is no special duty. Without a credible threat — as opposed to vague frustration or passing anger — the duty is not triggered. And without an identifiable victim, the clinician has no one specific to protect. Consider a client who says, "Sometimes I just want to hurt everyone." While this warrants clinical attention and thorough risk assessment, it likely does not meet the Tarasoff threshold because there is no identifiable intended victim. Contrast this with a client who says, "I have a gun, and I'm going to shoot my neighbor David when he gets home tonight." Here, all three elements converge: special relationship, credible threat, and identifiable victim.

Variations Across Jurisdictions

One of the most important — and most frequently tested — nuances of the Tarasoff duty is that it does not apply uniformly across the United States. Not all states have adopted the Tarasoff ruling. Some states have enacted statutes that impose a duty to warn or protect, while others have rejected it entirely. Texas, for instance, has historically not recognized a Tarasoff-type duty, while states like California and many others have codified it in statute. Some jurisdictions frame the duty as mandatory (the clinician must take protective action), while others frame it as permissive (the clinician may breach confidentiality to protect a potential victim without fear of liability, but is not required to do so). On the EPPP, the general principle tested is awareness that such a duty exists and knowing the basic elements required to trigger it.

For exam purposes, unless a question specifies a particular state's law, apply the general Tarasoff II framework: when a therapist has a special relationship with a client who makes a credible, serious threat against an identifiable victim, the therapist has a duty to take reasonable steps to protect that person. Also keep in mind that the Tarasoff duty represents a legally mandated exception to confidentiality — it is not something the psychologist chooses to do as a matter of personal judgment, but something the law requires in jurisdictions that have adopted the standard.

Example

A client tells their therapist, "I'm going to kill my ex-wife when she picks up the kids on Friday." This statement involves a serious threat, an identifiable victim, and an apparent timeline. The therapist has a duty to take protective action, which may include warning the ex-wife, contacting police, and potentially seeking involuntary hospitalization for the client.

Before we move on to HIPAA and federal privacy protections, remember this key takeaway: the Tarasoff duty is among the most critical exceptions to confidentiality you will encounter — both on the EPPP and in clinical practice. When in doubt, think through the three elements (special relationship, credible threat, identifiable victim), consider the full range of protective actions available to you, and document your reasoning thoroughly. A clinician who conducts a careful risk assessment and takes reasonable protective steps is far better positioned — ethically, legally, and clinically — than one who either ignores a threat or reflexively breaches confidentiality without adequate justification.

The HIPAA Privacy Rule (2003) established federal standards for protecting individually identifiable health information (Protected Health Information, or PHI). Key provisions for psychologists include:

Psychotherapy Notes Under HIPAA

Psychotherapy notes are afforded greater protection than other PHI. Authorization from the client is generally required for disclosure of psychotherapy notes, even to insurance companies. Psychotherapy notes do NOT include:

  • Medication prescription and monitoring
  • Session start and stop times
  • Treatment modalities and frequencies
  • Results of clinical tests
  • Diagnosis, functional status, treatment plan, symptoms, prognosis, and progress to date
EPPP Tip

HIPAA psychotherapy notes are ONLY the process notes about the content of therapy sessions, kept separately from the medical record. Treatment plans, diagnoses, and progress notes are NOT psychotherapy notes under HIPAA and can be shared for treatment, payment, and healthcare operations without special authorization.

Confidentiality with Minors

Working with minors presents unique confidentiality challenges because the legal right to consent (and access information) typically belongs to the parent or legal guardian, while the therapeutic relationship depends on the minor's trust.

  • Parents generally have the right to access their minor child's treatment records.
  • However, many practitioners negotiate a limited confidentiality agreement at the outset, explaining to both parents and the minor what will and will not be shared.
  • The psychologist should inform the minor about the limits of confidentiality in age-appropriate language.
  • In many states, minors can consent to their own mental health treatment at age 12-16 (depending on the jurisdiction), which may alter confidentiality obligations.
  • Mandatory reporting obligations (e.g., child abuse) always override confidentiality agreements with minors.

Confidentiality in Group Therapy

In a substance abuse recovery group, one member discloses a relapse. The therapist is bound by confidentiality — but the eight other group members are not. Any could mention it to a mutual acquaintance or post about it on social media. This reality makes group therapy confidentiality one of the most frequently tested EPPP concepts, paralleling the group therapy disclosure obligations in Standard 10.03.

The ethical obligation shifts from guaranteeing confidentiality to managing expectations and minimizing risk. Key obligations in group settings:

  • Discuss the importance of confidentiality with all group members at the outset
  • Establish group rules regarding confidentiality
  • Inform members that the psychologist cannot guarantee that other group members will not disclose information
  • Address breaches of confidentiality if they occur within the group

Consider how this plays out in practice. A psychologist leading an anxiety management group at a university counseling center begins the first session by explaining that everything shared within the group should remain within the group — and then adds, clearly and without hedging, that while the therapist is ethically and legally bound to protect their disclosures, other group members are not held to the same standard. This honest framing serves a dual purpose: it respects participants' autonomy by allowing them to make informed decisions about what they share, and it protects the therapist from liability should a breach occur. Group members might then collaboratively develop a confidentiality agreement — a shared commitment that, while not legally enforceable, sets a clear behavioral norm.

What happens when a breach does occur? Suppose a member of a grief support group learns that another member mentioned her late husband's cause of death to a coworker. The psychologist has an obligation to address this breach within the group — not punitively, but therapeutically. This might involve revisiting the group's confidentiality norms, processing the feelings of betrayal and vulnerability the breach has caused, and reinforcing the expectation that disclosures stay within the room. In some cases, a breach may be so damaging that it disrupts the therapeutic environment entirely, requiring the psychologist to make clinical judgments about the group's continued viability or composition.

The critical takeaway for the EPPP is this: the psychologist's duty is to inform, educate, and establish norms around confidentiality in group settings — but the psychologist cannot guarantee the behavior of non-professionals. This distinction between the therapist's obligation (which is robust) and the therapist's power (which is limited) is a nuance that exam questions frequently probe. Understanding this distinction will help you navigate not only test items but also the real-world complexities of running effective and ethically sound group treatments.

Mandated Reporting

Having explored the unique confidentiality challenges in group therapy — where the therapist cannot guarantee that fellow members will protect each other's disclosures — we now turn to a situation where the psychologist is not merely permitted but legally compelled to break confidentiality. Imagine this scenario: during an individual session, a parent casually mentions disciplining their six-year-old by holding the child's hand over a hot stove burner. The child, the parent explains, "needs to learn consequences." Your stomach drops. You know this conversation has just triggered a legal obligation that supersedes everything else in the therapeutic relationship — your duty as a mandated reporters to file a report with child protective services.

In all 50 U.S. states, psychologists are legally designated as mandated reporters for suspected child abuse and neglect. This is not a discretionary ethical guideline — it is a legal requirement backed by statute. The threshold for reporting is critically important and often misunderstood by early-career clinicians: reporting is based on reasonable suspicion, not certainty. You do not need to verify the abuse, gather corroborating evidence, or conduct any kind of independent investigation before making a report. In fact, attempting to investigate on your own can delay the report and may itself constitute a violation. If the information you have would lead a reasonable person to suspect that abuse or neglect has occurred, your obligation is triggered immediately.

Most states extend these mandated reporting requirements beyond children to include elder abuse and abuse of dependent adults — individuals who, because of age, disability, or other vulnerabilities, may be unable to protect themselves. A psychologist treating an elderly patient who arrives with unexplained bruises and expresses fear of a caregiver faces the same legal imperative as one who suspects a child is being harmed. The specific definitions and procedures vary by jurisdiction, which is why familiarity with your state's reporting statutes is not optional — it is a core professional competency.

The consequences of failing to report when mandated are severe and multifaceted. A psychologist who does not file a required report may face criminal penalties (including fines and, in some jurisdictions, misdemeanor charges), civil liability if the victim suffers additional harm that a timely report might have prevented, and formal ethics complaints that can jeopardize licensure. Consider the weight of that combination: a single failure to act can simultaneously expose you to prosecution, a lawsuit, and the loss of your professional standing.

To encourage compliance and protect those who report in good faith, most states have enacted immunity statutes that shield mandated reporters from civil or criminal liability even if the report ultimately proves unfounded. This legal protection exists precisely because the system depends on reporters acting on suspicion rather than certainty — and the cost of a false report is deemed far less harmful than the cost of an unreported case of abuse. As long as the report is made in good faith and based on reasonable suspicion, the reporter is protected.

Perhaps the most important principle for exam preparation — and for clinical practice — is this: mandated reporting obligations override both confidentiality and privilege. No therapeutic relationship, no matter how clinically sensitive or legally protected, can shield a psychologist from the duty to report suspected abuse. When a client discloses information that triggers a mandated report, the psychologist must act, even if doing so disrupts the therapeutic alliance. This is one of the clearest examples in professional psychology of a situation where the law draws a bright line that the clinician cannot negotiate around, and it underscores why informed consent at the outset of treatment should always include a clear explanation of the limits of confidentiality — including mandated reporting obligations.

Standard 4.05: Disclosures

Imagine you are treating a client who has recently expressed suicidal ideation. In the same week, an insurance company calls requesting session notes for reimbursement, a colleague asks you to consult on a similar case, and your client's estranged parent phones demanding to know what their adult child has been discussing in therapy. Each of these situations involves a potential disclosure of confidential information — and each demands a different response. Standard 4.05 provides the ethical framework for navigating exactly these moments, spelling out when disclosure is permissible and under what conditions.

The APA Ethics Code recognizes that confidentiality, while foundational, is not absolute. Psychologists may disclose confidential information when they have obtained the appropriate consent of the client or when disclosure is mandated or permitted by law. Crucially, any such disclosure must serve a valid purpose. Standard 4.05 identifies four recognized categories of permissible disclosure:

  • Providing professional services to the client
  • Obtaining professional consultations
  • Protecting the client, psychologist, or others from harm
  • Obtaining payment for services

Notice the logic behind these categories: each one reflects a situation in which withholding information would actually undermine the client's welfare, the safety of others, or the functioning of the professional relationship itself. A psychologist who refuses to share any diagnostic information with an insurance company, for example, may inadvertently prevent the client from receiving the coverage they need. Likewise, a psychologist who never consults with colleagues out of rigid adherence to confidentiality may miss clinical insights that would improve treatment. The key ethical skill is discerning which disclosures are justified and limiting what is shared to the minimum necessary for the purpose at hand.

One of the most sensitive disclosure questions arises when a client passes away. A grieving spouse calls your office, tearfully asking to read their partner's therapy notes — perhaps hoping to understand struggles the client never shared openly. The request feels deeply human and reasonable. But ethically and legally, the question is not whether the request is sympathetic; it is whether the person making it has the legal authority to access the deceased client's protected information.

Under most legal frameworks, the authority to consent to disclosure of a deceased person's confidential records transfers to the personal representative of the decedent. This individual is typically the executor named in the person's will, the administrator of the estate appointed by a court, or another legally designated representative. Psychologists should verify the legal standing of any individual requesting access to a deceased client's records before releasing information. A family member who is not serving in a formal legal capacity — for example, an adult child who was emotionally close to the client but was not named as executor — does not automatically have the right to obtain confidential records, even if the request seems reasonable or well-intentioned. HIPAA similarly recognizes the personal representative of a deceased individual as the party authorized to exercise the decedent's rights with respect to protected health information. This means that death does not dissolve confidentiality; it simply transfers the authority to authorize disclosure to a legally defined party.

Clinical Note

When a client dies, a spouse or family member may contact you requesting therapy records. Before disclosing any information, request documentation establishing that the person is the legally authorized representative of the estate (e.g., letters testamentary, court appointment as administrator). Absent such documentation, the psychologist should decline the request and direct the individual to obtain proper legal authorization. Responding with empathy while maintaining this boundary is essential — you can acknowledge the person's grief and explain the legal requirements without being dismissive or cold.

Standard 4.06: Consultations

Having just explored the conditions under which psychologists may disclose confidential information, it is important to focus on one of the most common everyday disclosure scenarios: consultation with colleagues. Consultation is a cornerstone of competent practice — it helps psychologists refine diagnostic impressions, navigate ethical dilemmas, and improve treatment planning. But every consultation carries a confidentiality risk, because sharing clinical details with a colleague inherently moves private information beyond the therapy room.

Standard 4.06 addresses this tension directly. When consulting with colleagues, psychologists do not disclose confidential information that could reasonably lead to the identification of a client unless they have obtained prior consent or the disclosure cannot be avoided. Even when some disclosure is necessary, they share only information essential to the consultation purpose — nothing more. Consider a psychologist who wants a colleague's perspective on a complex trauma case. Rather than saying, "My client Maria, the local school principal, described the following incident," the psychologist should strip identifying details: "I'm working with a middle-aged professional in a leadership role who presented with the following symptoms." This practice of de-identification honors the spirit of Standard 4.06 and protects the client while still allowing the consulting psychologist to benefit from peer expertise.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Privilege belongs to the client, not the therapist. Only the client (or their legal representative) can waive privilege.
  • A subpoena alone does not compel disclosure; a court order does. When receiving a subpoena, consult an attorney and notify the client.
  • The Tarasoff duty requires a special relationship, a credible serious threat, and an identifiable victim. The duty to protect can be fulfilled by means other than warning the victim.
  • Under HIPAA, psychotherapy notes (process notes kept separately) receive extra protection and generally require separate authorization for disclosure.
  • With minors, parents generally have access to records, but practitioners should negotiate confidentiality limits at the outset.
  • In group therapy, the psychologist cannot guarantee other members' confidentiality but must establish clear expectations.
  • Mandated reporting (child abuse, elder abuse) overrides confidentiality and privilege, and is based on reasonable suspicion, not certainty.
