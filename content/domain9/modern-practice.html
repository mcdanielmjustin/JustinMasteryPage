Telepsychology

A New York psychologist conducts therapy with a student who has returned to rural Montana for the summer — no mental health providers within 90 miles. A clinician provides CBT to an agoraphobic client who by definition struggles to leave home. Telepsychology — the provision of psychological services via telecommunication technologies — has moved from niche convenience to core competency, accelerated dramatically by COVID-19.

Key ethical and legal considerations for responsible telepsychology practice:

  • Competence: Psychologists must be competent in the use of the technology and understand its limitations. A clinician who is unfamiliar with videoconferencing software may miss nonverbal cues, fail to troubleshoot connection issues during a crisis moment, or inadvertently use a platform that lacks adequate security features. Competence here extends beyond technical skill to include understanding how the medium may affect the therapeutic relationship and assessment validity.
  • Informed consent: Must address the unique risks of telehealth (privacy, technology failures, emergency protocols). For instance, a client should understand that a Wi-Fi disruption could interrupt a session at a critical moment, that family members in adjacent rooms might overhear the conversation, and what the plan is if the connection drops during a discussion of suicidal ideation.
  • Confidentiality: Use of encrypted, HIPAA-compliant platforms; awareness of risks to privacy in the client's environment. A psychologist might use a fully encrypted telehealth platform, but if the client is taking the session on a shared family computer in a busy living room, confidentiality is compromised. Clinicians should routinely ask clients about their physical environment at the start of each session.
  • Interjurisdictional practice: Psychologists must be licensed in the state where the client is located (this is the most common regulatory challenge); PSYPACT (Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact) allows telepsychology across participating states. This is a high-yield EPPP topic — remember, the governing jurisdiction is determined by the client's location, not the provider's.
  • Emergency management: Providers must have a plan for managing crises when the client is at a remote location (e.g., knowing local emergency resources). Before beginning telepsychology services, clinicians should confirm the client's physical address, identify the nearest emergency room, and have contact information for local crisis services and law enforcement in the client's area.
  • APA Guidelines: The APA published Guidelines for the Practice of Telepsychology (2013) addressing these issues. These guidelines were groundbreaking in establishing a framework for ethical telepsychology practice and continue to serve as the primary professional reference for navigating the complexities of technology-mediated services.

Of all these considerations, interjurisdictional practice is the most legally consequential. Without proper authorization to practice in Montana, the New York psychologist violates Montana's licensing laws regardless of treatment quality. PSYPACT was designed to solve this problem, connecting to the licensing framework in the Legal & Professional chapter.

PSYPACT

The Psychology Interjurisdictional Compact (PSYPACT) is an interstate agreement that allows licensed psychologists in participating states to practice telepsychology and conduct temporary in-person practice across state lines without requiring additional licensure in each state. Modeled after similar compacts in nursing and medicine, PSYPACT streamlines the regulatory landscape by enabling psychologists who hold an Authority to Practice Interjurisdictional Telepsychology (APIT) or a Temporary Authorization to Practice (TAP) to serve clients in other member states. For exam purposes, remember that PSYPACT does not eliminate licensure requirements — rather, it creates a structured mechanism for mutual recognition among participating jurisdictions, significantly reducing barriers to care for clients in underserved or remote areas.

Forensic Psychology Issues

You have treated a client for two years. Then the client's defense attorney asks you to evaluate competency to stand trial. Something feels deeply wrong — and it should. Forensic psychology applies psychological knowledge to legal questions, but the psychologist's allegiance shifts from client welfare to the legal system's need for accurate, impartial information. Trying to serve both mandates simultaneously compromises the integrity of each — which is why Standard 2.01(f) on forensic competence requires understanding judicial rules before accepting these roles.

Key Forensic Concepts

  • Dual-role conflicts: Psychologists should generally avoid serving as both therapist and forensic evaluator for the same client. The therapeutic relationship is built on empathy, trust, and advocacy for the client's well-being, while the forensic role demands impartiality and allegiance to the truth. Occupying both positions simultaneously creates conflicting obligations that can undermine the validity of the evaluation and harm the therapeutic relationship.
  • Competence to stand trial: The legal standard for competency, established in Dusky v. United States (1960), requires that the defendant has both a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against them and the ability to consult meaningfully with their attorney. This is the most commonly requested forensic evaluation in criminal cases — and it is assessed at the time of trial, not at the time of the offense.
  • Insanity defense: Unlike competency (which is present-focused), the insanity defense looks backward to the defendant's mental state at the time of the offense. Various legal standards exist, including the M'Naghten rule, the ALI/Model Penal Code standard, and the irresistible impulse test. Most require demonstrating that the defendant did not know the nature or quality of the act or did not know that what they were doing was wrong. Forensic psychologists must understand which standard applies in the relevant jurisdiction.
  • Custody evaluations: In child custody disputes, the guiding legal principle is the "best interests of the child." Evaluators must remain impartial — they are not advocates for either parent — and must assess all relevant parties, including both parents and the child. A common error is conducting a custody evaluation after meeting with only one parent, which violates the standard of impartiality.
  • Risk assessment: Predicting future violence is one of the most consequential tasks in forensic psychology. Approaches range from unstructured clinical judgment (which is least reliable) to actuarial tools that use statistical algorithms, to structured professional judgment that combines empirical guidelines with clinical expertise. Understanding the strengths and limitations of each approach is critical for ethical and defensible practice.

Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychology

Given the high stakes of forensic work — where a psychologist's opinion can influence whether someone goes to prison, retains custody of a child, or is involuntarily committed — the profession has established dedicated ethical guidance. The APA published the Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychology (2013) to provide a framework for navigating the unique demands of this work. These guidelines do not replace the general APA Ethics Code but rather supplement it with forensic-specific considerations. Key areas addressed include:

  • The importance of impartiality and objectivity in forensic work — forensic psychologists strive to be unbiased regardless of which party retained them
  • Clarification of the psychologist's role, including identifying who the client is — the retaining attorney, the court, or the examinee — because this determination shapes the psychologist's obligations regarding confidentiality, informed consent, and reporting
  • Appropriate methods for conducting forensic assessments, including the use of instruments validated for forensic populations and questions
  • Limits on providing opinions beyond the scope of the available data — forensic psychologists should not overstate conclusions or offer opinions unsupported by their evaluation findings
  • The distinction between therapeutic and forensic roles, reinforcing that psychologists should not blur these boundaries

One of the most practical takeaways from these guidelines is the emphasis on informed consent in forensic contexts. Unlike therapy, where the client voluntarily seeks help, forensic examinees are often compelled to participate. The psychologist must clearly explain the nature and purpose of the evaluation, who will receive the results, and the limits of confidentiality — before the evaluation begins. Failing to do so not only violates ethical guidelines but can also render the evaluation's findings legally inadmissible.

Example

A psychologist is asked by an attorney to evaluate a client for competency to stand trial. The psychologist has been providing therapy to this individual for two years. The psychologist should decline the forensic evaluation because serving as both therapist and forensic evaluator creates a dual-role conflict. The therapeutic relationship is built on empathy and advocacy, while the forensic evaluation demands objectivity and impartiality — and occupying both roles simultaneously compromises each one. The psychologist should recommend that an independent forensic evaluator conduct the competency assessment, and can offer to provide treatment records if the client provides appropriate authorization.

Other Professional Issues

Duty to Report Colleague Impairment

When a psychologist becomes aware that a colleague may be impaired (due to substance abuse, mental illness, or personal problems), the first step is typically informal resolution (Standard 1.04) -- speaking directly with the colleague. If the impairment poses a risk of harm to clients and informal resolution is ineffective, reporting to the licensing board or ethics committee may be necessary (Standard 1.05).

Record Keeping and Retention

  • APA guidelines recommend retaining records for a minimum of 7 years after the last date of service for adults, and 3 years after a minor reaches age of majority
  • State laws may impose different (often longer) retention requirements
  • Records must be maintained securely and disposed of in a manner that protects confidentiality (e.g., shredding, secure electronic deletion)
  • Psychologists should plan for record transfer in the event of incapacity, retirement, or death

Termination and Abandonment

Psychologists terminate therapy when it becomes reasonably clear that the client no longer needs the service, is not likely to benefit, or is being harmed. Except where precluded by the actions of the client, the psychologist provides pretermination counseling and suggests alternative service providers as appropriate. Abrupt termination without reasonable notice and referral options may constitute abandonment.

Managed Care Issues

  • Psychologists must balance the financial constraints of managed care with their ethical obligations to clients
  • If a managed care organization limits sessions, the psychologist must inform the client and advocate for continued treatment if clinically needed
  • Psychologists should not provide inaccurate diagnoses solely to obtain insurance reimbursement

Social Media and Electronic Communications Ethics

The rapid expansion of digital technology has introduced new ethical challenges for psychologists. Social media, email, text messaging, online therapy platforms, and other electronic communications create novel boundary, confidentiality, and competence issues that psychologists must navigate carefully.

APA Telepsychology Guidelines (2013)

The APA published the Guidelines for the Practice of Telepsychology in 2013, providing a framework for the ethical delivery of psychological services via telecommunication technologies. Key principles include:

  • Informed consent for telehealth: Psychologists must obtain informed consent that specifically addresses the unique risks of technology-mediated services, including potential breaches of confidentiality, technology failures, procedures for emergencies, and the limitations of the medium (e.g., inability to read nonverbal cues fully via telephone).
  • Competence in technology: Psychologists must ensure they are competent not only in the clinical services they provide but also in the technology used to deliver them. This includes understanding encryption, platform security, data storage, and how to troubleshoot common technical issues.
  • Confidentiality risks: Telehealth introduces risks that do not exist in traditional face-to-face practice -- data breaches, unsecured Wi-Fi networks, screen visibility in the client's environment, and the possibility that others may overhear or observe sessions on the client's end.
  • Interjurisdictional practice: Psychologists must be aware of and comply with laws governing the practice of psychology in the jurisdiction where the client is located, not just where the psychologist is located.
Informed Consent for Telehealth

Informed consent for telepsychology must address, at minimum: the nature of the service, the technology being used, potential risks to confidentiality (e.g., data breaches, recording), procedures for technology failure, emergency protocols including local resources near the client, whether sessions may be recorded, and the limitations of the technology for the specific type of service being provided.

Social Media Boundary Issues

Social media has blurred the boundaries between the personal and professional lives of psychologists. Key ethical considerations include:

  • Googling clients: Searching for client information online without their knowledge or consent raises ethical concerns regarding privacy and the therapeutic relationship. Some argue it can compromise clinical objectivity by introducing extraclinical information. The APA has not issued a blanket prohibition, but psychologists should carefully consider whether the information is clinically necessary, document the rationale, and consider discussing the search with the client.
  • Accepting friend/follow requests: Psychologists should generally not accept friend or follow requests from current clients on personal social media accounts, as this constitutes a boundary crossing (or boundary violation) that compromises professional objectivity and confidentiality. Even acknowledging a therapeutic relationship online could breach confidentiality.
  • Social media discovery: Clients may search for information about their psychologist online. Psychologists should proactively manage their digital footprint and consider discussing social media boundaries during the informed consent process.
  • Testimonials and endorsements: The APA Ethics Code (Standard 5.05) prohibits psychologists from soliciting testimonials from current clients or others who, because of their circumstances, are vulnerable to undue influence. Online reviews and social media endorsements present modern challenges to this standard.
Example

A psychologist receives a Facebook friend request from a current therapy client. Accepting the request would create a dual relationship (personal and professional), expose the psychologist's personal life to the client and the client's personal posts to the psychologist, and could inadvertently reveal the therapeutic relationship to mutual connections. The appropriate response is to decline the request and address the issue therapeutically in the next session, discussing boundaries and the rationale for maintaining them.

Email and Text Communication with Clients

  • Email and text messages sent between psychologists and clients become part of the clinical record and are potentially discoverable in legal proceedings.
  • Standard email and SMS are not encrypted and do not meet HIPAA security requirements. Psychologists should use encrypted email platforms or secure patient portals when communicating clinical information electronically.
  • Informed consent should specify the acceptable methods and scope of electronic communication (e.g., email for scheduling only, not clinical content).
  • Response time expectations should be established clearly -- clients should be informed that email and text are not appropriate for emergencies.

Online Therapy Platforms and HIPAA Compliance

The proliferation of online therapy platforms (e.g., video-based telehealth services) has increased access to mental health care but also introduces compliance requirements:

  • All platforms used for telehealth must be HIPAA-compliant, meaning they employ end-to-end encryption, have signed Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) with the provider, and maintain appropriate data storage and access controls.
  • Consumer-grade platforms (e.g., standard Zoom, FaceTime, Skype) were temporarily permitted during the COVID-19 public health emergency, but standard practice requires HIPAA-compliant platforms.
  • Psychologists are responsible for verifying platform compliance regardless of what the platform company claims.

Digital Native vs. Digital Immigrant Considerations

The distinction between digital natives (individuals who grew up with digital technology) and digital immigrants (individuals who adopted technology later in life) is relevant to both service delivery and competence:

  • Younger clients may prefer text-based communication and may have different expectations about response times and platform accessibility.
  • Older clients may be less comfortable with technology and may require additional support in using telehealth platforms.
  • Psychologists must assess each client's technological competence and comfort level as part of the informed consent process for telepsychology.
  • Training programs are increasingly recognizing the need to prepare psychologists for technology-mediated practice, but a significant generational competence gap may exist among practitioners.

Social Media Posts by Psychologists

Psychologists who maintain a social media presence must be mindful of maintaining professional boundaries publicly:

  • Public posts expressing strong political, religious, or social opinions may affect the therapeutic relationship with clients who hold different views and may encounter those posts.
  • Sharing clinical vignettes on social media -- even when de-identified -- risks breaching confidentiality if clients recognize themselves or if the details are insufficient to fully obscure identity.
  • Psychologists should consider maintaining separate personal and professional social media accounts.
  • Self-disclosure through social media is involuntary and ongoing, unlike controlled self-disclosure in therapy, and its effects on the therapeutic relationship may be unpredictable.
EPPP Tip

The EPPP tests whether you understand that existing ethical principles (confidentiality, boundaries, competence, informed consent) apply to new technologies. No fundamentally new ethical principles are created by social media or telehealth -- rather, familiar principles must be applied to novel contexts. When in doubt on an EPPP question about technology ethics, apply the foundational standards (informed consent, confidentiality, competence, multiple relationships) to the technological scenario.

Psychologists -- even those who do not prescribe -- are expected to understand these treatments because they assess patient response, collaborate with prescribers, and may be asked about them by clients. Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT)

ECT involves the administration of a brief, controlled electrical current to the brain under general anesthesia and muscle relaxation, inducing a generalized seizure. Despite its controversial history, ECT remains one of the most effective treatments available for certain severe psychiatric conditions.

  • Mechanism: The exact mechanism is unknown, but theories include enhanced GABAergic transmission, serotonin sensitization, increased BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), normalization of HPA axis function, and anticonvulsant effects from repeated seizure induction.
  • Indications: Severe treatment-resistant depression, depression with psychotic features, acute suicidality (ECT has the fastest onset of antidepressant effect of any treatment), catatonia, severe mania unresponsive to medications, and (rarely) treatment-resistant schizophrenia.
  • Electrode placement: Bilateral (bitemporal) placement is generally more effective but associated with greater cognitive side effects. Right unilateral (non-dominant hemisphere) placement produces fewer cognitive side effects while maintaining efficacy when administered at adequate stimulus intensity (typically 6x seizure threshold).
  • Side effects: The primary cognitive side effect is retrograde amnesia (loss of memories formed before treatment), which is usually temporary but can be persistent for events close in time to the treatment. Anterograde amnesia (difficulty forming new memories) also occurs acutely but typically resolves within weeks. Headache and muscle soreness are common transient effects.
  • Course: A typical acute course involves 6-12 sessions (usually 3 per week), with noticeable improvement often occurring after 2-4 treatments. Maintenance ECT (weekly to monthly sessions) may be used to prevent relapse.
EPPP Tip

ECT is the fastest-acting treatment for severe depression and acute suicidality -- faster than any medication. Right unilateral electrode placement with adequate stimulus intensity is the preferred approach when minimizing cognitive side effects is a priority. The most commonly tested ECT fact: retrograde amnesia is the primary cognitive side effect, and bilateral placement causes more memory impairment than right unilateral.

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS)

TMS is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that uses focused magnetic pulses to stimulate or inhibit neural activity in specific brain regions. It does not require anesthesia or sedation, and the patient remains awake and alert throughout the procedure.

  • Mechanism: A magnetic coil placed against the scalp generates rapidly alternating magnetic fields that induce electrical currents in underlying cortical tissue. Repetitive TMS (rTMS) -- the form used therapeutically -- applies repeated pulses to produce lasting changes in neural excitability.
  • FDA-cleared indications: Treatment-resistant major depressive disorder (MDD) -- the primary indication -- and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). The FDA clearance for depression targets the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), an area associated with reduced activity in depression.
  • High-frequency vs. low-frequency: High-frequency stimulation (e.g., 10 Hz) of the left DLPFC is excitatory and is the standard depression protocol. Low-frequency stimulation (e.g., 1 Hz) is inhibitory and may be used on the right DLPFC or for other applications.
  • Advantages over ECT: No anesthesia required, no seizure induced, no cognitive side effects (no memory impairment), outpatient procedure, can drive immediately after treatment.
  • Limitations: Generally less effective than ECT for severe treatment-resistant depression; requires daily sessions over 4-6 weeks (typical course: 20-30 sessions); rare risk of seizure (~0.1%).

Ketamine and Esketamine

Ketamine / Esketamine

Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist that was originally developed as an anesthetic. It has been found to produce rapid antidepressant effects -- often within hours -- in contrast to traditional antidepressants, which typically require weeks. Esketamine (Spravato) is the S-enantiomer of ketamine, delivered as a nasal spray and FDA-approved for treatment-resistant depression (2019).

  • Mechanism: Ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist that triggers downstream effects including increased AMPA receptor signaling, enhanced BDNF release, and rapid synaptogenesis (formation of new synaptic connections). This represents a fundamentally different mechanism than traditional monoamine-based antidepressants.
  • Onset of action: Antidepressant effects can occur within hours of a single infusion (compared to 4-8 weeks for SSRIs), making ketamine a potentially life-saving option for acutely suicidal patients.
  • Administration: IV ketamine is administered off-label in clinical settings (infusion clinics). Esketamine (Spravato) nasal spray is FDA-approved and must be administered in a certified healthcare setting under direct observation due to dissociative effects and abuse potential (REMS program).
  • Side effects: Dissociative symptoms (feeling detached from reality), dizziness, nausea, increased blood pressure, sedation. Effects are typically transient (resolving within 2 hours).
  • Abuse potential: Ketamine is a Schedule III controlled substance with known abuse and dependence potential. This is a significant concern for long-term use and requires careful patient selection and monitoring.

Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

Research on psychedelic-assisted therapy has re-emerged as a significant area of psychiatric treatment research. Key developments include:

  • Psilocybin for depression: Psilocybin (the active compound in "magic mushrooms") has received FDA breakthrough therapy designation for treatment-resistant depression and major depressive disorder. It acts primarily as a 5-HT2A receptor agonist, producing profound alterations in consciousness that, when combined with psychological support, may lead to lasting improvements in mood and outlook.
  • MDMA for PTSD: MDMA (3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine) has been studied in Phase 3 clinical trials for PTSD. MDMA-assisted therapy involves administering MDMA in conjunction with psychotherapy sessions; the MDMA is hypothesized to reduce fear responses and enhance therapeutic alliance, facilitating trauma processing. MDMA increases release of serotonin, dopamine, norepinephrine, and oxytocin.
  • Therapeutic model: Unlike traditional pharmacotherapy (daily medication), psychedelic-assisted therapy typically involves only 1-3 dosing sessions, each accompanied by extensive preparatory and integration psychotherapy sessions. The psychotherapy component is considered integral to the treatment, not incidental.
  • Regulatory status: As of the mid-2020s, these substances remain Schedule I controlled substances for general use. Clinical use requires specific regulatory authorization (FDA-approved clinical trials, or state-level exemptions where available).
Example: Comparing Novel Biological Treatments

A patient with severe, treatment-resistant depression has failed multiple adequate trials of SSRIs, SNRIs, and augmentation strategies. The treatment team considers the following options: (1) ECT -- fastest onset, most effective for severe/treatment-resistant depression, but requires anesthesia and causes transient memory impairment. (2) TMS -- non-invasive, no cognitive side effects, but less effective than ECT for severe cases and requires daily sessions for weeks. (3) IV ketamine or esketamine -- rapid onset (hours), but effects may be short-lived without repeated dosing, and carries abuse potential. The choice depends on severity, urgency (acute suicidality favors ECT or ketamine), patient preference, and treatment history.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Malpractice requires proving all Four D's: Duty, Dereliction, Damage, and Direct causation.
  • Sexual misconduct is the most common basis for ethics complaints against psychologists.
  • Supervisors are liable for supervisees' acts (vicarious liability) and for their own negligent supervision (direct liability).
  • Licensure is mandatory and state-regulated; certification (e.g., ABPP) is voluntary and indicates specialty competence.
  • Practicing outside the legal scope of practice constitutes practicing without a license.
  • Prescriptive authority for psychologists exists in a limited number of states and requires additional postdoctoral training.
  • In telepsychology, psychologists must be licensed where the client is located; PSYPACT facilitates interstate practice.
  • Forensic psychologists should avoid dual-role conflicts (therapist and forensic evaluator for the same person).
  • Competency to stand trial (Dusky standard) requires rational/factual understanding of proceedings and ability to consult with counsel.
  • APA recommends retaining adult records for 7 years after last service; for minors, 3 years after reaching majority.
  • Termination without adequate notice and referral may constitute abandonment.
  • Telepsychology informed consent must address technology-specific risks (data breaches, emergency protocols, platform security) beyond standard informed consent elements.
  • Psychologists should generally not accept social media friend/follow requests from current clients -- this constitutes a boundary crossing that compromises confidentiality and professional objectivity.
  • Email and text communications with clients become part of the clinical record and must use encrypted, HIPAA-compliant channels for clinical content.
  • ECT is the fastest-acting treatment for severe depression and acute suicidality; retrograde amnesia is its primary cognitive side effect; right unilateral placement produces fewer cognitive effects than bilateral.
  • TMS (repetitive TMS targeting the left DLPFC) is FDA-cleared for treatment-resistant MDD and OCD; it is non-invasive, requires no anesthesia, and causes no cognitive side effects.
  • Ketamine/esketamine are NMDA antagonists producing rapid antidepressant effects (within hours); esketamine (Spravato) nasal spray is FDA-approved for treatment-resistant depression but carries abuse potential and dissociative side effects.
  • Psilocybin (5-HT2A agonist) has FDA breakthrough therapy designation for depression; MDMA-assisted therapy has been studied in Phase 3 trials for PTSD.
