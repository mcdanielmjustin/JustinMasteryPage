Anxiolytics & Sedatives — Benzodiazepines, Buspirone & Sleep Agents

Fundamental Content
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Overview

A client describes panic attacks that have overtaken her daily life. Her psychiatrist has started medication and wants your input on how it interacts with the CBT you are providing. Your clinical effectiveness depends on working knowledge of these medications — their mechanisms, side effects, and influence on the therapeutic process.

This chapter covers anxiolytics (benzodiazepines and buspirone) and anti-dementia medications. These classes connect to the GABA system fundamentals from Chapter 9 and appear regularly on the EPPP. We begin with benzodiazepines — revolutionary yet risky — then move to buspirone and anti-dementia drugs.

Benzodiazepines

Imagine a patient arriving at an emergency department in the grip of a severe panic attack — heart racing, chest tight, convinced she is dying. Within twenty minutes of receiving a medication, her breathing slows, her muscles relax, and the overwhelming terror begins to recede. The medication responsible for this rapid transformation is almost certainly a benzodiazepine. No other class of anxiolytic works this quickly, and that speed is both its greatest clinical asset and the source of its most serious risks.

Benzodiazepines are the most commonly prescribed anxiolytics, and understanding how they work begins with the brain's primary inhibitory neurotransmitter: GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid). GABA acts as the nervous system's braking mechanism — when it binds to GABA-A receptors on neurons, it opens chloride channels that allow negatively charged chloride ions to flow into the cell, making the neuron less likely to fire. Benzodiazepines do not directly activate these receptors on their own. Instead, they bind to a specific site on the GABA-A receptor complex and enhance GABA's natural effect by increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening. Think of it this way: GABA is the driver pressing the brake pedal, and benzodiazepines make the brakes more responsive. The result is widespread neuronal inhibition — reduced anxiety, muscle relaxation, sedation, and anticonvulsant effects.

This mechanism is a high-yield distinction for the EPPP: benzodiazepines increase the frequency of chloride channel opening (they need GABA present to work), whereas barbiturates increase the duration of channel opening and can activate the receptor even without GABA — which is why barbiturate overdose is far more dangerous. Keeping this frequency-versus-duration distinction firmly in mind will help you navigate exam questions that compare these two drug classes.

Common Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines vary considerably in how quickly they take effect, how long they last, and what clinical situations they best serve. The table below summarizes the agents you are most likely to encounter on the EPPP. Pay special attention to duration of action and notable clinical features, as these details frequently appear in exam items — for example, knowing which benzodiazepine is safest in liver impairment, or which one carries the highest abuse potential.

A few clinical pearls from this table deserve emphasis. Lorazepam's lack of active metabolites makes it the preferred choice when the liver cannot efficiently process drugs — as in elderly patients or those with hepatic disease. Alprazolam's short half-life means patients often experience rebound anxiety between doses, which can paradoxically drive dose escalation and increase dependence risk. And chlordiazepoxide holds a special place in psychopharmacology history: synthesized in 1955 by Leo Sternbach, it was the first benzodiazepine ever developed and launched the modern era of anxiolytic pharmacotherapy.

Clinical Considerations

The clinical profile of benzodiazepines is defined by a central tension: they are remarkably effective in the short term but carry substantial risks with prolonged use. As a psychologist consulting with prescribers or monitoring patients' treatment responses, you need to understand both sides of this equation. Consider a therapist working with a patient who has been taking alprazolam daily for two years. The patient reports that the medication "doesn't work like it used to" and has been taking extra pills on stressful days. This scenario illustrates several of the core clinical concerns outlined below.

  • Rapid onset of action (minutes to hours), unlike antidepressants which take weeks
  • Tolerance and dependence develop with chronic use; benzodiazepines should generally be prescribed for short-term use
  • Withdrawal can be medically dangerous (seizures, delirium); must be tapered gradually, never stopped abruptly
  • Cross-tolerance exists with alcohol and barbiturates (all act on GABA receptors)
  • Benzodiazepines are respiratory depressants; combined with opioids or alcohol, the risk of fatal respiratory depression increases significantly
  • Older adults are at increased risk for falls, cognitive impairment, and paradoxical reactions (agitation, disinhibition)

The concept of cross-tolerance is particularly important for clinical practice and the EPPP. Because benzodiazepines, alcohol, and barbiturates all enhance GABA activity, chronic use of any one of these substances produces tolerance to the others. This is why a patient with a history of heavy alcohol use may require higher-than-expected doses of a benzodiazepine for sedation — and it also explains why combining these substances is so dangerous. Their sedative and respiratory-depressant effects are additive, even synergistic, creating a potentially lethal combination.

Withdrawal from benzodiazepines deserves special emphasis because it is one of the few substance withdrawal syndromes that can be life-threatening. Abrupt discontinuation after chronic use can produce seizures, delirium, and in rare cases, death. For this reason, benzodiazepines must always be tapered gradually under medical supervision. The EPPP frequently tests the knowledge that both benzodiazepine and alcohol withdrawal can produce seizures — a fact that distinguishes these substances from opioids, whose withdrawal is extremely uncomfortable but rarely fatal.

For older adults, benzodiazepines pose unique hazards that psychologists should recognize. The Beers Criteria — a widely used guideline for medication safety in the elderly — lists benzodiazepines as potentially inappropriate medications for older adults due to increased sensitivity to sedation, heightened fall risk, and the possibility of paradoxical reactions in which the patient becomes more agitated or disinhibited rather than calmer. When you encounter an elderly patient who has become unexpectedly confused or combative after starting a new medication, consider whether a benzodiazepine might be the culprit.

Flumazenil (Romazicon)

Flumazenil is the specific benzodiazepine antagonist used to reverse benzodiazepine overdose. It works by competitively blocking the benzodiazepine binding site on the GABA-A receptor, effectively displacing the drug and restoring normal GABA function. While flumazenil can be lifesaving in acute overdose situations, it must be used with caution: in patients who have developed physical dependence on benzodiazepines, administering flumazenil can abruptly remove the drug's effect and precipitate dangerous withdrawal seizures. This is an important clinical detail that the EPPP may test — the reversal agent itself carries its own serious risks.

Buspirone (BuSpar)

What do you prescribe for a patient who needs long-term anxiolytic treatment but has a history of alcohol dependence? Or one who cannot afford benzodiazepine-induced cognitive dulling? This is the clinical niche Buspirone fills — a non-benzodiazepine anxiolytic that works through an entirely different mechanism with none of the dependency risks. Consider a 42-year-old with classic GAD and five years of alcohol use disorder, sober for eight months. Benzodiazepines are ruled out — their abuse potential and alcohol cross-tolerance could jeopardize his recovery. Buspirone is the answer.

  • Mechanism: Partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT1A receptors; does not act on GABA receptors
  • Onset: Takes 2-4 weeks to reach full effect (similar to antidepressants), making it unsuitable for acute anxiety relief
  • Advantages: No sedation, no tolerance or dependence, no withdrawal syndrome, no cross-tolerance with alcohol/benzodiazepines, no abuse potential
  • Disadvantages: Slow onset; not effective for acute anxiety or panic; patients who have previously used benzodiazepines may perceive it as ineffective due to the absence of the immediate sedating effect

The mechanism of buspirone is worth pausing on, because it explains nearly all of its clinical properties. Unlike benzodiazepines, which enhance GABA-A receptor function and produce immediate calming through widespread neural inhibition, buspirone works as a partial agonist at serotonin 5-HT1A receptors. This means it modulates serotonergic transmission in a gradual, modulatory fashion — much the way antidepressants do. The result is that therapeutic effects build over two to four weeks, and there is no immediate "hit" of sedation or euphoria. Because buspirone completely bypasses the GABA system, it produces no sedation, no motor impairment, no potentiation of alcohol effects, and no physiological dependence.

This last point — the absence of an immediate subjective effect — creates an interesting clinical challenge. Patients who have previously taken benzodiazepines often report that buspirone "doesn't work." They are accustomed to the rapid, tangible relief of a benzodiazepine and interpret the absence of that feeling as treatment failure. Clinicians must set expectations carefully at the outset: buspirone's benefits emerge gradually, and the absence of sedation is actually a feature, not a bug. This psychoeducation is part of responsible prescribing and can make the difference between treatment adherence and early dropout.

Consider another clinical scenario: a graduate student with GAD is terrified of becoming dependent on medication after watching a family member struggle with benzodiazepine withdrawal. Buspirone would be an ideal first-line choice — she can take it daily for months without developing tolerance, and if treatment is eventually discontinued, there is no withdrawal syndrome to manage. Contrast this with the careful taper required when discontinuing long-term benzodiazepine therapy, and the clinical appeal of buspirone for certain patient populations becomes immediately clear.

EPPP Tip

Buspirone is a favorite EPPP topic because it differs from benzodiazepines in every key way: slow onset, no sedation, no dependence, no withdrawal, no cross-tolerance with alcohol. If a question asks about an anxiolytic that is safe for patients with a history of substance abuse, buspirone is the answer. A helpful mnemonic: think of buspirone as the "anti-benzodiazepine" — for every characteristic feature of benzodiazepines (fast onset, sedation, dependence, withdrawal, cross-tolerance with alcohol, abuse potential), buspirone does the opposite. If an exam question describes a patient with anxiety and a substance use history, or asks which anxiolytic carries no risk of physiological dependence, buspirone should be your first thought.

Disulfiram (Antabuse) for Alcohol Use Disorder

Although not an anxiolytic, disulfiram (brand name Antabuse) is an important pharmacological agent used in the treatment of alcohol use disorder and is frequently tested on the EPPP. Disulfiram works through a form of aversion therapy at the biochemical level: it inhibits the enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase, which is responsible for metabolizing acetaldehyde, a toxic intermediate byproduct of alcohol metabolism. When a person taking disulfiram consumes even a small amount of alcohol, acetaldehyde rapidly accumulates in the body, producing a highly unpleasant constellation of symptoms known as the disulfiram-ethanol reaction.

This reaction typically includes severe nausea, vomiting, facial flushing, headache, dizziness, confusion, hypotension, and tachycardia. In severe cases, the reaction can cause respiratory depression, cardiovascular collapse, and even death—particularly with large amounts of alcohol. The aversive nature of these symptoms serves as a deterrent against drinking.

Disulfiram-Ethanol Reaction

A toxic physiological response triggered when alcohol is ingested while disulfiram is active in the body. Caused by accumulation of acetaldehyde due to inhibition of aldehyde dehydrogenase. Symptoms range from nausea and flushing to potentially life-threatening cardiovascular and respiratory complications.

Clinically, disulfiram is most effective when the patient is highly motivated and when medication compliance is supervised (e.g., by a spouse or treatment program). Patients must be fully informed about the risks and must avoid not only alcoholic beverages but also hidden sources of alcohol such as certain mouthwashes, sauces, and topical preparations. Two other medications approved for alcohol use disorder—naltrexone (an opioid antagonist that reduces craving and the rewarding effects of alcohol) and acamprosate (which modulates glutamate activity to reduce post-withdrawal distress)—operate through different mechanisms and do not produce aversive reactions.

Beta-Blockers for Performance Anxiety

Beta-adrenergic blockers such as propranolol (Inderal) and atenolol (Tenormin) are sometimes used off-label to manage the somatic manifestations of anxiety, particularly in situations involving performance or public speaking. These medications work by blocking beta-adrenergic receptors in the peripheral nervous system, thereby reducing sympathetically mediated physical symptoms such as tachycardia, tremor, sweating, and muscle tension. By dampening these autonomic arousal responses, beta-blockers can help individuals perform more effectively in high-pressure situations.

A critical distinction for the EPPP is that beta-blockers address only the physical symptoms of anxiety. They do not directly alleviate the subjective emotional experience of fear or worry, nor do they target the cognitive components of anxiety such as catastrophic thinking or excessive apprehension. This makes them most appropriate for performance anxiety (e.g., stage fright, public speaking anxiety) where somatic symptoms are the primary impediment to functioning, rather than for generalized anxiety disorder or panic disorder where cognitive and emotional symptoms predominate.

Propranolol is lipophilic and crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily than atenolol, which is more cardioselective. Common side effects include hypotension, bradycardia, fatigue, and bronchospasm (contraindicated in asthma). Beta-blockers are not associated with dependence or withdrawal syndromes comparable to benzodiazepines, making them a relatively low-risk option for situational use.

Clinical Note

A musician experiencing hand tremor and rapid heartbeat before recitals may benefit from a single dose of propranolol taken 30–60 minutes before performing. However, a patient with pervasive worry, rumination, and emotional distress associated with GAD would not find adequate relief from a beta-blocker alone, because these drugs do not target the psychological dimensions of anxiety.

Anti-Dementia Medications

Imagine a 72-year-old retired professor who begins misplacing her keys, forgetting her grandchildren's names, and struggling to follow conversations she once led with ease. Her family, desperate for answers, asks whether medication can "fix" what's happening to her brain. This is one of the most heartbreaking conversations in clinical practice — because the honest answer is complicated. While we do have medications that can help, they work by managing symptoms and temporarily slowing cognitive decline, not by halting or reversing the disease itself. Understanding what these drugs can and cannot do is essential for any clinician working with aging populations, and it is a topic the EPPP expects you to know well.

Having just explored anxiolytics and sedatives — medications that modulate systems like GABA and serotonin to manage anxiety — we now shift to a very different clinical challenge: the progressive loss of cognitive function in Alzheimer's disease. The neurotransmitter systems involved here are different (primarily acetylcholine and glutamate), but the pharmacological logic is similar: identify what's going wrong at the neurochemical level and intervene to restore some degree of balance. Currently available medications for Alzheimer's disease provide symptomatic relief but do not halt or reverse the underlying neurodegenerative process. This distinction — palliative versus curative — is critical for setting realistic expectations with patients and families alike.

Cholinesterase Inhibitors

One of the earliest and most consistent findings in Alzheimer's disease research is the loss of cholinergic neurons — the brain cells that produce and release acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter critical for memory formation, attention, and learning. As these neurons degenerate, acetylcholine levels plummet, and cognitive function deteriorates. The pharmacological strategy behind cholinesterase inhibitors is elegant in its simplicity: if you can't produce enough acetylcholine, at least prevent the breakdown of what little remains. These drugs inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, which is responsible for breaking down acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, thereby increasing acetylcholine availability in the brain. Think of it like plugging a leaky bucket — you're not adding more water, but you're keeping more of what you have. Cholinesterase inhibitors are the first-line treatment for mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease.

For the EPPP, donepezil (Aricept) is the name you are most likely to encounter. It is the most widely prescribed cholinesterase inhibitor, largely because of its convenient once-daily dosing and its approval across all stages of Alzheimer's disease — mild, moderate, and severe. Rivastigmine is noteworthy for its transdermal patch formulation, which is particularly useful for patients who have difficulty swallowing pills or who experience significant gastrointestinal side effects. Galantamine adds a dual mechanism by also modulating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, potentially enhancing cholinergic transmission through a second pathway.

Common side effects: Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, decreased appetite, dizziness, bradycardia (all related to increased cholinergic activity). Because these drugs boost acetylcholine throughout the body — not just in the brain — the side effect profile reflects widespread cholinergic stimulation. Gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequent reason patients discontinue treatment, which is why the transdermal patch formulation of rivastigmine can be a valuable clinical alternative.

Memantine (Namenda)

While cholinesterase inhibitors address the acetylcholine deficit in Alzheimer's disease, memantine targets a completely different neurotransmitter system: glutamate. In a healthy brain, glutamate is the primary excitatory neurotransmitter, essential for learning and memory. But in Alzheimer's disease, damaged neurons leak excessive glutamate, which overstimulates NMDA receptors on neighboring cells. This chronic overstimulation — called excitotoxicity — essentially floods neurons with calcium, leading to further cell damage and death. It's like leaving a faucet running until the sink overflows and damages the floor. Memantine works by partially blocking NMDA receptors, reducing this toxic flood without completely shutting down normal glutamate signaling.

  • Mechanism: NMDA receptor antagonist; blocks excessive glutamate activity (thought to protect against excitotoxicity)
  • Uses: Moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease; often used in combination with a cholinesterase inhibitor
  • Side effects: Generally well-tolerated; dizziness, headache, constipation, confusion

A key clinical point — and one the EPPP frequently tests — is that memantine occupies a different treatment niche than the cholinesterase inhibitors. While donepezil and its cousins are first-line for mild to moderate stages, memantine is reserved for moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease. In practice, clinicians often combine memantine with a cholinesterase inhibitor (most commonly donepezil) to target both the cholinergic and glutamatergic systems simultaneously, a strategy supported by evidence showing modest additional benefit over monotherapy.

EPPP Tip

Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) work by increasing acetylcholine and are used for mild to moderate Alzheimer's. Memantine is an NMDA antagonist used for moderate to severe Alzheimer's. These drugs slow symptom progression but do NOT cure or reverse the disease.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

Sodium Oxybate (Xyrem) for Narcolepsy

Sodium oxybate (brand name Xyrem) is the sodium salt of gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB) and represents a uniquely versatile treatment for narcolepsy. It is the medication considered most effective for simultaneously addressing multiple core symptoms of the disorder: excessive daytime sleepiness, disrupted nighttime sleep, and cataplexy (sudden, emotion-triggered episodes of muscle weakness or paralysis).

Sodium oxybate acts primarily as a GABA-B receptor agonist and also binds to specific GHB receptors. When taken at bedtime (typically in two divided doses—one at bedtime and one 2.5 to 4 hours later), it consolidates slow-wave sleep and overall nocturnal sleep architecture. This consolidation of nighttime sleep is believed to underlie the reduction in daytime sleepiness. The anticataplectic effect is thought to involve suppression of abnormal REM sleep intrusions, since cataplexy is considered a dissociated manifestation of REM atonia occurring during wakefulness.

Because GHB has significant abuse potential and a history of illicit use, sodium oxybate is classified as a Schedule III controlled substance and is available only through a restricted distribution program (REMS). Side effects include nausea, dizziness, enuresis, and—at excessive doses—respiratory depression. Psychologists should be aware that patients taking sodium oxybate may report initial confusion or sleepwalking, and the medication's association with GHB may raise concerns that warrant psychoeducation.

THC and the Mesolimbic Reward Pathway

Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the principal psychoactive constituent of marijuana, generates its reinforcing and euphoric effects through activation of CB1 cannabinoid receptors in the brain's mesolimbic dopamine system. Specifically, THC stimulates CB1 receptors on inhibitory interneurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), reducing GABAergic inhibition of dopaminergic neurons. This disinhibition leads to increased dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens, the brain region most closely associated with reward, motivation, and reinforcement learning.

This dopaminergic mechanism is shared, in principle, with virtually all substances that carry abuse potential—including opioids, stimulants, nicotine, and alcohol—though each drug achieves dopamine elevation through different upstream pathways. The magnitude of THC-induced dopamine release is generally more modest than that produced by cocaine or amphetamines, which partly explains why cannabis dependence typically develops more gradually and presents with milder withdrawal symptoms. Nevertheless, chronic THC exposure can downregulate CB1 receptors and alter dopaminergic signaling, contributing to tolerance, withdrawal irritability, and motivational deficits observed in heavy, long-term users.

Example

A patient who has used marijuana daily for several years reports feeling unmotivated, anhedonic, and irritable when unable to use. These symptoms reflect neuroadaptive changes in the reward circuitry: chronic CB1 activation has diminished baseline dopamine signaling in the nucleus accumbens, meaning the patient experiences reduced pleasure from naturally rewarding activities and discomfort during abstinence.

LSD and Serotonergic Hallucinogens

Lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD) is a potent hallucinogenic substance that produces profound alterations in perception, cognition, and mood at microgram-level doses. Its primary pharmacological mechanism involves agonism at serotonin (5-HT) receptors, particularly the 5-HT2A receptor subtype. Activation of 5-HT2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex and other cortical regions is considered the principal driver of LSD's characteristic effects, which include vivid visual hallucinations, synesthesia (blending of sensory modalities), altered sense of time, and intensified emotional experiences.

LSD belongs to a broader class of serotonergic hallucinogens (also called classical psychedelics) that includes psilocybin and mescaline, all of which share 5-HT2A agonist activity as their core mechanism. Unlike substances that act primarily on dopamine or GABA systems, these compounds do not typically produce physical dependence or withdrawal syndromes. However, rapid tolerance (known as tachyphylaxis) develops within days of repeated use, and cross-tolerance occurs among serotonergic hallucinogens. Adverse psychological effects can include panic reactions ("bad trips"), persistent perceptual disturbances (hallucinogen persisting perception disorder, or HPPD), and, in vulnerable individuals, precipitation of psychotic episodes.

Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD)

A condition in which individuals experience recurrent perceptual disturbances—such as visual trailing, halos, or geometric patterns—long after the hallucinogen has been eliminated from the body. HPPD can be distressing and persistent, and it is recognized as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-5.

Dronabinol (Marinol)

Dronabinol is a synthetic analogue of tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary psychoactive compound found in cannabis. It received FDA approval for the management of nausea and vomiting caused by cancer chemotherapy, particularly in patients who have not responded adequately to conventional antiemetic treatments. Dronabinol has also been approved for appetite stimulation in patients with AIDS-related wasting syndrome.

Dronabinol acts as an agonist at cannabinoid CB1 and CB2 receptors. CB1 receptors are densely concentrated in brain regions involved in nausea regulation, appetite, pain modulation, and mood. By activating these receptors, dronabinol suppresses the emetic reflex and stimulates appetite. Because it is a Schedule III controlled substance (reflecting moderate abuse potential), prescribers must weigh its therapeutic benefits against risks including euphoria, dizziness, sedation, paranoid ideation, and cognitive impairment. Patients with a history of psychiatric disorders may be particularly vulnerable to dysphoric or psychotomimetic reactions.

Clinical Note

Psychologists working in oncology or palliative care settings should be aware that patients taking dronabinol may present with mood changes, perceptual disturbances, or cognitive slowing that can mimic psychiatric conditions. A thorough medication review is essential before attributing such symptoms to a primary psychological disorder.

  • Benzodiazepines enhance GABA activity; rapid onset but carry risks of dependence, tolerance, and dangerous withdrawal (seizures). Never discontinue abruptly.
  • Flumazenil is the benzodiazepine antagonist used in overdose.
  • Buspirone is a 5-HT1A partial agonist; no sedation, no dependence, no cross-tolerance with alcohol; takes 2-4 weeks to work.
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) increase acetylcholine and are used for mild to moderate Alzheimer's.
  • Memantine is an NMDA antagonist used for moderate to severe Alzheimer's.
  • Anti-dementia drugs slow symptom progression but do not cure or reverse the disease.
