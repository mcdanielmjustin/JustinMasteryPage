Introduction to Antipsychotic Medications

A 22-year-old college student is brought to the psychiatric emergency department by campus police after barricading himself in his dorm room, convinced that his roommates are recording his thoughts and transmitting them to a government surveillance network. He has not slept in four days, his speech jumps between loosely connected ideas, and he responds to voices that no one else can hear. The attending psychiatrist administers haloperidol — a first-generation antipsychotic — and within hours, the agitation subsides and the paranoid delusions begin to loosen their grip. But over the next several days, the treatment team notices something troubling: his muscles have become rigid, his gait has slowed to a shuffle, and his face has lost its expressiveness. The medication that rescued him from psychosis has introduced a new problem — one rooted in the very mechanism that made it effective.

Antipsychotic medications (also called neuroleptics) are the primary pharmacological treatment for schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders. They are also used for bipolar disorder, severe agitation, Tourette's disorder, and as adjuncts for treatment-resistant depression. Antipsychotics are divided into two main classes: first-generation (typical) and second-generation (atypical). For the EPPP, understanding the differences between these two classes — particularly their mechanisms of action, side effect profiles, and the major adverse effects (EPS, tardive dyskinesia, NMS, and metabolic syndrome) — is essential. This chapter covers the conventional antipsychotics; the next chapter addresses the atypical agents.

Although this chapter focuses on antipsychotic agents, psychologists should be aware that other medication classes are frequently prescribed alongside or as alternatives to antipsychotics in complex clinical presentations. Among these, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine (Effexor) and duloxetine (Cymbalta) play a significant role in contemporary psychopharmacology. SNRIs inhibit the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, providing a dual-action mechanism that distinguishes them from SSRIs. This dual mechanism makes them effective across a broad range of conditions, including major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, and neuropathic pain syndromes such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy and fibromyalgia. Duloxetine has received specific FDA approval for several chronic pain conditions, while venlafaxine is particularly noted for its dose-dependent pharmacology — at lower doses it functions primarily as a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, with norepinephrine reuptake inhibition becoming more prominent at higher doses. Common side effects of SNRIs include nausea, dizziness, insomnia, and elevated blood pressure (particularly with venlafaxine at higher doses). Abrupt discontinuation of either medication can produce a pronounced discontinuation syndrome characterized by dizziness, nausea, irritability, and sensory disturbances often described as "brain zaps."

Example

A patient with comorbid major depression and diabetic neuropathy may benefit from duloxetine because it addresses both the mood disorder and the chronic pain condition through a single medication, reducing polypharmacy burden.

The Dopamine Hypothesis of Schizophrenia

A young man arrives at an emergency department hearing voices, convinced that government agents monitor his thoughts through an implanted chip. Yet his family reports months of withdrawal — days in bed, flat emotion, abandoned coursework. These two symptom clusters pose schizophrenia's central puzzle: how can one disorder produce both florid psychosis and motivational collapse? The answer lies in the dopamine hypothesis, which proposes dysregulated dopamine running in opposite directions across different brain circuits. Building on the four dopamine pathways covered in the previous chapter:

  • Positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thought) are associated with excess dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway — the reward and salience circuit that projects from the ventral tegmental area to limbic structures. When this pathway is hyperactive, the brain assigns overwhelming significance to irrelevant stimuli, generating the delusions and perceptual distortions that characterize psychosis.
  • Negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, alogia, anhedonia) are associated with reduced dopamine activity in the mesocortical pathway (prefrontal cortex) — the circuit responsible for executive function, motivation, and emotional expression. When dopamine is deficient here, the result is the cognitive dulling and motivational collapse that families often describe as "losing" the person they knew.

This framework explains a clinical paradox: a first-generation antipsychotic broadly blocks D2 receptors, fading the delusions (correcting mesolimbic excess) but deepening flat affect and withdrawal (worsening mesocortical deficit). The same drug that helps one pathway hurts another. This trade-off is why atypical antipsychotics were developed — and why the dopamine hypothesis, in its modern form, is about complex imbalance across circuits, not simply "too much dopamine."

Four Dopamine Pathways

For the EPPP, you need to know these four pathways and what happens when dopamine is blocked in each. Think of them as four distinct "channels" of dopamine activity, each with its own clinical story: Mesolimbic: Excess dopamine leads to positive symptoms — hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking. This is the pathway antipsychotics are designed to target. Mesocortical: Reduced dopamine leads to negative and cognitive symptoms — flat affect, poor motivation, and impaired working memory. Blocking dopamine here makes these symptoms worse, not better. Nigrostriatal: Dopamine blockade here causes EPS (extrapyramidal symptoms) and tardive dyskinesia, because this pathway normally coordinates smooth, voluntary movement — the same pathway affected in Parkinson's disease. Tuberoinfundibular: Dopamine blockade here increases prolactin, causing hyperprolactinemia — which can lead to breast enlargement, galactorrhea, menstrual irregularities, and sexual dysfunction. A helpful mnemonic: Meso-Limbic = Lively psychosis; Meso-Cortical = Cognitive/negative; Nigro-Striatal = Stiffness (movement); Tubero-Infundibular = Titration of prolactin.

As we move into our discussion of first-generation antipsychotics, keep this four-pathway map firmly in mind. It will serve as your clinical compass for understanding why these medications produce the specific side-effect profiles they do — and why the search for better antipsychotics eventually led to the development of the atypical agents.

First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics

In the 1950s, chlorpromazine produced revolutionary results — psychotic patients confined to state hospitals for years became calm and coherent within days. But celebration was tempered by an observation: many patients developed muscle stiffness, tremors, and Parkinson-like movements. The era of antipsychotic pharmacotherapy had begun, along with its enduring challenge: balancing therapeutic benefits against motor side effects.

First-generation antipsychotics (FGAs), also called "typical" or "conventional" antipsychotics, work primarily by blocking dopamine D2 receptors throughout the brain. As you'll recall from the dopamine hypothesis, blocking D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway is what reduces positive symptoms like hallucinations and delusions. However, because these drugs are not selective — they block D2 receptors across all four dopamine pathways — they inevitably produce side effects in the nigrostriatal pathway (causing movement disorders) and the tuberoinfundibular pathway (causing elevated prolactin levels). Understanding this broad-spectrum D2 blockade is the key to understanding virtually everything about FGA pharmacology. These medications are classified by potency, a distinction that has direct, clinically meaningful implications for which side effects a patient is most likely to experience.

High-Potency Typical Antipsychotics

The concept of "potency" in pharmacology refers to how much of a drug is needed to achieve a therapeutic effect — not how effective the drug is. A high-potency antipsychotic requires lower doses to achieve D2 blockade, but this tight binding to dopamine receptors comes at a cost: a significantly greater risk of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), those Parkinson-like movement disturbances that plagued early antipsychotic treatment. On the positive side, high-potency agents tend to produce less sedation, fewer anticholinergic effects (like dry mouth, constipation, and blurred vision), and less orthostatic hypotension — the sudden drop in blood pressure that can cause dizziness or fainting when a patient stands up. Think of it this way: high-potency agents are "cleaner" in terms of blocking primarily dopamine receptors, but that precision means the motor system takes the brunt of the side effects.

Consider a clinical scenario: a young man is brought to the emergency department in an acutely agitated psychotic state. The psychiatrist orders intramuscular haloperidol because it works quickly and effectively to manage the crisis. Within hours, the agitation subsides — but over the next few days, the treatment team notices the patient developing muscle rigidity and a shuffling gait. This is the classic high-potency trade-off in action: excellent efficacy against positive symptoms, but the nigrostriatal pathway is paying the price.

A few details from this table are worth highlighting for exam preparation. Haloperidol (Haldol) remains one of the most widely recognized antipsychotics in clinical practice and is a frequent topic on licensing exams. Both haloperidol and fluphenazine are available in long-acting injectable (decanoate) formulations, which are invaluable for patients with chronic psychotic disorders who struggle with medication adherence — a single injection can provide weeks of therapeutic coverage. Pimozide (Orap) stands out because it is specifically FDA-approved for Tourette's disorder, making it a unique entry in this class; however, its risk of QT prolongation (a cardiac conduction abnormality that can lead to dangerous arrhythmias) limits its use and requires careful cardiac monitoring.

Low-Potency Typical Antipsychotics

If high-potency agents are relatively selective D2 blockers, low-potency typical antipsychotics are the pharmacological equivalent of a wide-angle lens: they block dopamine receptors but also exert substantial effects on histamine, acetylcholine, and alpha-adrenergic receptors. This broader receptor profile means that higher doses are needed to achieve adequate D2 blockade, but the upside is a lower risk of EPS. The downside? Patients are much more likely to experience significant sedation (from histamine blockade), anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, and blurred vision (from muscarinic receptor blockade), and orthostatic hypotension (from alpha-1 adrenergic blockade). An easy mnemonic: low potency means "low EPS but lots of everything else."

Picture an older adult with schizophrenia who is prescribed chlorpromazine. She feels drowsy throughout the day, notices her mouth is constantly dry, and nearly falls when she stands up too quickly from her chair — a dangerous combination in an elderly patient. Her treatment team must weigh whether the reduction in psychotic symptoms justifies these burdensome and potentially hazardous side effects, particularly the fall risk associated with orthostatic hypotension.

Chlorpromazine (Thorazine) deserves special attention as a landmark in psychiatric history. Discovered in the 1950s, it was the drug that launched the psychopharmacological revolution and fundamentally transformed the treatment of severe mental illness. Its 100 mg dose serves as the standard reference point — the "chlorpromazine equivalent" — against which all other antipsychotic potencies are compared. However, chronic use can produce photosensitivity (increased vulnerability to sunburn) and corneal deposits, reminders that even groundbreaking medications carry long-term costs. Thioridazine (Mellaril), meanwhile, carries the highest risk of retinitis pigmentosa — a serious and potentially irreversible eye condition — among all antipsychotics, along with dangerous QT prolongation. These cardiac and ophthalmological risks have severely restricted its clinical use, making it essentially a last-resort medication today.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

Imagine a young man brought to the emergency department just hours after receiving his first injection of haloperidol. His neck is twisted painfully to one side, his eyes are locked in an upward gaze, and he is terrified. Or picture a middle-aged woman who has taken chlorpromazine faithfully for years and now cannot stop her tongue from darting in and out of her mouth — a movement she barely notices but that profoundly affects how others interact with her. These are not rare curiosities. They are among the most clinically significant and distressing adverse effects in all of psychopharmacology, and understanding them is essential for any practitioner who prescribes, monitors, or provides therapy to patients taking antipsychotic medications.

In the preceding section, we noted that high-potency typical antipsychotics such as haloperidol bind tightly to dopamine D2 receptors and carry a greater risk of movement-related side effects, while low-potency agents like chlorpromazine trade that risk for more sedation and anticholinergic burden. Now we turn to those movement-related side effects in detail — a family of disorders collectively known as extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS). These are movement disorders caused by dopamine D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway, the brain circuit that fine-tunes voluntary movement. When antipsychotic drugs block too many D2 receptors in this pathway — particularly beyond the roughly 80% occupancy threshold — the delicate balance between dopamine and acetylcholine in the basal ganglia is disrupted, and motor control goes awry. They are most common with high-potency typical antipsychotics, though any agent with significant D2 antagonism can be the culprit.

What makes EPS especially important for clinical practice is that these symptoms unfold along a predictable timeline, from reactions that emerge within hours of the first dose to disorders that develop only after months or years of chronic treatment. Recognizing where a patient falls on that timeline is the key to accurate diagnosis and appropriate intervention. Let's walk through each of these in a bit more detail so the clinical picture comes alive. Acute dystonia is often the first EPS a patient encounters — and it can be genuinely frightening. A classic board-style vignette involves a young male patient (young men are at highest risk) who develops torticollis or oculogyric crisis within hours of starting a high-potency antipsychotic. The treatment is swift and gratifying: an intramuscular injection of benztropine or diphenhydramine typically resolves the crisis within minutes, restoring the dopamine-acetylcholine balance in the basal ganglia by blocking the acetylcholine side of the equation.

Akathisia, appearing within days to weeks, is more insidious. Patients describe an unbearable inner restlessness — a need to move, pace, or shift their weight — that can be so distressing it contributes to medication nonadherence or even suicidality. Crucially, akathisia is often mistaken for psychotic agitation, which can lead a clinician to increase the antipsychotic dose and inadvertently make the problem worse. This diagnostic trap is a favorite testing point on the EPPP. Unlike acute dystonia and parkinsonism, akathisia responds best not to anticholinergics but to beta-blockers like propranolol, benzodiazepines, or dose reduction of the offending agent.

Drug-induced parkinsonism (also called pseudoparkinsonism) develops over weeks to months and closely mimics idiopathic Parkinson's disease — bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, resting "pill-rolling" tremor, shuffling gait, and mask-like facies. The underlying mechanism is the same dopamine depletion in the nigrostriatal pathway. Anticholinergics such as benztropine or the dopamine-releasing agent amantadine are first-line treatments, along with dose reduction when clinically feasible.

Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is the most feared of all extrapyramidal symptoms because it typically appears only after months to years of antipsychotic use and may be irreversible even after the offending drug is stopped. The hallmark presentation involves involuntary, repetitive orofacial movements — lip smacking, tongue protrusion, lateral jaw movements, and grimacing — though the trunk and extremities can also be affected. The pathophysiology is thought to involve dopamine receptor supersensitivity that develops after prolonged blockade. In a paradox that the EPPP frequently tests, abruptly discontinuing the antipsychotic can temporarily unmask or worsen TD, because the supersensitive receptors are suddenly exposed to unblocked dopamine. Two VMAT2 (vesicular monoamine transporter 2) inhibitors — valbenazine (Ingrezza) and deutetrabenazine (Austedo) — are now FDA-approved specifically for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia, representing the first targeted pharmacotherapy for this condition.

EPPP Tip

Know the timeline: acute dystonia appears within hours to days, akathisia within days to weeks, parkinsonism within weeks to months, and tardive dyskinesia after months to years of use. TD is the most feared because it may be irreversible and paradoxically may worsen when the offending drug is discontinued. A helpful mnemonic for the order is "Days, Weeks, Months, Years" — the four EPS types march along this timeline in the same sequence they appear in the table. Also remember that akathisia is the one most commonly confused with worsening psychosis, and anticholinergics are not its first-line treatment — beta-blockers are.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • The dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia posits excess dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway (positive symptoms) and reduced dopamine in the mesocortical pathway (negative/cognitive symptoms).
  • Know the four dopamine pathways: mesolimbic (psychosis), mesocortical (negative symptoms), nigrostriatal (movement/EPS), tuberoinfundibular (prolactin).
  • High-potency typicals (haloperidol, fluphenazine): low doses needed, high EPS risk, less sedation and anticholinergic effects.
  • Low-potency typicals (chlorpromazine, thioridazine): higher doses needed, lower EPS risk, more sedation, anticholinergic effects, and orthostatic hypotension.
  • Chlorpromazine (100 mg) is the benchmark unit for antipsychotic potency comparisons.
  • EPS timeline: acute dystonia (hours-days), akathisia (days-weeks), parkinsonism (weeks-months), tardive dyskinesia (months-years).
  • Akathisia is commonly mistaken for worsening psychosis; treated with beta-blockers (propranolol), not anticholinergics.
  • Tardive dyskinesia may be irreversible; VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) are FDA-approved treatments.
  • Haloperidol and fluphenazine are available as long-acting decanoate injections for patients with adherence difficulties.
  • Pimozide (Orap) is specifically FDA-approved for Tourette's disorder but carries QT prolongation risk.
