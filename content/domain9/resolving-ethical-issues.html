Overview: Resolving Ethical Issues

Imagine this scenario: A psychologist working in a correctional facility is ordered by the warden to share confidential therapy notes for an upcoming parole hearing — without the client's consent. The psychologist knows that breaching confidentiality without proper authorization violates the APA Ethics Code, yet the institutional authority is pressing hard, and state regulations seem ambiguous on the matter. What should the psychologist do? Where does one even begin to navigate such a conflict? The answer lies in the very first standard of the APA Ethics Code, which was designed precisely for moments like these — moments when ethical principles collide with real-world pressures, legal mandates, or organizational demands.

Standard 1 of the APA Ethics Code addresses what psychologists should do when they encounter ethical problems, conflicts between ethics and law or organizational demands, or knowledge of others' ethical violations. Rather than offering a single rigid rule, this standard provides a structured framework — a decision-making roadmap — for working through the kinds of ethical dilemmas that inevitably arise across every domain of professional practice, from clinical work and research to consultation and forensic evaluation. It is foundational in the truest sense: without a clear process for resolving ethical tensions, every other standard in the Code would lack the mechanism for real-world application.

Think of Standard 1 as the "meta-standard" — it doesn't tell you what specific ethical behavior looks like in therapy, assessment, or research (those details come in later standards), but it tells you what to do when you're stuck, conflicted, or confronted with someone else's misconduct. It answers the question that every early-career psychologist eventually faces: "I know something isn't right here — now what?" The steps outlined in this standard include clarifying the conflict, attempting informal resolution when appropriate, consulting with colleagues or ethics committees, and — when necessary — taking more formal action such as filing an ethics complaint.

Consider another common scenario: A graduate student observes her clinical supervisor making dismissive, culturally insensitive remarks to a client during a therapy session. The student feels deeply uncomfortable but is uncertain whether this rises to the level of an ethical violation — and she worries about the power differential involved in confronting her supervisor. Standard 1 speaks directly to situations like these, offering guidance on when and how to address perceived violations by others while also recognizing the complexities of authority, loyalty, and professional risk. It acknowledges that resolving ethical issues is rarely a clean, mechanical process; it requires judgment, courage, and a willingness to engage with ambiguity.

Standard 1 is frequently tested on the EPPP because ethical conflicts are inherent in professional psychology, and knowing the proper steps for resolving them is essential. Exam questions often present nuanced vignettes in which law, organizational policy, and ethical obligations pull in different directions — and the correct answer hinges on understanding the resolution process that Standard 1 lays out. Mastering this standard doesn't just prepare you for the exam; it prepares you for the daily realities of ethical practice, where the "right thing to do" is sometimes unclear and the path forward must be carefully reasoned rather than simply memorized.

Standard 1: Resolving Ethical Issues

Imagine you're a psychologist working in a busy hospital system. One morning, you discover that an administrator has been selectively quoting your psychological evaluation to justify denying a patient's disability claim — twisting your clinical conclusions beyond recognition. That same week, a colleague confides that another psychologist on staff has been engaging in a dual relationship with a current client. And to complicate matters further, your supervisor asks you to release raw test data in a way that conflicts with APA ethical guidelines but aligns with a new state regulation. What do you do first? Who do you tell? What steps must you take, and in what order?

These are exactly the kinds of dilemmas that Standard 1 of the APA Ethics Code was designed to address. Standard 1 provides psychologists with a structured framework for navigating ethical problems, resolving conflicts between ethics and law or organizational demands, and responding appropriately when they learn of ethical violations committed by colleagues. It is, in many ways, the "meta-standard" — the standard about what to do when standards collide with reality. Understanding its provisions deeply is essential not only for the EPPP but for every day of professional practice.

1.01 Misuse of Psychologists' Work

Psychologists invest tremendous care in producing accurate assessments, research findings, and clinical reports. But once that work leaves their hands, others may distort it — sometimes carelessly, sometimes deliberately. A forensic evaluation might be quoted out of context in a custody hearing. A research finding might be cherry-picked by a media outlet to support a sensational headline. When psychologists learn that their work is being misused or misrepresented by others, Standard 1.01 requires them to take reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse or misrepresentation. "Reasonable steps" is key language here: the standard doesn't demand that psychologists achieve a perfect correction, but it does require active effort — contacting the misrepresenting party, issuing a clarifying statement, or reaching out to the audience that received the distorted information.

Consider a school psychologist who discovers that a principal has been using her psychoeducational report to justify placing a student in a more restrictive environment than the report actually recommended. Under Standard 1.01, the psychologist cannot simply shrug and move on. She is ethically obligated to take constructive action — perhaps requesting a meeting with the IEP team to clarify her findings, or providing a written addendum that accurately reflects her conclusions. The obligation is to act, not merely to feel troubled.

1.02 Conflicts Between Ethics and Law, Regulations, or Other Governing Legal Authority

One of the most challenging situations a psychologist can face is discovering that what the law demands and what the Ethics Code requires are pulling in opposite directions. Standard 1.02 provides a clear, multi-step process for these moments. When psychologists' ethical responsibilities conflict with law, regulations, or other governing legal authority, they must first clarify the nature of the conflict — articulating precisely which ethical standard is in tension with which legal requirement. Next, they make known their commitment to the Ethics Code to the relevant parties. Finally, they take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict in a manner consistent with the General Principles and Ethical Standards of the APA code. Critically, under no circumstances does this standard permit a psychologist to violate fundamental human rights, regardless of what a law or regulation may appear to demand.

This standard recognizes a difficult truth: psychologists do not operate in a vacuum. They practice within legal systems, governmental regulations, and institutional hierarchies that sometimes impose requirements at odds with ethical practice. The standard does not naively insist that ethics always trump law — but it does insist that psychologists engage the conflict transparently, advocate for ethical practice, and never become passive instruments of policies that violate human rights.

Example

A psychologist working in a correctional facility is ordered by administration to provide confidential therapy records to correctional officers. This conflicts with Standard 4 (Confidentiality). Under 1.02, the psychologist should clarify the conflict, advocate for confidentiality, and try to resolve the situation -- but cannot simply ignore the legal authority without taking constructive action.

1.03 Conflicts Between Ethics and Organizational Demands

Closely related to Standard 1.02, but distinct in important ways, Standard 1.03 addresses what happens when the conflict is not with the law but with an organization. Perhaps a managed care company demands that a psychologist limit treatment to four sessions regardless of clinical need, or a university department pressures a psychologist to alter research findings to secure continued funding. When the demands of an organization with which psychologists are affiliated conflict with the Ethics Code, psychologists must clarify the nature of the conflict, make known their commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve the conflict — the same three-step process as in 1.02.

However, Standard 1.03 includes an important escape valve: if the conflict is truly unresolvable despite good-faith efforts, the psychologist may adhere to the requirements of law, regulations, or other governing legal authority. Notice the careful language here — the psychologist may defer to legal authority, not simply to organizational preference. An organization's internal policy does not carry the same weight as law. This distinction matters on the EPPP: if an organization demands something unethical and there is no legal mandate backing the demand, the psychologist cannot simply comply and claim the conflict was unresolvable. Genuine, documented efforts at resolution are expected before any deference occurs.

1.04 Informal Resolution of Ethical Violations

Learning that a colleague may be violating the Ethics Code puts a psychologist in an uncomfortable but important position. The instinct might be to report the behavior immediately — or conversely, to look the other way. Standard 1.04 charts a middle course that respects collegiality while protecting the public. When psychologists believe there has been an ethical violation by another psychologist, they attempt to resolve the issue by bringing it to the attention of that individual, if an informal resolution appears appropriate and the intervention does not violate any confidentiality rights.

Think of it as the professional equivalent of a conversation before a confrontation. If you notice that a colleague has been inadvertently revealing client names in the break room, the ethical path typically begins with a private, respectful conversation with that colleague — not with an immediate complaint to the licensing board. This approach preserves professional relationships, allows for the possibility that the violation was unintentional, and often produces faster correction than formal proceedings. However, informal resolution has clear boundaries: it is not appropriate when the violation is serious enough to cause substantial harm (such as a sexual relationship with a client), and it must never be pursued if doing so would breach someone's confidentiality.

EPPP Tip

On the EPPP, when asked "what should a psychologist do first?" upon learning a colleague may be acting unethically, the answer is usually to attempt informal resolution (1.04) -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution (e.g., sexual contact with a client).

1.05 Reporting Ethical Violations

When informal resolution is not appropriate — either because the violation is too serious or because an informal attempt has already failed — Standard 1.05 provides the next step. If an apparent ethical violation has substantially harmed or is likely to substantially harm a person or organization and is not appropriate for informal resolution, psychologists take further action such as referral to state or national ethics committees or licensing boards. This is the formal reporting pathway, reserved for situations where the stakes are high and collegial conversation is insufficient or inappropriate.

However, Standard 1.05 includes two important exceptions. First, it does not apply when reporting the violation would itself violate someone's confidentiality rights — for instance, if you learned about a colleague's misconduct through a therapy session with the colleague's client. Second, it does not apply when psychologists have been retained specifically to review the work of another psychologist whose professional conduct is already in question — a carve-out designed to protect the integrity of expert review processes. Understanding these exceptions is critical for EPPP preparation, as test items often present scenarios designed to test whether you know when reporting is required versus when it would be ethically inappropriate.

1.06 Cooperating with Ethics Committees

Once an ethics investigation is underway, the process depends on the cooperation of the professionals involved. Standard 1.06 establishes that psychologists cooperate in ethics investigations, proceedings, and resulting requirements of the APA or any affiliated state psychological association to which they belong. This is not a polite suggestion — failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation. The rationale is straightforward: the self-regulatory system that protects both the public and the profession can only function if psychologists participate in it honestly and fully. A psychologist who stonewalls an ethics committee undermines the entire framework of professional accountability, and the Ethics Code treats that obstruction with corresponding seriousness.

1.07 Improper Complaints

The ethics complaint system is a powerful tool — and like any powerful tool, it can be weaponized. Standard 1.07 guards against this possibility by prohibiting psychologists from filing or encouraging the filing of ethics complaints that are made with reckless disregard for or willful ignorance of facts that would disprove the allegation. In practice, this means that a psychologist who files a retaliatory complaint against a colleague — knowing full well the allegation is baseless — is committing an ethical violation. The standard protects the integrity of the complaint process and shields innocent psychologists from malicious or careless accusations by their peers.

Note the precise language: the standard targets "reckless disregard" and "willful ignorance," not honest mistakes. A psychologist who files a complaint in good faith based on available information, only to have the complaint later dismissed, has not violated Standard 1.07. The standard addresses intent and negligence, not outcomes.

1.08 Unfair Discrimination Against Complainants and Respondents

Filing or being the subject of an ethics complaint is stressful enough without facing professional retaliation on top of it. Standard 1.08 addresses this concern directly: psychologists do not deny persons employment, advancement, admissions, or other benefits solely because they have made or been the subject of an ethics complaint. This protection runs in both directions — it shields both the person who filed the complaint and the person accused. A training director, for example, cannot refuse to admit an otherwise qualified applicant simply because that applicant once filed an ethics complaint against a former supervisor, nor can a department chair refuse to promote a colleague solely because a complaint was once filed against them.

However, the standard includes an important qualifier: it does not preclude taking action based on the outcome of ethics proceedings. If a formal investigation results in a finding of misconduct and specific sanctions, those outcomes can legitimately factor into employment and advancement decisions. The distinction is between the mere existence of a complaint — which should not be held against anyone — and the substantiated results of a completed investigation, which may be appropriately considered. This nuance is worth internalizing for both the EPPP and for the ethical practice it tests.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • When ethics conflict with law, psychologists must clarify the conflict, make known their commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve it (Standard 1.02). This never permits violating human rights.
  • The first response to learning of a colleague's ethical violation is usually informal resolution (Standard 1.04) -- bringing it to the colleague's attention -- UNLESS doing so would violate confidentiality or the violation is too serious for informal resolution.
  • When informal resolution is not appropriate, the psychologist should take further action such as referral to an ethics committee or licensing board (Standard 1.05).
  • Cooperating with ethics investigations is mandatory; failure to cooperate is itself an ethics violation (Standard 1.06).
  • Filing an ethics complaint with reckless disregard for the facts is prohibited (Standard 1.07).
  • Psychologists may not retaliate against those who file ethics complaints (Standard 1.08).
  • If a psychologist's work is being misused or misrepresented, they must take reasonable steps to correct or minimize the misuse (Standard 1.01).
