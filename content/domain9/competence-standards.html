Standard 2 — Competence, Emergency Services & Scope of Practice

Fundamental Content

Overview of Standard 2: Competence

A clinical psychologist trained primarily in adult cognitive-behavioral therapy receives a referral for a seven-year-old child with suspected autism spectrum disorder. The parents are eager to begin treatment and ask the psychologist to conduct the evaluation. Though well-intentioned, the psychologist has no formal training in pediatric assessment or autism-specific diagnostic instruments. Proceeding with the evaluation would risk misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment recommendations — precisely the kind of harm that Standard 2 of the APA Ethics Code was designed to prevent.

Standard 2 addresses the foundational requirement that psychologists practice only within the boundaries of their competence, based on their education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study, or professional experience. Competence is not a fixed trait but a dynamic, evolving professional obligation — and this standard is one of the most frequently tested areas on the EPPP because it intersects with virtually every professional activity a psychologist undertakes, from therapy and assessment to research, teaching, and consultation.

Competence Defined

Professional competence refers to the ability to effectively and appropriately apply the knowledge, skills, and attitudes necessary to fulfill one's professional roles. It encompasses both intellectual understanding and practical skill, shaped by ongoing education and experience.

Standard 2.01: Boundaries of Competence

Imagine a psychologist who has spent her entire career specializing in adult anxiety disorders. One day, a desperate parent calls: their seven-year-old has been refusing to attend school, crying inconsolably each morning, and the family's pediatrician suspects separation anxiety disorder. The psychologist empathizes deeply and wants to help — but she has never treated a child. Should she take the case? This is precisely the kind of question that Standard 2.01 was designed to answer, and getting it right has profound implications for client welfare, professional integrity, and — not incidentally — the EPPP.

Having established in the preceding section that professional competence encompasses knowledge, skills, and attitudes shaped by ongoing education and experience, we now turn to the ethical boundaries that define where and how psychologists may practice. Standard 2.01 establishes the fundamental rule: psychologists provide services, teach, and conduct research only within the boundaries of their competence. This standard is not a single blanket prohibition — it is a nuanced, multi-part provision that addresses everything from foundational training requirements to the realities of emerging techniques, unfamiliar populations, and forensic contexts. Understanding each subsection is essential for ethical practice and for the licensing examination.

2.01(a) -- Education, Training, and Experience

At the heart of Standard 2.01 is a straightforward principle: psychologists provide services only in areas in which they have established competence through education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study, or professional experience. Returning to our opening scenario, the anxiety specialist would need to ask herself whether she has sufficient grounding in child development, pediatric assessment, and evidence-based interventions for children before accepting the referral. Simply being a licensed psychologist does not grant blanket authority to treat all populations or all conditions. Competence is typically demonstrated through:

  • Graduate coursework in the specific area
  • Supervised clinical or research experience
  • Postdoctoral training or specialty fellowships
  • Continuing education in the specific domain
  • Peer consultation and supervision

Think of competence as a credential that must be earned area by area, not a universal license to practice. A neuropsychologist with extensive training in traumatic brain injury assessment is not, by virtue of that expertise alone, competent to conduct child custody evaluations. Each new domain of practice requires its own foundation of preparation. On the EPPP, questions about this subsection often test whether a psychologist's existing training is sufficient for a novel request — or whether additional steps are needed before proceeding.

2.01(b) -- New Areas of Practice

Psychology does not stand still. New treatment modalities emerge, technology reshapes service delivery, and societal changes create demands that no graduate program could have anticipated a decade earlier. Subsection 2.01(b) addresses this reality directly: when psychologists wish to provide services in areas where recognized professional standards do not yet exist, they take reasonable steps to ensure the competence of their work and to protect clients, students, and others from harm. This provision acknowledges that psychology is an evolving field and that rigid adherence to only well-established practices would leave many clients without care — but it simultaneously insists that innovation must be pursued responsibly.

Example

A psychologist trained in cognitive-behavioral therapy wants to begin providing telepsychology services. Under 2.01(b), the psychologist should obtain training in telehealth best practices, learn the relevant technology, understand the legal requirements of interstate practice, and seek consultation from colleagues experienced in telepsychology before beginning.

Notice the key phrase: "reasonable steps." The Ethics Code does not demand perfection or the completion of an entirely new doctoral program before a psychologist enters an emerging area. Instead, it requires a thoughtful, documented effort to acquire the knowledge and skills needed to practice safely. This might involve attending workshops, reviewing the relevant literature, consulting with experienced colleagues, or arranging peer supervision. For EPPP purposes, remember that 2.01(b) applies when professional standards in the new area are not yet well established — distinguishing it from situations where standards exist but the individual psychologist simply hasn't met them.

2.01(c) -- Unfamiliar Populations

Consider a psychologist practicing in a rural community who begins receiving referrals from a recently resettled refugee population. She has strong general clinical skills but no formal training in working with this specific cultural group. Can she ethically see these clients? Subsection 2.01(c) provides guidance: in areas where recognized training standards for serving specific populations do not yet exist, psychologists with closely related prior training or experience may provide such services, with appropriate study, training, supervision, and/or consultation. This is particularly relevant to working with diverse cultural, ethnic, or linguistic groups, where the psychologist's existing therapeutic competence provides a foundation but culturally responsive practice demands additional learning.

The ethical mandate here is not to turn clients away simply because a perfect match of training and population does not exist — that approach could leave vulnerable communities entirely without care. Rather, the psychologist must actively seek out the knowledge she needs: reading the literature on the population's cultural context, consulting with cultural brokers or community leaders, pursuing relevant continuing education, and, when possible, arranging supervision with a colleague who has more experience with the population in question. This subsection reflects psychology's commitment to both competence and access to care.

2.01(d) -- Forensic Activities

Sometimes circumstances — rather than personal ambition — push a psychologist beyond their competence boundaries. Imagine a psychologist working as the sole mental health provider in a remote correctional facility. An inmate presents with symptoms suggesting a serious psychotic disorder, but the psychologist's training is primarily in substance use disorders, and no psychiatrist or psychologist with expertise in psychotic disorders is available for weeks. Subsection 2.01(d) addresses this predicament: when psychologists are asked to provide services to individuals for whom appropriate mental health services are not available and for which psychologists have not obtained the requisite competence, they may provide services with the closest related competence — but only until a qualified provider becomes available.

This is a stopgap provision, not a permanent license. The psychologist in this scenario would be ethically justified in providing basic stabilization and supportive care, but she must make active efforts to connect the client with a more qualified provider as soon as one can be located. For the EPPP, note the critical distinction between this subsection and the emergency provision in Standard 2.02 (which we will address next): 2.01(d) applies when services are unavailable for an extended period, not necessarily during an acute crisis. The emphasis is on providing the best available care while working toward an appropriate referral.

2.01(e) -- New Techniques

Closely related to 2.01(b), this subsection focuses specifically on emerging treatment techniques or assessment methods. In emerging areas where generally recognized standards for preparatory training do not yet exist, psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure competence and protect clients from harm. For example, when virtual reality exposure therapy was first introduced, no standardized training curriculum existed. A psychologist wishing to implement VR-based treatments would need to study the available evidence, consult with researchers developing the technology, practice the technique under supervision when possible, and carefully monitor client outcomes — all before integrating it into routine practice. The common thread across 2.01(b) and 2.01(e) is the obligation to act responsibly in the face of novelty, balancing innovation with client safety.

2.01(f) -- Familiarity with Relevant Factors

Forensic psychology sits at the intersection of clinical expertise and legal process, and this intersection creates unique competence demands. When assuming forensic roles, psychologists must be knowledgeable about the judicial or administrative rules governing their participation. This means understanding the rules of evidence, the standards for expert testimony (such as the Daubert or Frye standards, depending on jurisdiction), the specific legal questions at issue in the case, and the differences between therapeutic and forensic relationships. A psychologist who is an excellent clinician but unfamiliar with the adversarial nature of courtroom proceedings, the limits of confidentiality in forensic contexts, or the relevant legal standards of the jurisdiction risks providing testimony that is unhelpful, misleading, or ethically problematic. Before accepting a forensic role, a psychologist should ensure that her understanding of the legal landscape is as solid as her clinical knowledge.

Taken together, the subsections of Standard 2.01 create a flexible but demanding framework. They recognize that the world psychologists work in is dynamic — new populations, new technologies, new legal contexts — while insisting that the profession's first obligation is always to practice within the limits of what one can do competently and safely. As we turn next to Standard 2.02, we will see what happens when genuine emergencies override the usual boundaries.

Standard 2.02: Providing Services in Emergencies

Having established that psychologists must practice within clearly defined boundaries of competence, an important question naturally arises: What happens when a crisis erupts and no one else is available to help? Imagine a devastating hurricane strikes a coastal community. Evacuation shelters overflow with traumatized families, children separated from parents, and individuals experiencing acute psychotic episodes. The community's few mental health providers have evacuated or are themselves displaced. A psychologist who specializes in neuropsychological assessment — someone who has never conducted crisis intervention or disaster mental health work — arrives at a shelter as a volunteer. People desperately need psychological support right now. Should this psychologist turn them away because trauma intervention falls outside their typical scope of practice?

Standard 2.02 answers this question with a compassionate and practical provision. In emergencies, when individuals need psychological services and no other mental health professionals are available to provide them, psychologists may deliver services outside their usual areas of competence to ensure that people in crisis are not left without care. This standard recognizes a fundamental ethical reality: rigidly adhering to competence boundaries while someone suffers a preventable psychological crisis would itself constitute an ethical failure. The APA Ethics Code balances the imperative to practice competently with the imperative to prevent harm through inaction.

Critically, this emergency exception carries a built-in time limit. The psychologist must discontinue these services as soon as the emergency ends or as soon as appropriately trained providers become available — whichever comes first. The neuropsychologist volunteering at the hurricane shelter provides crisis counseling and psychological first aid for the duration of the acute disaster, but once trauma-specialized clinicians arrive or the emergency phase passes, that psychologist steps back and returns to their area of expertise. This provision is not a permanent expansion of one's competence — it is a temporary bridge that ensures no one falls through the cracks during a crisis.

Consider another scenario: a psychologist who works exclusively with adults in a private practice encounters a situation at a community health center during a regional emergency where the only patients seeking help are young children exhibiting acute stress reactions. Under ordinary circumstances, this psychologist would refer those children to a child specialist. But during the emergency, with no child psychologists available, Standard 2.02 permits — and arguably encourages — the psychologist to provide whatever supportive services they can. The ethical obligation to help in a crisis temporarily supersedes the usual requirement to stay within one's defined competence boundaries.

EPPP Tip

Standard 2.02 is the emergency exception to competence boundaries. On the EPPP, if a question describes a crisis situation (e.g., a natural disaster) where no other provider is available, the psychologist may provide services outside their typical area of competence. However, this is temporary — they must stop once the emergency ends or a qualified provider is found. Watch for answer choices that frame this as an absolute rule ("a psychologist must never practice outside their competence"); the emergency exception makes such absolutes incorrect. Also note that both conditions must be present: there must be a genuine emergency, and other appropriate services must be unavailable. A psychologist cannot invoke this standard simply because a situation feels urgent if qualified providers are accessible.

Standard 2.03: Maintaining Competence

Imagine a psychologist who earned their doctorate fifteen years ago. Their training was rigorous, their internship demanding, and they passed the EPPP on their first attempt. But since then, they haven't attended a single workshop, read a journal article, or sought peer consultation. They continue to use the same assessment instruments and therapeutic approaches they learned in graduate school — even as the evidence base has shifted dramatically beneath them. Are they still competent? The answer, according to the APA Ethics Code, is almost certainly no. Competence is not a credential you earn once and carry forever; it is a living commitment that demands ongoing cultivation throughout your career.

We've just seen how Standard 2.02 permits psychologists to temporarily step beyond their competence boundaries in genuine emergencies. Standard 2.03 addresses something more fundamental: the obligation to ensure that your existing competence doesn't erode over time. Where Standards 2.01 and 2.02 deal with the boundaries of competence — what you can and cannot practice — Standard 2.03 speaks to its depth and currency within the areas you already claim as your own.

Psychologists undertake ongoing efforts to develop and maintain their competence. This is not a vague aspiration but an active, career-long ethical obligation. In practice, maintaining competence includes:

  • Staying current with scientific and professional developments in their areas of practice
  • Pursuing continuing education (CE) activities
  • Engaging in peer consultation and supervision
  • Reviewing current literature and research findings
  • Attending professional conferences and workshops

Consider a clinical psychologist specializing in anxiety disorders. Over the past decade, the evidence base for treating specific phobias has evolved considerably — new research on inhibitory learning models has reshaped how many clinicians conceptualize and deliver exposure therapy. A psychologist who maintains competence would be aware of these developments, might attend a workshop on updated exposure protocols, and would integrate the best available evidence into their practice. One who does not risks providing outdated — and potentially less effective — care to their clients, even within their stated area of expertise.

Continuing Education Requirements

The ethical mandate of Standard 2.03 is reinforced by regulatory teeth. Most state licensing boards require psychologists to complete a specified number of continuing education (CE) credits during each renewal period (typically 20-40 hours per biennium). These requirements serve as a structural safeguard, ensuring that licensees engage in at least a minimum level of professional development. Many states go further by mandating specific CE topics such as ethics, cultural diversity, suicide prevention, or evidence-based practice — reflecting areas where outdated knowledge poses particular risk to the public.

It is worth noting, however, that CE requirements represent a floor, not a ceiling. Simply accumulating the minimum number of credits does not automatically mean a psychologist has fulfilled their ethical obligation under Standard 2.03. A neuropsychologist who completes all required CE hours in workshops on practice management, while neglecting to stay current with developments in neuroimaging or concussion assessment, may satisfy the licensing board's requirements but still fall short of the ethical standard. For the EPPP, remember that maintaining competence is first and foremost an ethical obligation — one that exists independently of, and often exceeds, state regulatory mandates.

The Competence Constellation Model

But what exactly does "competence" encompass? The concept is broader and more multidimensional than it might initially appear. A psychologist could be highly skilled in administering cognitive assessments but lack the reflective self-awareness to recognize when personal biases are affecting their clinical judgment. They might be an excellent therapist but a poor supervisor. Rodolfa et al. (2005) proposed a competence constellation model that captures this complexity by distinguishing between two categories: foundational competencies — the underlying knowledge, skills, and attitudes that support all professional functions — and functional competencies — the specific activities psychologists perform in their professional roles.

Think of foundational competencies as the soil from which all professional work grows, and functional competencies as the distinct plants that emerge from that soil. A psychologist's ability to conduct effective intervention (a functional competency) depends on their capacity for reflective self-assessment, their grasp of scientific methods, and their sensitivity to cultural diversity (all foundational competencies). This model is particularly useful for understanding why competence maintenance requires attention to both domains — a therapist who keeps up with the latest CBT protocols but neglects self-reflection about their own countertransference reactions has only partially maintained their competence.

Foundational CompetenciesFunctional Competencies
Reflective practice and self-assessmentAssessment, diagnosis, and case conceptualization
Scientific knowledge and methodsIntervention
Relationships (therapeutic alliance)Consultation
Ethical and legal standardsResearch and evaluation
Individual and cultural diversitySupervision and teaching
Interdisciplinary systemsManagement and administration

For the EPPP, the key takeaway from the competence constellation model is the distinction between these two tiers. If a question asks about self-assessment or cultural awareness, recognize these as foundational competencies — the bedrock that underlies all professional activity. If a question asks about assessment, therapy, consultation, or supervision, these are functional competencies — the specific professional roles psychologists perform. Understanding this framework helps you appreciate why Standard 2.03 demands such a broad commitment: maintaining competence means tending to both the roots and the branches of your professional practice.

Standard 2.04: Bases for Scientific and Professional Judgments

Imagine a psychologist who, after attending a weekend workshop on a novel therapeutic technique, begins applying it broadly across their caseload — despite the absence of controlled studies supporting its use and without consideration of how it fits the unique needs of each client. When a colleague questions the approach, the psychologist replies, "It just feels right to me." This scenario cuts to the heart of why Standard 2.04 exists: psychology's credibility as a profession depends on practitioners anchoring their work not in intuition or enthusiasm, but in the established scientific and professional knowledge base of the discipline.

Having explored in the previous section how psychologists maintain competence over time through ongoing education and self-assessment, we now turn to a closely related but distinct question: on what foundation should psychologists build their professional decisions in the first place? Standard 2.04 addresses this directly by requiring that every clinical judgment, assessment interpretation, treatment recommendation, and professional opinion be grounded in defensible, empirically informed reasoning. It is not enough to be competent in a general sense — one must also ensure that specific judgments flow from the right sources of knowledge.

In practice, this means psychologists should rely on multiple pillars of professional knowledge that reinforce and check one another:

  • Peer-reviewed research and empirical evidence
  • Established theories grounded in scientific investigation
  • Professional guidelines and practice parameters
  • Clinical judgment informed by training and experience, not personal bias or speculation

Consider how these pillars work together in a clinical context. A psychologist evaluating a child for ADHD would draw on peer-reviewed diagnostic criteria and validated rating scales (empirical evidence), use a developmental framework for understanding attention regulation (established theory), follow professional guidelines such as those published by the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (practice parameters), and integrate all of this with their own clinical observations and training — while remaining vigilant against confirmation bias or premature conclusions. Standard 2.04 does not require that every decision be dictated by a single study; rather, it demands that the overall reasoning process be scientifically grounded and professionally defensible.

This standard also serves as a safeguard against the misuse of professional authority. When a psychologist offers testimony in a custody hearing, writes a report for a disability determination, or recommends a particular treatment approach, the weight of that opinion derives from its scientific basis. Opinions rooted in personal ideology, unexamined assumptions, or outdated training undermine public trust and can cause real harm. Standard 2.04 thus functions as both an ethical mandate and a quality-control mechanism for the profession.

Evidence-Based Practice

The APA Presidential Task Force (2006) defined evidence-based practice in psychology (EBPP) as the integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture, and preferences. EBPP is the operational expression of Standard 2.04. Notice that this definition is intentionally three-legged: research evidence alone does not dictate practice, nor does clinical experience alone justify it. Instead, EBPP asks psychologists to weave together empirical findings, seasoned professional judgment, and a deep respect for the individual sitting across from them — their cultural background, values, and treatment goals. When exam questions reference Standard 2.04, they are often testing whether you understand that scientific grounding and individualized clinical reasoning are complementary, not competing, obligations.

As we move to Standard 2.05, we shift from the knowledge base underlying professional judgments to the practical question of how psychologists extend their work through others — and what responsibilities follow when tasks are delegated to supervisees, assistants, or other professionals.

Standard 2.05: Delegation of Work to Others

No psychologist works in complete isolation. Whether in a busy group practice, a university training clinic, or a research lab, the reality of professional life means that psychologists routinely rely on others — supervisees, psychometrists, research assistants, administrative staff — to carry out portions of their work. But here is the critical ethical question: when you hand off a task, you do not hand off responsibility. Standard 2.05 addresses exactly this tension, spelling out the obligations that follow whenever a psychologist delegates work to another person. If Standard 2.04 asks psychologists to ground their own judgments in science and professional knowledge, Standard 2.05 extends that commitment outward by ensuring that anyone acting on a psychologist's behalf also meets appropriate standards of competence and ethical conduct.

Consider a clinical psychologist who supervises a first-year practicum student in a community mental health center. The supervisor asks the student to conduct an intake interview with a new client. This seems routine enough — but what if the client presents with active suicidal ideation and a complex trauma history? The supervisor cannot simply assign the case and walk away. Standard 2.05 requires that the psychologist thoughtfully evaluate whether the student has the training and skill to handle the situation, provide adequate oversight, and step in when the demands of the case exceed the student's current competence. Delegation, in other words, is not abdication. It is a structured, ethically governed process.

Psychologists who delegate work to employees, supervisees, research or teaching assistants, or others must meet three interrelated obligations:

  • Take reasonable steps to avoid delegating work to persons with a multiple relationship with those being served — for instance, a psychologist should not ask a graduate assistant to score the neuropsychological battery of a client who happens to be the assistant's neighbor and close friend
  • Authorize only those responsibilities the person can be expected to perform competently based on their education, training, or experience — meaning you match the task to the delegate's actual skill level, not simply to what is convenient
  • Ensure that such persons perform the services competently — which requires ongoing supervision, review of work products, and a willingness to intervene when quality falls short

Notice how these three requirements work together as a coherent system. The first protects the integrity of the professional relationship by screening for conflicts of interest. The second sets appropriate boundaries on what is delegated in the first place. And the third imposes a continuing duty of quality assurance — the psychologist must monitor, not merely assign. Together, they reflect a core ethical principle: the delegating psychologist retains ultimate accountability for the services provided under their authority, regardless of who physically carries out the task.

Example

A psychologist delegates psychological testing administration to a psychometrist. Under 2.05, the psychologist must ensure the psychometrist is properly trained in administering the specific test, must supervise the work appropriately, and retains ultimate responsibility for the interpretation and clinical use of test results. If, for instance, the psychometrist deviates from standardized administration procedures — failing to time a subtest correctly or providing unauthorized prompts — the ethical responsibility falls squarely on the delegating psychologist. This is why careful initial training, clear written protocols, and periodic observation of the psychometrist's work are not just best practices but ethical imperatives under this standard.

For exam purposes, remember that Standard 2.05 is frequently tested in scenarios involving supervision and the use of unlicensed personnel. The key takeaway is straightforward but essential: delegation does not diminish the psychologist's responsibility. Whether the delegate is a seasoned psychometrist, a predoctoral intern, or an undergraduate research assistant, the psychologist must ensure that the work assigned falls within the person's competence, that multiple relationship concerns have been addressed, and that quality monitoring remains active throughout. When you encounter exam questions about who bears responsibility for errors made by a supervisee or assistant, the answer almost always points back to the supervising psychologist — because Standard 2.05 makes the ethical chain of accountability unmistakably clear.

Standard 2.06: Personal Problems and Conflicts

Imagine a psychologist going through a bitter divorce. She finds herself distracted during therapy sessions, losing focus on what clients are saying, and at times feeling so emotionally overwhelmed that she struggles to maintain appropriate boundaries. One evening, she realizes she gave a client advice that reflected her own marital frustrations rather than the client's therapeutic needs. This scenario — a psychologist whose personal difficulties spill over into professional work — is precisely what Standard 2.06 was designed to address, and it represents one of the most commonly tested ethics topics on the EPPP.

Having just explored Standard 2.05 and the psychologist's responsibility when delegating work to others, we now turn inward — to the psychologist's responsibility to monitor their own functioning. While the previous standards focus on ensuring others are competent, Standard 2.06 asks a more personal and sometimes uncomfortable question: Am I currently competent to do this work? Psychologists are human beings, subject to the same stressors, grief, illness, substance use difficulties, and emotional turmoil that bring clients into treatment. The Ethics Code recognizes this reality and provides clear guidance on what psychologists must do when personal problems threaten their professional effectiveness.

2.06(a) -- Awareness of Personal Problems

The first component of this standard establishes a preventive obligation: psychologists must not begin professional activities when they know — or reasonably should know — that their personal problems create a substantial likelihood of impaired performance. Notice the language here is forward-looking and proactive. It does not merely say "stop when you notice harm." It says don't start if impairment is foreseeable. For example, a psychologist recently diagnosed with a serious substance use disorder should not take on a caseload of new clients struggling with addiction when the psychologist's own condition is untreated and likely to compromise clinical judgment. Similarly, a psychologist experiencing acute grief after the sudden death of a child would need to carefully evaluate whether beginning bereavement counseling with new clients is something they can do competently at that moment.

This standard also includes the important "should know" qualifier, which means that willful blindness is not an excuse. A psychologist who is drinking heavily every night and arriving at work hungover cannot later claim, "I didn't realize it was affecting my work." The standard holds psychologists to the level of awareness that a reasonable professional would have about how their personal difficulties might impair their competence.

2.06(b) -- Taking Appropriate Action

When psychologists become aware — whether gradually or suddenly — that personal problems are beginning to interfere with their ability to perform work-related duties adequately, the Ethics Code requires them to take concrete, appropriate steps. Insight alone is not enough; action must follow awareness. The standard identifies several potential responses, and in practice the right course of action depends on the severity and nature of the impairment:

  • Obtaining professional consultation or assistance
  • Determining whether they should limit, suspend, or terminate work-related duties
  • Seeking personal therapy
  • Taking a leave of absence if necessary

Consider a clinical psychologist who begins experiencing significant anxiety after being the victim of a violent crime. She notices that conducting trauma-focused therapy sessions triggers her own distress, and her case notes are becoming less thorough. The ethically appropriate response is not to simply "push through." Instead, she might first consult with a trusted colleague, then enter her own therapy, and simultaneously decide to refer her trauma-focused clients to another provider while she recovers — continuing to see only those clients whose treatment does not exacerbate her symptoms. If her symptoms worsen, a temporary leave of absence may become necessary. The key principle is that the psychologist bears primary responsibility for recognizing and addressing their own impairment — not their clients, not their employer, and not a licensing board.

EPPP Tip

EPPP questions about impaired psychologists test whether you know that the ethical response involves the psychologist taking responsibility for their own impairment. The first step is self-awareness; the second is taking appropriate corrective action (consultation, therapy, limiting practice). Colleagues who become aware of an impaired psychologist should attempt informal resolution (Standard 1.04) by speaking to the psychologist directly. On exam questions, be wary of answer choices that jump immediately to filing an ethics complaint — the Ethics Code favors informal resolution as a first step when feasible. Also remember that Standard 2.06 places the burden of self-monitoring squarely on the impaired psychologist; waiting for someone else to notice the problem does not satisfy the standard's requirements.

Scope of Practice

Having explored the ethical obligations surrounding competence — from maintaining boundaries of expertise to managing personal problems that could impair professional functioning — we now arrive at a concept that sits at the intersection of ethics and law. Consider this scenario: a clinical psychologist licensed in Oregon relocates to Texas. She has years of experience, strong training, and excellent clinical skills. But when she reviews Texas licensing regulations, she discovers that certain activities she routinely performed in Oregon are not included in the Texas scope of practice for psychologists. Despite being fully competent, she is not legally authorized to perform those activities in her new state. This distinction — between what you are trained to do and what the law says you may do — is one of the most tested concepts on the EPPP and one of the most consequential in real-world practice.

Scope of practice refers to the legally defined range of activities that a licensed professional is authorized to perform. Unlike competence, which is grounded in individual training and ethical standards, scope of practice is established by state licensing laws and varies across jurisdictions. A psychologist practicing in one state may have prescriptive authority, while a psychologist in a neighboring state does not — not because of differences in training, but because of differences in law. Understanding where scope of practice begins and ends is essential for ethical and legal practice, and for answering EPPP questions that probe the distinction between ethical obligations and legal boundaries.

Competence vs. Scope of Practice

These two concepts are closely related, and EPPP questions frequently test whether you can distinguish between them. Think of scope of practice as the outer legal fence around an entire profession's permitted activities, while competence is the smaller, individualized space within that fence where a particular psychologist is ethically qualified to work. A psychologist might be legally permitted to conduct forensic evaluations under state law, but if she has never received forensic training, she lacks the individual competence to do so ethically. Conversely, a psychologist might have extensive competence in an area — say, prescribing psychotropic medications after completing specialized training — but if her state's scope of practice laws do not authorize psychologists to prescribe, she cannot legally do so regardless of her expertise. The table below crystallizes these distinctions:

Competence (Ethical)Scope of Practice (Legal)
Defined by the Ethics Code and professional standardsDefined by state statutes and licensing regulations
Based on individual training, education, and experienceBased on the profession's legally permitted activities
Self-assessed and regulated by professional normsExternally imposed by law and regulation
Can be narrower than scope of practiceSets the outer boundary of permitted practice

Notice the asymmetry in the last row: a psychologist's individual competence can be — and often is — narrower than the profession's legal scope of practice. You are not expected to be competent in every activity your license theoretically permits. However, competence should never exceed scope of practice; no amount of training authorizes you to perform activities that your state's laws reserve for other professions. On the EPPP, the correct answer almost always recognizes that both conditions must be met: the activity must fall within the legal scope of practice, and the individual psychologist must possess the competence to perform it.

Example

A state's scope of practice for psychologists may include neuropsychological assessment. However, a psychologist trained only in clinical therapy has not established individual competence in neuropsychological assessment, even though it falls within the profession's legal scope of practice. The psychologist should not perform neuropsychological assessments without obtaining appropriate training. Now flip the scenario: imagine a psychologist with extensive neuropsychological training relocates to a jurisdiction where the scope of practice statute does not explicitly include neuropsychological assessment as a permitted activity for psychologists. Despite her deep competence, she would need to verify her legal authority before proceeding — because competence without legal authorization is not sufficient.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Psychologists practice only within the boundaries of their competence based on education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study, or professional experience.
  • The emergency exception (2.02) allows temporary practice outside competence boundaries when no other provider is available during a crisis.
  • Maintaining competence (2.03) is an ongoing obligation requiring continuing education and professional development.
  • Professional judgments must be based on established scientific and professional knowledge (2.04), not personal opinion or speculation.
  • When delegating work (2.05), the psychologist retains responsibility for ensuring the quality and competence of the delegated services.
  • Personal problems that may impair functioning must be addressed proactively (2.06) through consultation, therapy, or limiting practice.
  • Scope of practice (legal) differs from competence (ethical); a psychologist may have legal authority to perform an activity without having individual competence to do so.
