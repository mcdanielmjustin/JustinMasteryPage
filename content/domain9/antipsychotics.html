Introduction to Antipsychotic Medications

Antipsychotic medications (also called neuroleptics) are the primary pharmacological treatment for schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders. They are also used for bipolar disorder, severe agitation, Tourette's disorder, and as adjuncts for treatment-resistant depression. Antipsychotics are divided into two main classes: first-generation (typical) and second-generation (atypical).

For the EPPP, understanding the differences between these two classes -- particularly their mechanisms of action, side effect profiles, and the major adverse effects (EPS, tardive dyskinesia, NMS, and metabolic syndrome) -- is essential.

Although this chapter focuses on antipsychotic agents, psychologists should be aware that other medication classes are frequently prescribed alongside or as alternatives to antipsychotics in complex clinical presentations. Among these, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) such as venlafaxine (Effexor) and duloxetine (Cymbalta) play a significant role in contemporary psychopharmacology. SNRIs inhibit the reuptake of both serotonin and norepinephrine, providing a dual-action mechanism that distinguishes them from SSRIs. This dual mechanism makes them effective across a broad range of conditions, including major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, and neuropathic pain syndromes such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy and fibromyalgia. Duloxetine has received specific FDA approval for several chronic pain conditions, while venlafaxine is particularly noted for its dose-dependent pharmacology — at lower doses it functions primarily as a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, with norepinephrine reuptake inhibition becoming more prominent at higher doses. Common side effects of SNRIs include nausea, dizziness, insomnia, and elevated blood pressure (particularly with venlafaxine at higher doses). Abrupt discontinuation of either medication can produce a pronounced discontinuation syndrome characterized by dizziness, nausea, irritability, and sensory disturbances often described as "brain zaps."

Example

A patient with comorbid major depression and diabetic neuropathy may benefit from duloxetine because it addresses both the mood disorder and the chronic pain condition through a single medication, reducing polypharmacy burden.

The Dopamine Hypothesis of Schizophrenia

Having explored how antidepressants and other psychotropic medications work by targeting specific neurotransmitter systems, we now turn to a class of medications designed to address one of the most debilitating psychiatric conditions: schizophrenia. To understand how antipsychotic medications work — and why they help with some symptoms while sometimes worsening others — we first need to understand the neurobiological theory that has shaped their development for more than half a century.

Consider a young man in his early twenties who arrives at an emergency department convinced that government agents are monitoring his thoughts through a chip implanted in his skull. He hears voices commenting on his behavior, and his speech jumps between loosely connected ideas. His family reports that over the past several months, he has also become increasingly withdrawn — spending days in bed, showing little emotion, and abandoning his college coursework. These two clusters of symptoms — the florid psychotic experiences and the quiet erosion of motivation and affect — represent a central puzzle in schizophrenia. How can a single disorder produce such dramatically different symptom profiles? The answer, according to the most influential neurobiological model of schizophrenia, lies in dopamine — but not in a simple "too much" or "too little" story.

The dopamine hypothesis proposes that schizophrenia symptoms result from dysregulated dopamine activity in the brain — but critically, the dysregulation runs in opposite directions depending on which brain circuit is involved. This is not a single switch flipped the wrong way; it is more like a seesaw, with dopamine activity tipped too high in some pathways and too low in others:

  • Positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thought) are associated with excess dopamine activity in the mesolimbic pathway — the reward and salience circuit that projects from the ventral tegmental area to limbic structures. When this pathway is hyperactive, the brain assigns overwhelming significance to irrelevant stimuli, generating the delusions and perceptual distortions that characterize psychosis.
  • Negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, alogia, anhedonia) are associated with reduced dopamine activity in the mesocortical pathway (prefrontal cortex) — the circuit responsible for executive function, motivation, and emotional expression. When dopamine is deficient here, the result is the cognitive dulling and motivational collapse that families often describe as "losing" the person they knew.

This dual-pathway framework is clinically essential because it explains a frustrating paradox that every prescriber confronts. Returning to our young man in the emergency department: a clinician might administer a first-generation antipsychotic that broadly blocks dopamine D2 receptors throughout the brain. Within days, the paranoid delusions begin to fade and the voices quiet — the mesolimbic excess is being reined in. But over the following weeks, his family notices he seems even more flat, more withdrawn, more cognitively sluggish than before. The same medication that corrected the dopamine surplus in one pathway has deepened the dopamine deficit in another. Understanding this trade-off is the key to understanding why different classes of antipsychotics exist and why atypical antipsychotics were developed.

This helps explain why typical antipsychotics, which broadly block dopamine D2 receptors, effectively treat positive symptoms but often worsen negative symptoms and cause movement disorders (due to dopamine blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway). The dopamine hypothesis, in its modern form, is not merely about excess dopamine — it is about a complex imbalance across multiple neural circuits, each with distinct clinical consequences.

Four Dopamine Pathways

For the EPPP, you need to know these four pathways and what happens when dopamine is blocked in each. Think of them as four distinct "channels" of dopamine activity, each with its own clinical story: Mesolimbic: Excess dopamine leads to positive symptoms — hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking. This is the pathway antipsychotics are designed to target. Mesocortical: Reduced dopamine leads to negative and cognitive symptoms — flat affect, poor motivation, and impaired working memory. Blocking dopamine here makes these symptoms worse, not better. Nigrostriatal: Dopamine blockade here causes EPS (extrapyramidal symptoms) and tardive dyskinesia, because this pathway normally coordinates smooth, voluntary movement — the same pathway affected in Parkinson's disease. Tuberoinfundibular: Dopamine blockade here increases prolactin, causing hyperprolactinemia — which can lead to breast enlargement, galactorrhea, menstrual irregularities, and sexual dysfunction. A helpful mnemonic: Meso-Limbic = Lively psychosis; Meso-Cortical = Cognitive/negative; Nigro-Striatal = Stiffness (movement); Tubero-Infundibular = Titration of prolactin.

As we move into our discussion of first-generation antipsychotics, keep this four-pathway map firmly in mind. It will serve as your clinical compass for understanding why these medications produce the specific side-effect profiles they do — and why the search for better antipsychotics eventually led to the development of the atypical agents.

First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics

Now that we understand how dopamine pathways contribute to schizophrenia's symptoms, we can appreciate why the first antipsychotic medications targeted dopamine so directly — and why that singular focus came with a predictable set of trade-offs. Imagine a clinician in the 1950s witnessing something remarkable for the first time: a patient with florid psychosis — hearing commanding voices, convinced that government agents are monitoring his thoughts — becomes calm and coherent within days of receiving a new medication called chlorpromazine. It was nothing short of revolutionary. Patients who had been confined to state hospitals for years could suddenly engage in conversation, feed themselves, and sometimes return home. But the celebration was tempered by a troubling observation: many of these same patients began developing involuntary muscle stiffness, tremors, and restless movements that resembled Parkinson's disease. The era of antipsychotic pharmacotherapy had begun — and with it, the enduring clinical challenge of balancing therapeutic benefits against motor side effects.

First-generation antipsychotics (FGAs), also called "typical" or "conventional" antipsychotics, work primarily by blocking dopamine D2 receptors throughout the brain. As you'll recall from the dopamine hypothesis, blocking D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway is what reduces positive symptoms like hallucinations and delusions. However, because these drugs are not selective — they block D2 receptors across all four dopamine pathways — they inevitably produce side effects in the nigrostriatal pathway (causing movement disorders) and the tuberoinfundibular pathway (causing elevated prolactin levels). Understanding this broad-spectrum D2 blockade is the key to understanding virtually everything about FGA pharmacology. These medications are classified by potency, a distinction that has direct, clinically meaningful implications for which side effects a patient is most likely to experience.

High-Potency Typical Antipsychotics

The concept of "potency" in pharmacology refers to how much of a drug is needed to achieve a therapeutic effect — not how effective the drug is. A high-potency antipsychotic requires lower doses to achieve D2 blockade, but this tight binding to dopamine receptors comes at a cost: a significantly greater risk of extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS), those Parkinson-like movement disturbances that plagued early antipsychotic treatment. On the positive side, high-potency agents tend to produce less sedation, fewer anticholinergic effects (like dry mouth, constipation, and blurred vision), and less orthostatic hypotension — the sudden drop in blood pressure that can cause dizziness or fainting when a patient stands up. Think of it this way: high-potency agents are "cleaner" in terms of blocking primarily dopamine receptors, but that precision means the motor system takes the brunt of the side effects.

Consider a clinical scenario: a young man is brought to the emergency department in an acutely agitated psychotic state. The psychiatrist orders intramuscular haloperidol because it works quickly and effectively to manage the crisis. Within hours, the agitation subsides — but over the next few days, the treatment team notices the patient developing muscle rigidity and a shuffling gait. This is the classic high-potency trade-off in action: excellent efficacy against positive symptoms, but the nigrostriatal pathway is paying the price.

Pimozide (Orap) stands out because it is specifically FDA-approved for Tourette's disorder, making it a unique entry in this class; however, its risk of QT prolongation (a cardiac conduction abnormality that can lead to dangerous arrhythmias) limits its use and requires careful cardiac monitoring.

Low-Potency Typical Antipsychotics

If high-potency agents are relatively selective D2 blockers, low-potency typical antipsychotics are the pharmacological equivalent of a wide-angle lens: they block dopamine receptors but also exert substantial effects on histamine, acetylcholine, and alpha-adrenergic receptors. This broader receptor profile means that higher doses are needed to achieve adequate D2 blockade, but the upside is a lower risk of EPS. The downside? Patients are much more likely to experience significant sedation (from histamine blockade), anticholinergic effects such as dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, and blurred vision (from muscarinic receptor blockade), and orthostatic hypotension (from alpha-1 adrenergic blockade). An easy mnemonic: low potency means "low EPS but lots of everything else."

Picture an older adult with schizophrenia who is prescribed chlorpromazine. She feels drowsy throughout the day, notices her mouth is constantly dry, and nearly falls when she stands up too quickly from her chair — a dangerous combination in an elderly patient. Her treatment team must weigh whether the reduction in psychotic symptoms justifies these burdensome and potentially hazardous side effects, particularly the fall risk associated with orthostatic hypotension.

Chlorpromazine (Thorazine) deserves special attention as a landmark in psychiatric history. Discovered in the 1950s, it was the drug that launched the psychopharmacological revolution and fundamentally transformed the treatment of severe mental illness. Its 100 mg dose serves as the standard reference point — the "chlorpromazine equivalent" — against which all other antipsychotic potencies are compared. However, chronic use can produce photosensitivity (increased vulnerability to sunburn) and corneal deposits, reminders that even groundbreaking medications carry long-term costs. Thioridazine (Mellaril), meanwhile, carries the highest risk of retinitis pigmentosa — a serious and potentially irreversible eye condition — among all antipsychotics, along with dangerous QT prolongation. These cardiac and ophthalmological risks have severely restricted its clinical use, making it essentially a last-resort medication today.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

Imagine a young man brought to the emergency department just hours after receiving his first injection of haloperidol. His neck is twisted painfully to one side, his eyes are locked in an upward gaze, and he is terrified. Or picture a middle-aged woman who has taken chlorpromazine faithfully for years and now cannot stop her tongue from darting in and out of her mouth — a movement she barely notices but that profoundly affects how others interact with her. These are not rare curiosities. They are among the most clinically significant and distressing adverse effects in all of psychopharmacology, and understanding them is essential for any practitioner who prescribes, monitors, or provides therapy to patients taking antipsychotic medications.

In the preceding section, we noted that high-potency typical antipsychotics such as haloperidol bind tightly to dopamine D2 receptors and carry a greater risk of movement-related side effects, while low-potency agents like chlorpromazine trade that risk for more sedation and anticholinergic burden. Now we turn to those movement-related side effects in detail — a family of disorders collectively known as extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS). These are movement disorders caused by dopamine D2 blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway, the brain circuit that fine-tunes voluntary movement. When antipsychotic drugs block too many D2 receptors in this pathway — particularly beyond the roughly 80% occupancy threshold — the delicate balance between dopamine and acetylcholine in the basal ganglia is disrupted, and motor control goes awry. They are most common with high-potency typical antipsychotics, though any agent with significant D2 antagonism can be the culprit.

What makes EPS especially important for clinical practice is that these symptoms unfold along a predictable timeline, from reactions that emerge within hours of the first dose to disorders that develop only after months or years of chronic treatment. Recognizing where a patient falls on that timeline is the key to accurate diagnosis and appropriate intervention. Let's walk through each of these in a bit more detail so the clinical picture comes alive. Acute dystonia is often the first EPS a patient encounters — and it can be genuinely frightening. A classic board-style vignette involves a young male patient (young men are at highest risk) who develops torticollis or oculogyric crisis within hours of starting a high-potency antipsychotic. The treatment is swift and gratifying: an intramuscular injection of benztropine or diphenhydramine typically resolves the crisis within minutes, restoring the dopamine-acetylcholine balance in the basal ganglia by blocking the acetylcholine side of the equation.

Akathisia, appearing within days to weeks, is more insidious. Patients describe an unbearable inner restlessness — a need to move, pace, or shift their weight — that can be so distressing it contributes to medication nonadherence or even suicidality. Crucially, akathisia is often mistaken for psychotic agitation, which can lead a clinician to increase the antipsychotic dose and inadvertently make the problem worse. This diagnostic trap is a favorite testing point on the EPPP. Unlike acute dystonia and parkinsonism, akathisia responds best not to anticholinergics but to beta-blockers like propranolol, benzodiazepines, or dose reduction of the offending agent.

Drug-induced parkinsonism (also called pseudoparkinsonism) develops over weeks to months and closely mimics idiopathic Parkinson's disease — bradykinesia, cogwheel rigidity, resting "pill-rolling" tremor, shuffling gait, and mask-like facies. The underlying mechanism is the same dopamine depletion in the nigrostriatal pathway. Anticholinergics such as benztropine or the dopamine-releasing agent amantadine are first-line treatments, along with dose reduction when clinically feasible.

Tardive dyskinesia (TD) is the most feared of all extrapyramidal symptoms because it typically appears only after months to years of antipsychotic use and may be irreversible even after the offending drug is stopped. The hallmark presentation involves involuntary, repetitive orofacial movements — lip smacking, tongue protrusion, lateral jaw movements, and grimacing — though the trunk and extremities can also be affected. The pathophysiology is thought to involve dopamine receptor supersensitivity that develops after prolonged blockade. In a paradox that the EPPP frequently tests, abruptly discontinuing the antipsychotic can temporarily unmask or worsen TD, because the supersensitive receptors are suddenly exposed to unblocked dopamine. Two VMAT2 (vesicular monoamine transporter 2) inhibitors — valbenazine (Ingrezza) and deutetrabenazine (Austedo) — are now FDA-approved specifically for the treatment of tardive dyskinesia, representing the first targeted pharmacotherapy for this condition.

EPPP Tip

Know the timeline: acute dystonia appears within hours to days, akathisia within days to weeks, parkinsonism within weeks to months, and tardive dyskinesia after months to years of use. TD is the most feared because it may be irreversible and paradoxically may worsen when the offending drug is discontinued. A helpful mnemonic for the order is "Days, Weeks, Months, Years" — the four EPS types march along this timeline in the same sequence they appear in the table. Also remember that akathisia is the one most commonly confused with worsening psychosis, and anticholinergics are not its first-line treatment — beta-blockers are.

Several patient characteristics increase vulnerability to tardive dyskinesia (TD). Older adults are at the greatest risk, particularly those treated with first-generation (conventional) antipsychotics over extended periods. Other established risk factors include female sex, African American ethnicity, the presence of a mood disorder (especially major depressive disorder), longer duration of antipsychotic exposure, and higher cumulative doses. The combination of advanced age and conventional antipsychotic use represents the highest-risk scenario for TD development. Because TD may be irreversible, clinicians should use the lowest effective dose and conduct regular monitoring using standardized tools such as the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS), with particular vigilance in elderly patients.

Clinical Note

When an exam question asks which patient is most likely to develop tardive dyskinesia, look for the combination of older age and long-term use of a conventional (typical) antipsychotic. This profile consistently represents the highest-risk group for this potentially permanent movement disorder.

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)

Having explored the extrapyramidal symptoms that can emerge with antipsychotic use — from acute dystonia to the potentially irreversible movements of tardive dyskinesia — we now turn to what is arguably the most dangerous adverse effect of these medications. Imagine a patient who was recently started on a high-potency typical antipsychotic like haloperidol. Over the course of several days, the nursing staff notices progressively worsening muscle rigidity, a climbing fever, confusion, and wildly fluctuating vital signs. By the time the on-call physician is paged, the patient's temperature has soared past 104°F, their muscles are rigid as iron, and laboratory results reveal dramatically elevated creatine phosphokinase (CPK) levels — a marker of severe muscle breakdown. This is not just a bad side effect. This is a medical emergency.

This clinical picture describes Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome, a rare but potentially fatal reaction to antipsychotic medications. Unlike the extrapyramidal symptoms discussed previously, which are uncomfortable and sometimes debilitating but generally not immediately life-threatening, NMS can kill. It demands rapid recognition and urgent intervention — making it one of the highest-stakes topics you'll encounter in psychopharmacology.

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS)

NMS is a rare but life-threatening medical emergency caused by antipsychotic medications (especially high-potency typicals, but can occur with any antipsychotic). Mortality rate is approximately 10-20% if untreated. The syndrome is thought to result from sudden, severe dopamine receptor blockade in the hypothalamus (disrupting thermoregulation) and in the basal ganglia (producing extreme rigidity). While most commonly associated with conventional antipsychotics, NMS has been reported with atypical antipsychotics and even with antiemetic medications that block dopamine receptors.

Clinical Features of NMS (mnemonic: FEVER)

Recognizing NMS quickly is essential because delays in treatment directly increase mortality risk. The mnemonic FEVER captures the cardinal features of this syndrome, and it's worth committing to memory — both for the exam and for clinical practice. Picture our patient from the opening scenario as you work through each feature:

  • Fever (hyperthermia, often >40C/104F)
  • Encephalopathy (altered mental status, confusion, delirium)
  • Vitals unstable (autonomic instability: tachycardia, labile blood pressure, diaphoresis)
  • Elevated enzymes (elevated creatine phosphokinase/CPK from muscle breakdown)
  • Rigidity (severe "lead pipe" muscle rigidity)

Notice how these features make physiological sense when you consider the mechanism: massive dopamine blockade in the hypothalamus disrupts the body's thermostat (fever), while blockade in the basal ganglia causes severe rigidity. The muscle rigidity is so intense that it leads to rhabdomyolysis — the breakdown of muscle tissue — which releases CPK and myoglobin into the bloodstream. The autonomic instability (unstable vitals) and encephalopathy (confusion) reflect widespread central nervous system disruption. The term "lead pipe rigidity" is particularly important to remember — it describes a constant, uniform resistance to passive movement, distinct from the "cogwheel rigidity" more commonly associated with Parkinson-like EPS.

Treatment of NMS

When NMS is suspected, treatment must begin immediately. Every hour of delay increases the risk of irreversible organ damage or death. The treatment approach addresses both the underlying cause — dopamine blockade — and the dangerous physiological consequences of the syndrome:

  • Immediate discontinuation of the antipsychotic
  • Supportive care (cooling, IV fluids, cardiac monitoring)
  • Dantrolene (muscle relaxant) for rigidity
  • Bromocriptine (dopamine agonist) to restore dopaminergic activity
  • Benzodiazepines may be used adjunctively

The logic of treatment mirrors the pathophysiology: because NMS results from excessive dopamine blockade, bromocriptine — a dopamine agonist — works to restore dopaminergic activity that the antipsychotic shut down. Meanwhile, dantrolene acts directly on skeletal muscle to reduce the dangerous rigidity and the muscle breakdown that follows from it. Think of it this way: bromocriptine addresses the central cause (dopamine blockade in the brain), while dantrolene addresses the most dangerous peripheral consequence (muscle rigidity and rhabdomyolysis). For exam purposes, remember both medications by name — they are frequently tested.

NMS vs. Serotonin Syndrome

Both involve hyperthermia and altered mental status, but NMS features lead-pipe rigidity and develops over days, while serotonin syndrome features hyperreflexia, clonus, and myoclonus and develops within hours. NMS is caused by dopamine blockade; serotonin syndrome by serotonin excess. This distinction is a classic exam question. A helpful way to remember: NMS is "slow and stiff" (developing over days with rigid muscles), while serotonin syndrome is "fast and twitchy" (developing within hours with jerky, hyperactive reflexes). Also consider the medication history — NMS follows antipsychotic use, while serotonin syndrome typically follows the initiation or dose increase of serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs) or the combination of multiple serotonergic agents.

Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics

Having explored the serious adverse effects of first-generation antipsychotics — from the acute dystonic reactions that can frighten patients in their first days of treatment, to the potentially irreversible movements of tardive dyskinesia, to the life-threatening emergency of neuroleptic malignant syndrome — it becomes clear why the field was eager for alternatives. The development of second-generation antipsychotics represented a genuine paradigm shift in psychopharmacology, one driven by a deceptively simple question: What if an antipsychotic could block dopamine effectively enough to reduce psychosis while simultaneously buffering the brain against the movement disorders and motivational blunting that plagued older medications?

Atypical antipsychotics answered that question through a dual receptor strategy: they block both dopamine D2 receptors and serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. This dual mechanism, known as serotonin-dopamine antagonism (SDA), is thought to reduce EPS risk and improve negative symptoms compared to typical antipsychotics. Here's the logic: serotonin normally inhibits dopamine release in certain brain pathways. By blocking 5-HT2A receptors, atypicals essentially "release the brake" on dopamine in the nigrostriatal pathway (reducing movement side effects) and in the mesocortical pathway (potentially improving negative symptoms like flat affect and social withdrawal). Meanwhile, they still block enough D2 activity in the mesolimbic pathway to manage positive symptoms like hallucinations and delusions. Think of it as a more targeted approach — dampening dopamine where it's overactive while preserving or even enhancing dopamine function where it's needed.

Clinically, this matters enormously. Consider a 24-year-old graduate student experiencing his first episode of psychosis — auditory hallucinations and paranoid delusions that have disrupted his studies and relationships. His psychiatrist faces a choice: a first-generation antipsychotic that may effectively suppress his hallucinations but leave him cognitively dulled and at risk for debilitating movement disorders, or an atypical antipsychotic that may address his psychosis while better preserving his cognitive functioning and motivation. For a young person hoping to return to graduate school, that difference can shape the entire trajectory of recovery. This is why atypical antipsychotics have become first-line treatments for most patients with schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders — not because they are free of side effects (they certainly are not), but because their side effect profile is, for many patients, more manageable.

Common Atypical Antipsychotics

While all atypical antipsychotics share the general principle of serotonin-dopamine antagonism, they are far from interchangeable. Each has a distinct receptor binding profile that translates into a unique constellation of benefits, side effects, and clinical niches. A few clinical stories bring these distinctions to life. Clozapine occupies a truly unique position: it is the only antipsychotic with robust evidence for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, typically defined as failure to respond adequately to at least two other antipsychotic trials. Imagine a patient who has been hospitalized repeatedly, tried haloperidol, risperidone, and olanzapine, and continues to hear commanding voices that make independent living impossible. Clozapine may be the medication that finally quiets those voices — and it is also the only antipsychotic proven to reduce suicidality in schizophrenia. But this remarkable efficacy comes with a serious catch: a 1-2% risk of agranulocytosis, a dangerous drop in white blood cells that can leave a patient fatally vulnerable to infection. This is why clozapine requires mandatory blood monitoring through a centralized registry — weekly complete blood counts for the first six months, biweekly for the next six months, and monthly thereafter. No blood draw, no medication. It's one of the most carefully regulated prescriptions in all of medicine.

Aripiprazole stands out for a completely different reason: its mechanism. Unlike every other antipsychotic — typical or atypical — aripiprazole is a partial dopamine agonist rather than a full antagonist. Think of a partial agonist as a "dopamine thermostat." In brain regions where dopamine is too high (the mesolimbic pathway in psychosis), it competes with dopamine at the receptor and reduces signaling — functioning more like a blocker. But in regions where dopamine is too low (the mesocortical pathway, contributing to negative symptoms), it provides just enough stimulation to partially restore function. This stabilizing quality means aripiprazole carries low risks for EPS, metabolic side effects, and sedation, making it an attractive first choice for many patients. Its most notable side effect is akathisia — that restless, driven-to-move feeling — which can be distressing enough to prompt discontinuation if not recognized and managed.

Risperidone behaves almost like a bridge between the typical and atypical worlds. At lower doses, it maintains a favorable atypical profile. But as the dose climbs, its D2 blockade intensifies, and it begins to look pharmacologically more like a conventional antipsychotic — with increased EPS risk and significant prolactin elevation. Elevated prolactin can cause galactorrhea (inappropriate breast milk production), menstrual irregularities, sexual dysfunction, and with chronic elevation, reduced bone density. For this reason, risperidone requires especially careful dose management, and clinicians should monitor for prolactin-related symptoms, particularly in younger patients and women of reproductive age.

Olanzapine is often described as one of the most effective atypicals for acute symptom control, but its reputation is shadowed by the most pronounced metabolic side effects in its class — substantial weight gain, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance that can progress to type 2 diabetes. A patient started on olanzapine might gain 15 to 30 pounds within the first year, fundamentally altering their cardiovascular risk profile. This metabolic burden, discussed in detail in the next section on metabolic syndrome, means that olanzapine requires vigilant monitoring of weight, fasting glucose, and lipid panels — and often prompts a difficult clinical conversation about balancing psychiatric stability against long-term physical health.

EPPP Tip

Key facts to know: Clozapine = treatment-resistant schizophrenia + agranulocytosis risk + mandatory blood monitoring. Olanzapine = worst metabolic side effects. Aripiprazole = unique partial D2 agonist mechanism. Risperidone = highest prolactin elevation among atypicals.

A helpful mnemonic for the EPPP: think of the atypicals as having traded one set of problems for another. First-generation antipsychotics were "movement medications" — effective but plagued by EPS and tardive dyskinesia. Second-generation antipsychotics shifted the burden toward "metabolic medications" — generally easier on the motor system but introducing concerns about weight, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk. Neither generation is categorically superior; rather, the clinical art lies in matching the right medication to the right patient, weighing the specific risks that matter most for that individual's health, functioning, and quality of life.

While agranulocytosis is most closely associated with clozapine, it is important to recognize the broader category of neutropenia — a reduction in neutrophil count that compromises the body's ability to fight bacterial infections. Agranulocytosis represents the most severe end of this spectrum, defined by an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below 500 cells/μL, which places the patient at critical risk for life-threatening infections. With clozapine, the incidence of agranulocytosis is approximately 1–2%, with the highest risk occurring during the first six months of treatment. Other second-generation antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, quetiapine) have been associated with neutropenia on rare occasions, though at rates far lower than clozapine. The mandatory blood monitoring program for clozapine — known as the Clozapine REMS (Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy) — requires regular ANC checks: weekly for the first six months, biweekly for the next six months, and monthly thereafter if counts remain stable. Treatment must be immediately discontinued if ANC falls below specified thresholds. This monitoring requirement is a defining feature of clozapine management and a frequently tested concept.

Metabolic Syndrome

Having explored the hematological dangers of clozapine — particularly the life-threatening risk of agranulocytosis — we now turn to a different but equally consequential class of adverse effects that extends well beyond a single medication. Imagine a patient who begins olanzapine for treatment-resistant psychosis. Within six months, her psychotic symptoms have improved remarkably, but she has gained 25 pounds, her fasting blood glucose has climbed into the prediabetic range, and her triglyceride levels have doubled. Her psychiatrist now faces a painful clinical dilemma: the medication that rescued her mental health is simultaneously eroding her physical health. This scenario plays out routinely in clinical practice and illustrates why understanding metabolic syndrome is not a peripheral concern — it is central to responsible prescribing and long-term patient care.

Second-generation antipsychotics (especially clozapine and olanzapine) carry significant risk of metabolic syndrome, a cluster of interrelated metabolic disturbances that, when they co-occur, dramatically increase a patient's risk for cardiovascular disease, stroke, and premature death. The syndrome is not a single disease but rather a constellation of physiological changes that tend to travel together, each amplifying the danger of the others. Specifically, metabolic syndrome includes:

  • Weight gain (often 10-30+ pounds)
  • Dyslipidemia (elevated triglycerides, decreased HDL)
  • Hyperglycemia / Type 2 diabetes
  • Hypertension

The mechanisms behind these metabolic effects are complex and not fully understood, but they appear to involve the medications' actions on histamine H1 receptors and serotonin 5-HT2C receptors — both of which play roles in appetite regulation and energy metabolism. Clozapine and olanzapine have the strongest affinity for these receptors, which helps explain why they sit at the top of the metabolic risk hierarchy. The clinical significance cannot be overstated: individuals with serious mental illness already have a life expectancy 15 to 25 years shorter than the general population, and cardiovascular disease — fueled in part by antipsychotic-induced metabolic changes — is the leading cause of that mortality gap.

Because of these risks, metabolic monitoring (fasting glucose, lipid panel, weight, waist circumference, blood pressure) is recommended at baseline and at regular intervals for all patients on atypical antipsychotics. Guidelines from the American Diabetes Association and the American Psychiatric Association call for reassessing weight at every visit during the first several months, checking fasting glucose and lipids at 12 weeks, and then continuing surveillance at least annually thereafter. In practice, psychologists who work collaboratively with prescribers play an important role in ensuring that patients understand the importance of these monitoring protocols and in encouraging behavioral strategies — such as dietary modifications and physical activity programs — that can mitigate metabolic risk without requiring a medication change.

Metabolic Risk by Medication (High to Low)

Not all second-generation antipsychotics carry the same metabolic burden. Understanding the relative risk across medications is clinically essential because it directly informs treatment selection, especially for patients who already have risk factors such as obesity, family history of diabetes, or existing dyslipidemia. The hierarchy below is well established in the research literature and frequently tested on licensing examinations:

  1. Clozapine and Olanzapine (highest risk)
  2. Quetiapine and Risperidone (moderate risk)
  3. Aripiprazole, Ziprasidone, and Lurasidone (lowest risk)

A useful clinical mnemonic is to think of clozapine and olanzapine as the "metabolic heavyweights" — powerful in efficacy but equally potent in their metabolic toll. At the other end of the spectrum, aripiprazole, ziprasidone, and lurasidone are sometimes described as "metabolically friendly," though this label is relative rather than absolute; even these agents warrant baseline and ongoing monitoring. Ziprasidone, for example, carries its own unique concern — QTc prolongation — reminding clinicians that selecting a lower-metabolic-risk agent does not eliminate the need for vigilant safety monitoring. The clinical art lies in balancing symptom control against the full profile of side-effect risks for each individual patient, a calculation that shifts with every new piece of medical history.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Typical antipsychotics work via D2 blockade; atypicals combine D2 and 5-HT2A blockade.
  • High-potency typicals (haloperidol) = high EPS risk, low sedation. Low-potency typicals (chlorpromazine) = low EPS risk, high sedation.
  • EPS timeline: acute dystonia (hours-days), akathisia (days-weeks), parkinsonism (weeks-months), tardive dyskinesia (months-years).
  • NMS is a medical emergency: hyperthermia, lead-pipe rigidity, autonomic instability, elevated CPK. Treat with dantrolene and bromocriptine.
  • Clozapine is the gold standard for treatment-resistant schizophrenia but requires blood monitoring for agranulocytosis.
  • Olanzapine has the highest metabolic risk among commonly used atypicals.
  • Aripiprazole is unique as a partial D2 agonist.
  • Metabolic monitoring is recommended for all patients on atypical antipsychotics.
  • Tardive dyskinesia may be irreversible and can worsen when the offending drug is stopped.
