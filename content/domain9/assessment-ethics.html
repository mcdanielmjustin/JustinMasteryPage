Assessment (Std 9)

Test use, informed consent, test security, interpretation
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Overview of Standard 9: Assessment

Standard 9 of the APA Ethics Code governs the ethical conduct of psychological assessment. This is one of the most heavily tested standards on the EPPP because assessment is a core professional activity that directly affects clients' lives -- from clinical diagnoses to forensic evaluations, educational placements, and employment decisions.

The standard establishes requirements for the proper use, administration, interpretation, and security of psychological tests and assessment instruments. Psychologists must ensure that their assessments are scientifically grounded, culturally appropriate, and conducted with appropriate informed consent.

Assessment vs. Testing

Psychological assessment is a comprehensive process that integrates information from multiple sources (tests, interviews, observations, records) to answer referral questions. Psychological testing refers specifically to the administration and scoring of standardized instruments. Assessment is the broader, clinical process; testing is one component of assessment.

Standard 9.01: Bases for Assessments

Psychologists base the opinions contained in their recommendations, reports, and diagnostic or evaluative statements, including forensic testimony, on information and techniques sufficient to substantiate their findings.

9.01(a) -- Sufficient Information

Psychologists provide opinions only after they have conducted an examination of the individual adequate to support their statements or conclusions. When a personal examination is not practical, psychologists document the efforts they made and the results of those efforts, clarify the probable impact of their limited information on the reliability and validity of their opinions, and appropriately limit the nature and extent of their conclusions or recommendations.

EPPP Tip

A frequent EPPP scenario: A psychologist is asked to provide a diagnosis or custody recommendation based solely on record review (without examining the individual). Standard 9.01 does not prohibit this, but requires the psychologist to clearly state the limitations and restrict conclusions accordingly. When a personal examination is not practical, the psychologist must document this and qualify their opinions.

9.01(b) -- Limitations When Personal Examination Is Not Possible

Except as noted in 9.01(c), psychologists provide opinions of the psychological characteristics of individuals only after they have conducted an examination adequate to support their statements. When, despite reasonable efforts, such an examination is not practical, psychologists document their efforts and the impact on conclusions.

9.01(c) -- Record Review Without Examination

When psychologists conduct a record review or provide consultation or supervision and an individual examination is not warranted or necessary, they explain this and the sources of information on which they based their conclusions and recommendations.

Standard 9.02: Use of Assessments

Psychologists administer, adapt, score, interpret, or use assessment techniques, interviews, tests, or instruments in a manner and for purposes that are appropriate in light of the research on or evidence of the usefulness and proper application of the techniques.

9.02(a) -- Appropriate Use

Psychologists use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for use with members of the population tested. When such validity or reliability has not been established, psychologists describe the strengths and limitations of test results and interpretation.

9.02(b) -- Outdated Norms and Cultural Factors

Psychologists use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for the specific purpose and population. When assessing individuals from populations not represented in the normative sample, psychologists must consider cultural, linguistic, and individual factors that may affect interpretation and clearly note these limitations in reports.

Example

A psychologist administers an intelligence test normed on English-speaking Americans to a recent immigrant whose first language is Spanish. The psychologist must note the cultural and linguistic limitations of the assessment, consider using instruments validated for Spanish speakers, and avoid drawing definitive conclusions about intellectual ability based on the potentially biased results.

Standard 9.03: Informed Consent in Assessments

Imagine you're a psychologist about to begin a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation with a new client. Before you open the first test booklet, there's a critical step that must occur — one that shapes the entire ethical foundation of the assessment process. The client needs to understand what's about to happen, why it's happening, who will see the results, and what the limits of privacy are. This is the essence of informed consent in assessments, and it's far more than a form to be signed. It represents the client's right to make a knowing, voluntary decision about participating in a process that could profoundly affect their life — from diagnostic labels to custody decisions to disability determinations.

Having just discussed the ethical use of assessments under Standard 9.02 — including the obligation to use instruments appropriate for the individual being tested — we now turn to what must happen before any assessment begins. Standard 9.03 establishes the requirement that psychologists obtain informed consent for assessments, evaluations, or diagnostic services. However, the Ethics Code recognizes that certain contexts make traditional informed consent impractical or unnecessary. Psychologists are not required to obtain informed consent in the following circumstances:

  1. Testing is mandated by law or governmental regulations
  2. Informed consent is implied because testing is conducted as a routine educational, institutional, or organizational activity (e.g., admission testing)
  3. The purpose of the testing is to evaluate decisional capacity

The third exception is particularly worth noting: if a psychologist has been asked to determine whether a person can make competent decisions — for example, an elderly patient whose family is concerned about dementia — requiring that person to provide truly informed consent before the evaluation would create a logical paradox. You'd be asking someone to demonstrate the very capacity you've been asked to assess. Similarly, when a court orders a forensic evaluation or when students routinely take placement tests at school, the traditional consent process is either legally superseded or contextually implied. Even so, as you'll see below, notification and explanation are never entirely waived.

9.03(a) -- Explaining the Purpose

When informed consent is required, it must be more than a vague assurance that "we'll be doing some testing today." Consider a parent bringing their child in for a psychoeducational evaluation. Ethical practice demands that the psychologist explain, in language the parent can readily understand, what types of tests will be administered, what they're designed to measure, how long the process will take, and what will happen with the results. Informed consent includes an explanation of the nature and purpose of the assessment, fees, involvement of third parties, and limits of confidentiality in a language reasonably understandable to the person being assessed or their legal representative. For instance, if the evaluation has been requested by the child's school district, the parent needs to know that a report will be shared with school personnel — this is the kind of third-party involvement that must be disclosed upfront. The key phrase here is "reasonably understandable": a psychologist working with a client who has limited education shouldn't rely on jargon-heavy consent forms but should instead use clear, plain language and check for genuine comprehension.

9.03(b) -- Individuals Lacking Capacity

Some of the most ethically delicate assessment situations arise when the person being evaluated cannot provide meaningful consent on their own — a young child, an adult with significant intellectual disability, or a patient with advanced Alzheimer's disease. In these cases, the psychologist's obligations don't simply vanish; they become layered. For persons who lack the capacity to consent (e.g., due to cognitive impairment), psychologists provide appropriate explanation at a level the person can understand, seek the person's assent even when formal consent isn't possible, consider the person's preferences and best interests, and obtain consent from a legally authorized person such as a guardian or parent. This standard reflects a deeply humanistic principle: even when someone cannot legally consent, their dignity and autonomy still matter. A psychologist evaluating a teenager with moderate intellectual disability should still explain, in simple terms, what the testing involves and why it's being done — and should pay attention to whether the teen seems willing or reluctant to participate. Obtaining a parent's signature on a consent form does not absolve the psychologist from engaging respectfully with the individual being assessed.

9.03(c) -- Court-Ordered or Mandated Assessment

Forensic and legal contexts create a unique tension in the consent process. When a judge orders a defendant to undergo a psychological evaluation, that individual cannot simply decline. Yet the absence of choice doesn't eliminate the psychologist's ethical responsibilities. When services are court ordered or otherwise mandated, psychologists inform the individual of the nature of the anticipated services, including whether the services are court ordered or mandated and any limits of confidentiality, before proceeding. Picture a forensic psychologist meeting with a parent involved in a custody dispute. Even though this parent must submit to the evaluation, the psychologist should clearly explain: "This evaluation was ordered by the court. I'll be assessing your parenting capacity and psychological functioning. My report will be submitted to the judge and will be available to both attorneys. I am not acting as your therapist, and what you tell me is not confidential in the way therapy would be." This kind of transparent notification protects the individual from the mistaken belief that they're in a therapeutic relationship and ensures they understand the evaluative — not supportive — nature of the interaction.

EPPP Tip

Court-ordered assessments still require notification -- the psychologist must tell the individual about the nature of services and limits of confidentiality, even though the person cannot refuse the assessment. Informed consent is modified but not eliminated in mandated evaluations. On the EPPP, watch for answer choices that suggest mandated evaluations require no communication with the examinee at all — that's incorrect. The obligation shifts from obtaining consent to providing clear, upfront notification. Think of it this way: the person loses the right to say no, but they never lose the right to know what's happening and why.

Standard 9.04: Release of Test Data

Having just explored informed consent in assessments — including the special considerations for court-ordered evaluations — we now turn to one of the most frequently tested and practically confusing areas of the APA Ethics Code: what happens after the assessment is complete and someone wants to see the results. Imagine you've just finished a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation. The client's attorney calls requesting "everything from the testing." Do you send the scored protocols? The raw data sheets? The test booklet itself? Your clinical notes? The answer depends on a critical distinction that every practicing psychologist must master — and one the EPPP tests repeatedly.

The key lies in understanding what qualifies as test data versus what constitutes test materials. These two categories look similar on the surface, but the ethical obligations surrounding each are quite different. Test data refers to raw and scaled scores, client responses to test questions or stimuli, and psychologists' notes and recordings concerning client statements and behavior during an examination. Think of test data as everything that is uniquely generated by or about the client during the assessment process. Test materials, by contrast, are the instruments themselves — manuals, protocols, test questions or stimuli, and scoring keys. These belong to the test, not to the client, and their release is governed by separate ethical obligations related to test security (covered under Standard 9.11). When that attorney calls asking for "everything," you need to know which pile each document falls into.

9.04(a) -- Client Request

When a client (or their legal representative) signs a release authorizing the disclosure of test data, the general rule is straightforward: psychologists provide the requested test data to the client or to other persons identified in the release. This aligns with the broader ethical principle of respecting client autonomy — after all, the data were generated about them and in many ways belong to their clinical record. However, the standard wisely builds in an important exception. Psychologists may refrain from releasing test data to protect a client or others from substantial harm or misuse or misrepresentation of the data or the test, recognizing that in many instances release of confidential information under these circumstances is regulated by law.

Consider a clinical example: you've conducted a custody evaluation, and one parent demands the raw data, stating they intend to post the other parent's responses on social media. Here, releasing the data could cause substantial harm — both to the other parent and potentially to the integrity of the assessment process. In such a scenario, you could ethically withhold the data despite having a signed release. But notice the careful language of the standard: this is a "may" provision, not a "shall." It gives you discretion, not a mandate. And critically, you must remain aware that applicable laws — such as HIPAA, which generally treats test data as part of the medical record — may override your ethical judgment and require release regardless. This intersection of ethics and law is a favorite EPPP testing point.

9.04(b) -- Absent Client Release

What happens when there is no signed client release? Perhaps the client refuses to authorize disclosure, or perhaps the request comes from a third party with no release on file. In the absence of a client release, psychologists provide test data only as required by law or court order. This is a firm boundary: without legal compulsion, you do not release test data to anyone the client hasn't authorized. A requesting attorney, an insurance company, or even another psychologist has no right to the data without either client consent or a legal mandate. This provision underscores the fundamental principle that test data, because they contain intimate details of a client's psychological functioning, deserve robust confidentiality protections.

For example, if a forensic psychologist receives a subpoena (not a court order) for test data and the client has not signed a release, the psychologist should not automatically comply. A subpoena is issued by an attorney, not a judge, and does not carry the same legal force as a court order. The appropriate response is typically to notify the client, seek legal guidance, and — if necessary — assert the privilege on the client's behalf until a judge rules on the matter. Understanding the difference between a subpoena and a court order in the context of releasing test data is essential both for ethical practice and for EPPP success.

Test Data vs. Test Materials

Test data (9.04) = the client's responses, scores, and behavioral observations -- generally released to the client upon request. Test materials (9.11) = the test itself (questions, stimuli, manuals, scoring keys) -- protected from release to maintain test security. A helpful mnemonic: test data are about the person; test materials are about the test. If a document would exist regardless of which client sat down — such as a test manual or a blank answer sheet — it is a test material. If it was created because this particular client participated — such as their scores, their specific answers, or your behavioral observations — it is test data. This distinction is frequently tested on the EPPP.

Standard 9.05: Test Construction

Having explored the critical distinction between test data and test materials — and the responsibilities psychologists carry when releasing assessment information — we now turn to where every assessment instrument begins: its construction. Before a test can generate data worth protecting, before scores can be interpreted or released, someone must build the test itself. And Standard 9.05 establishes that this process is far from casual — it demands rigorous scientific discipline at every stage.

Consider a psychologist developing a new screening measure for postpartum depression tailored to a specific cultural community. She cannot simply compile a list of questions that seem relevant, administer them, and call the result a validated instrument. Instead, she must carefully evaluate whether her items capture the construct she intends to measure, whether the instrument produces consistent scores across administrations, whether it performs equitably across demographic groups, and whether adequate norms exist to guide interpretation. The stakes are enormous: a poorly constructed screening tool might miss mothers in genuine crisis or, conversely, pathologize normal cultural expressions of distress.

Standard 9.05 addresses exactly this responsibility: psychologists who develop tests and other assessment techniques use appropriate psychometric procedures and current scientific or professional knowledge for test design, standardization, validation, reduction or elimination of bias, and recommendations for use. In other words, the Ethics Code holds test developers to the same empirical rigor that the field demands in any scientific endeavor — because an assessment instrument is, at its core, a measurement tool, and measurement tools must be precise, fair, and well-calibrated.

This standard applies specifically to psychologists who create new assessment instruments, and it requires that such instruments meet accepted standards for reliability, validity, and fairness. Reliability means the instrument yields consistent, reproducible results; validity means it actually measures what it claims to measure; and fairness means the instrument does not systematically disadvantage particular groups due to cultural, linguistic, or demographic bias. A test that is reliable but biased — or valid for one population but untested in another — falls short of the standard's requirements.

For the EPPP, remember that Standard 9.05 is specifically about the construction side of assessment. It complements the standards that govern test use, interpretation, and data release by ensuring that the foundation — the instrument itself — is psychometrically sound. Think of it this way: Standards 9.02 through 9.04 govern how clinicians wield assessment tools responsibly, while 9.05 governs how developers forge those tools in the first place. A brilliant clinician cannot compensate for a fundamentally flawed instrument, which is why the Ethics Code addresses both sides of the equation.

Standard 9.06: Interpreting Assessment Results

When interpreting assessment results, including automated interpretations, psychologists take into account the purpose of the assessment as well as the various test factors, test-taking abilities, and other characteristics of the person being assessed, such as situational, personal, linguistic, and cultural differences, that might affect psychologists' judgments or reduce the accuracy of their interpretations. They indicate any significant limitations of their interpretations.

Example

A psychologist administering a neuropsychological battery to a client who recently suffered a concussion notes that the client was experiencing headaches and fatigue during testing. The psychologist must note these factors in the report as they may have attenuated test performance, potentially leading to an overestimation of cognitive impairment.

Standard 9.07: Assessment by Unqualified Persons

Psychologists do not promote the use of psychological assessment techniques by unqualified persons, except when such use is conducted for training purposes with appropriate supervision. When psychologists delegate scoring and interpretation to others (e.g., technicians, computer scoring services), they retain responsibility for the appropriate application, interpretation, and use of assessment instruments.

EPPP Tip

A psychometrist or testing technician may administer and score tests under supervision, but the psychologist retains full responsibility for interpretation and for ensuring the delegate is properly trained. This connects to Standard 2.05 (Delegation of Work to Others).

Standard 9.08: Obsolete Tests and Outdated Test Results

9.08(a) -- Obsolete Tests

Psychologists do not base their assessment or intervention decisions or recommendations on data or test results that are outdated for the current purpose.

9.08(b) -- Outdated Instruments

Psychologists do not base decisions or recommendations on tests and measures that are obsolete and not useful for the current purpose.

Example

A psychologist continuing to use the WISC-III (published 1991) for a cognitive evaluation when the WISC-V (published 2014) is available would potentially violate Standard 9.08, as the older version has outdated norms that may not accurately represent current population characteristics. There may be exceptions (e.g., research comparing results across versions), but for clinical purposes the most current edition should be used.

Standard 9.09: Test Scoring and Interpretation Services

Psychologists who offer assessment or scoring services to other professionals accurately describe the purpose, norms, validity, reliability, and applications of the procedures and any special qualifications applicable to their use. Psychologists select scoring and interpretation services (including automated services) on the basis of evidence of the validity of the program and procedures as well as on other appropriate considerations.

Computer-Based Test Interpretation (CBTI)

Psychologists who use computer-based test interpretation services must understand the limitations of such services. Automated interpretations are probabilistic statements based on group data and may not apply to a specific individual. The psychologist, not the computer program, is responsible for the final clinical interpretation.

Standard 9.10: Explaining Assessment Results

Imagine a client who has just completed a lengthy neuropsychological battery — three hours of testing that felt exhausting and, at times, anxiety-provoking. The testing is finished, the scores are computed, and now the client sits across from you and asks the most natural question in the world: "So, what did you find?" How you handle that moment — whether you provide a clear, compassionate explanation of what the results mean — is not merely good clinical practice. It is an ethical obligation codified in Standard 9.10 of the APA Ethics Code.

Having just explored the responsibilities psychologists carry when using scoring and interpretation services (Standard 9.09), we now turn to what happens after the numbers are generated: communicating those results to the people they belong to. No matter how sophisticated our instruments or how accurate our computer-generated interpretations, the assessment process remains incomplete until the person who was assessed understands what the findings mean for them.

Standard 9.10 establishes a clear default expectation: psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure that explanations of results are provided to the individual being assessed or to a designated representative. This obligation holds regardless of who actually scored and interpreted the test — whether the psychologist did it personally, delegated it to a trained employee or assistant, or used an automated or outside scoring service. The psychologist retains responsibility for ensuring that a meaningful explanation reaches the client.

Consider a school psychologist who administers a cognitive and achievement battery to a 9-year-old. The child's parents are the designated representatives, and Standard 9.10 requires the psychologist to take reasonable steps to explain the results to them in language they can understand — not simply mail home a printout of standard scores. Similarly, when a clinical psychologist administers a personality assessment to an adult therapy client, the psychologist should provide feedback that helps the client understand the findings and how they connect to treatment planning. This feedback session is often one of the most therapeutically powerful moments in the entire assessment process.

However, the standard recognizes that certain professional contexts create legitimate exceptions to this default rule. In some organizational consulting arrangements, preemployment or security screenings, and forensic evaluations, the nature of the professional relationship may preclude sharing results directly with the person assessed. A forensic psychologist conducting a competency-to-stand-trial evaluation for the court, for example, may not be in a position to provide a detailed explanation of findings to the defendant. A psychologist screening applicants for a law enforcement agency may report results only to the hiring authority. These are legitimate exceptions — but they come with a critical caveat: the person being assessed must be clearly informed in advance that they will not receive an explanation of results. This advance notification transforms what might otherwise feel like a baffling or unfair withholding of information into a transparent, ethically grounded boundary.

EPPP Tip

The default rule is that assessment results should be explained to the individual. Exceptions exist for organizational, forensic, and security contexts — but only when the person was informed in advance that results would not be shared with them. The EPPP tests whether you understand both the rule and its exceptions. When facing exam questions on this standard, look for answer choices that emphasize the two-part structure: (1) the general obligation to explain results, and (2) the requirement for prior notification when that obligation will not apply. A wrong answer on the exam often presents a scenario where the psychologist withholds results without having provided advance notice — that is the ethical violation the standard is designed to prevent.

Standard 9.11: Maintaining Test Security

Imagine a scenario: a client's attorney demands a complete copy of a neuropsychological test battery — not just the scores and responses, but the actual test booklets, stimulus cards, and scoring keys. If you hand everything over, the test is effectively compromised for future use — any future examinee who gains access to those materials could prepare responses in advance, rendering the instrument useless. But if you refuse entirely, you may appear to be withholding information the client has a right to see. This tension — between protecting the instruments that make valid assessment possible and honoring the rights of the people we assess — is exactly what Standard 9.11 addresses.

The term test materials refers to manuals, instruments, protocols, and test questions or stimuli — essentially, the proprietary components that make a test function as a standardized measurement tool. These materials often represent decades of research, careful norming, and significant financial investment by test publishers. Once compromised, a test may lose its validity entirely, which harms not just the publisher but every future client who might have benefited from an accurate assessment. Under Standard 9.11, psychologists are required to make reasonable efforts to maintain the integrity and security of test materials and other assessment techniques, doing so in a manner consistent with law, contractual obligations, and the broader Ethics Code.

Note the language carefully: the standard says "reasonable efforts," not absolute protection at all costs. This phrasing acknowledges that psychologists operate within legal systems that may, at times, compel disclosure. A court order, for instance, might override a psychologist's preference to keep materials secure. But even in such cases, the psychologist should advocate for protective measures — such as having materials reviewed only by a qualified professional rather than entered into open court records — to minimize unnecessary exposure.

Balancing Security and Client Rights

This is where the EPPP loves to test your understanding of a distinction introduced earlier in the Ethics Code (Standard 9.04): the difference between test data and test materials. These two categories may seem straightforward in theory, but in clinical practice they overlap in ways that demand careful professional judgment. Test security concerns must be balanced against clients' legitimate rights to access their own data, and getting this balance wrong — in either direction — can create serious ethical and legal problems.

Think of it this way: a client who completed an IQ assessment has a right to know their scores, to understand how they performed, and to obtain their responses if they request them. That information belongs in the domain of test data — it is about the client. But the test manual that explains how to administer and score the WAIS, the scoring algorithms, and the actual test questions themselves? Those are test materials — they belong to the instrument, not to the individual examinee. Releasing them would jeopardize the test's utility for everyone. Here is how the distinction plays out in practice:

  • A client may receive their scores and responses (test data)
  • A client should not receive the test manual, scoring key, or stimulus materials (test materials)
  • When test data are intertwined with test materials (e.g., a response booklet that includes test questions), the psychologist must use professional judgment to balance client rights with test security

That third bullet point is the one that generates the most exam questions — and the most real-world headaches. Consider a projective test response form where the client's answers are written directly on pages that also contain the test stimuli. You cannot release one without the other. In such cases, the psychologist might redact the stimulus portions, provide a summary of responses rather than the raw form, or release materials only to another qualified professional who can interpret them appropriately. The key principle the EPPP expects you to grasp is that neither test security nor client access is an absolute trump card; rather, psychologists must navigate the tension thoughtfully, document their reasoning, and aim for solutions that honor both values to the greatest extent possible.

Key Takeaways for the EPPP

  • Psychologists base assessment opinions on sufficient information and appropriate techniques (9.01). When personal examination is not possible, they must document limitations and qualify their conclusions.
  • Assessment instruments must be valid and reliable for the specific population and purpose (9.02). Cultural, linguistic, and individual factors must be considered.
  • Informed consent is required for assessments except when mandated by law, implied by context, or evaluating decisional capacity (9.03).
  • Test data (scores, responses, observations) can be released to clients upon request; test materials (manuals, questions, stimuli) are protected for test security (9.04 vs. 9.11).
  • Psychologists do not use obsolete tests or outdated test results for current purposes (9.08).
  • Computer-based interpretations are tools, not substitutes for clinical judgment; the psychologist retains responsibility for the final interpretation (9.09).
  • Assessment results should be explained to the individual unless the person was informed in advance that results would not be shared (9.10).
  • Psychologists retain full responsibility for assessment even when administration or scoring is delegated to others (9.07).
