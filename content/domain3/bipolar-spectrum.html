Bipolar I and Bipolar II Disorders

Bipolar Spectrum: Diagnostic Hierarchy
Bipolar Spectrum

Bipolar II
At least 1 hypomanic (4+ days) + 1 major depressive episode; NO full mania — if mania occurs, upgrades to Bipolar I
Cyclothymic Disorder
2 years (1 year in youth) of subthreshold hypo/depressive symptoms; no episode meets full criteria; 15-50% risk of progressing to Bipolar I or II

Symptom-free intervals cannot exceed 2 consecutive months

Genetics and Heritability of Bipolar Disorder

Bipolar disorder is among the most heritable of all psychiatric conditions. Research using twin methodology has consistently demonstrated a strong genetic contribution, with heritability estimates approximating 90% according to the DSM-5-TR. Monozygotic (identical) twin concordance rates for bipolar disorder are substantially higher than dizygotic (fraternal) twin concordance rates, supporting the conclusion that genetic factors play a predominant role in disease liability. First-degree biological relatives of individuals with bipolar I or bipolar II disorder carry a significantly elevated risk — approximately 10 times greater than the general population — of developing the disorder themselves.

Despite this strong heritability, bipolar disorder does not follow a simple Mendelian inheritance pattern. Instead, it is considered a polygenic condition in which multiple genes of small effect interact with one another and with environmental factors. Stressful life events, disrupted circadian rhythms, and substance use can serve as precipitating triggers in genetically vulnerable individuals. The high heritability estimate also has clinical implications: thorough family psychiatric history is essential when evaluating patients who present with mood symptoms, particularly when distinguishing between unipolar depression and an initial depressive episode that may herald bipolar disorder.

Example

A 22-year-old presents with a severe depressive episode and no prior manic or hypomanic history. Her mother has Bipolar I disorder and her maternal uncle was also diagnosed with bipolar disorder. Given the approximately 90% heritability and elevated familial risk, the clinician should carefully screen for any subthreshold hypomanic symptoms and monitor closely for mood elevation, particularly if antidepressant medication is initiated.

Bipolar I Disorder

Bipolar I disorder requires the occurrence of at least one manic episode. The manic episode may have been preceded by or followed by hypomanic or major depressive episodes, but these are not required for diagnosis. During a manic episode, at least three of the following symptoms are present (four if mood is only irritable):

  1. Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity
  2. Decreased need for sleep (e.g., feels rested after 3 hours)
  3. More talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking
  4. Flight of ideas or subjective experience of racing thoughts
  5. Distractibility
  6. Increase in goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
  7. Excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences

The mnemonic DIG FAST (Distractibility, Indiscretion/Impulsivity, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness) helps recall mania criteria.

Bipolar II Disorder

Bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode. Bipolar II is not a "milder" form of Bipolar I -- the depressive episodes are often more chronic and debilitating. If a full manic episode ever occurs, the diagnosis changes to Bipolar I.

Clinical Pearl

Bipolar II is frequently misdiagnosed as MDD because patients typically present during depressive episodes and may not recognize hypomania as pathological. Screening for a history of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, and increased activity is essential. Antidepressant monotherapy in unrecognized Bipolar II can precipitate a manic switch.

Cyclothymic Disorder

Having explored Bipolar I and Bipolar II — conditions defined by full-blown manic, hypomanic, and major depressive episodes — we now turn to a mood disorder that lives in a subtler but no less disruptive space. Imagine a client who describes her emotional life as a perpetual roller coaster that never quite reaches the dramatic peaks or plunging drops of a classic bipolar presentation. Some weeks she feels energized and confident, taking on extra projects and socializing late into the night with little need for sleep. Then, seemingly without warning, she slides into days of low motivation, self-doubt, and fatigue — not severe enough to keep her in bed, but enough to make her cancel plans and fall behind at work. She has felt this way, off and on, for years. She has never experienced a full manic episode or a major depressive episode, yet the relentless oscillation has eroded her relationships, her career, and her sense of self. This pattern captures the essence of cyclothymic disorder.

Cyclothymic disorder involves a chronic, fluctuating mood disturbance characterized by numerous periods of hypomanic symptoms alternating with periods of depressive symptoms — none of which meet the full diagnostic criteria for either a hypomanic episode or a major depressive episode. To qualify for the diagnosis, this pattern must persist for at least 2 years in adults (or 1 year in children and adolescents), and during that window the individual must not have been symptom-free for more than 2 consecutive months at a time. The word "chronic" is critical here: cyclothymia is not a brief mood fluctuation but a persistent temperamental instability that colors nearly every aspect of daily functioning.

One of the most clinically important features of cyclothymic disorder is its longitudinal risk. Research indicates there is a 15–50% risk of eventually developing Bipolar I or Bipolar II disorder. This makes cyclothymia more than a standalone diagnosis — it can function as a prodromal or precursor condition, much like how persistent subthreshold symptoms in other domains can foreshadow full syndromal illness. Because of this elevated conversion risk, clinicians should monitor individuals with cyclothymia carefully over time, watching for escalation of hypomanic symptoms into full hypomania or mania, or deepening of depressive periods into major depressive episodes.

Cyclothymic disorder is frequently underdiagnosed for several reasons. First, the symptoms never reach the dramatic intensity that typically drives a person to seek treatment. Clients often describe themselves as "moody" or attribute their emotional variability to personality rather than a mood disorder. Second, because the depressive periods fall short of major depression, clinicians who are not specifically screening for mood cycling may overlook the pattern entirely. Third, the chronic nature of cyclothymia means many individuals have never known a stable emotional baseline — they may genuinely not realize that the constant ups and downs are pathological. In clinical practice, a careful timeline of mood episodes, constructed collaboratively with the client and ideally corroborated by family members, can be invaluable in uncovering the cyclothymic pattern that might otherwise remain hidden beneath vague complaints of emotional instability.

For the EPPP, remember the key distinguishing features: cyclothymic disorder requires at least 2 years of mood cycling (1 year for children and adolescents), symptom-free intervals cannot exceed 2 months, episodes never meet full criteria for hypomania or major depression, and the condition carries a significant risk of progression to Bipolar I or II. This last point — the conversion risk — is a frequently tested concept and underscores why cyclothymia belongs firmly within the bipolar spectrum rather than being dismissed as mere moodiness.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD)

Where cyclothymia involves unpredictable mood cycling, PMDD is tethered to a precise biological rhythm — the menstrual cycle. For some women, the week before menses brings emotional upheaval severe enough to impair functioning, then lifts once menstruation begins.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) is classified as a depressive disorder in the DSM-5. It involves the presence of 5 or more symptoms in the final week before the onset of menses, with symptoms beginning to improve within a few days after the onset of menses and becoming minimal or absent in the week post-menses.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

At least one of the following core affective symptoms must be present:

  1. Marked affective lability (e.g., mood swings, feeling suddenly sad or tearful, increased sensitivity to rejection)
  2. Marked irritability or anger or increased interpersonal conflicts
  3. Marked depressed mood, feelings of hopelessness, or self-deprecating thoughts
  4. Marked anxiety, tension, and/or feelings of being keyed up or on edge

Plus one or more of the following additional symptoms (to reach a total of at least 5):

  • Decreased interest in usual activities
  • Subjective difficulty in concentration
  • Lethargy, easy fatigability, or marked lack of energy
  • Marked change in appetite, overeating, or specific food cravings
  • Hypersomnia or insomnia
  • A sense of being overwhelmed or out of control
  • Physical symptoms such as breast tenderness or swelling, joint or muscle pain, a sensation of bloating, or weight gain

Clinical Pearl

PMDD diagnosis must be confirmed by prospective daily ratings for at least 2 consecutive symptomatic menstrual cycles. Retrospective self-report alone is insufficient because women often overestimate premenstrual symptom severity when recalling past cycles. This prospective confirmation requirement is unique among DSM-5 diagnoses.

Distinction from PMS

Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) is a common, less severe condition that affects up to 75% of menstruating women. PMDD is distinct from PMS in that PMDD causes significant functional impairment in social, occupational, or interpersonal domains, whereas PMS involves milder symptoms that do not substantially interfere with daily functioning. PMDD prevalence is estimated at 1.8-5.8% of menstruating women.

EPPP Example

PMDD is a new addition to the depressive disorders chapter in DSM-5. The key features to remember are: (1) symptoms must occur in the final week before menses and remit after onset; (2) at least one of four core affective symptoms is required; (3) prospective daily ratings over 2 cycles are required for confirmation.

Etiological Models of Depression

Lewinsohn's behavioral model of depression (1974) offers an operant conditioning perspective. According to Lewinsohn, depression develops when an individual experiences a low rate of response-contingent positive reinforcement. When a person's behaviors no longer produce rewarding outcomes — whether due to environmental changes (e.g., loss of a relationship, job loss), skill deficits in eliciting reinforcement from others, or a reduced capacity to experience pleasure — the frequency of adaptive behaviors decreases. This behavioral withdrawal leads to the dysphoric mood, passivity, and social isolation characteristic of depression. The depressed person may initially receive attention and sympathy from others, but over time these social reinforcers diminish as others become frustrated or avoidant, further reducing positive reinforcement and deepening the depressive cycle.

Lewinsohn identified three primary pathways through which reinforcement deficits arise: (1) the environment offers few available reinforcers, (2) the individual lacks the social skills necessary to obtain reinforcement, or (3) the individual has a diminished ability to enjoy experiences that were previously rewarding.

Example

After relocating to a new city for work, a 32-year-old woman loses her established social network. She stops attending group activities because initial attempts to connect with new people feel unrewarding. As her activity level drops, her mood worsens, and she becomes increasingly isolated — further reducing opportunities for positive social reinforcement. From Lewinsohn's perspective, this cycle illustrates how a low rate of response-contingent reinforcement produces and maintains depression.

Seligman's (1975) learned helplessness model proposes that depression results from perceived inability to control outcomes. The reformulated model (Abramson et al., 1978) added attributional style: internal, stable, and global attributions for negative events are depressogenic.

Suicide Risk Assessment

Understanding these etiological models — particularly the role of hopelessness in Seligman's reformulated learned helplessness theory and Beck's cognitive model — provides essential context for the clinical priority that follows: assessing suicide risk. Every clinician will encounter suicidal clients — and how well you assess, document, and respond to that risk can literally mean the difference between life and death. For the EPPP, this topic is not only clinically essential but also heavily tested, spanning questions about risk factors, protective factors, legal and ethical obligations, and the critical distinction between outdated practices and current best-practice guidelines.

Consider this scenario: A 52-year-old man presents to your outpatient practice following a recent divorce. He reports pervasive hopelessness, has been drinking heavily, and mentions — almost in passing — that he recently purchased a firearm "for protection." He denies active suicidal ideation when you ask directly. Should you feel reassured? The answer, of course, is no. Suicide risk assessment requires you to look beyond a client's self-report and integrate demographic data, psychiatric history, behavioral warning signs, and situational factors into a comprehensive clinical picture. Let's examine the key components you need to know.

Clinical Note: No-Suicide Contracts

Historically, some clinicians have used no-suicide contracts (also called "contracts for safety") in which a client verbally or in writing agrees not to engage in self-harm. However, research consistently demonstrates that these agreements do not reduce the likelihood of suicidal behavior. A client in acute crisis may agree to such a contract to avoid hospitalization, to please the clinician, or out of social compliance — none of which reflect genuine safety. Furthermore, no-suicide contracts offer no legal protection to the therapist in the event of a client's suicide. Courts have not recognized these contracts as meeting the standard of care for suicide risk management. Current best practices instead emphasize the use of collaborative safety planning, which involves working with the client to identify warning signs, coping strategies, social supports, professionals to contact during crisis, and means restriction steps. The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention is a widely adopted evidence-based alternative that shifts the focus from a passive promise to an active, personalized set of strategies the client can use when suicidal urges arise. Clinicians should also conduct thorough, ongoing risk assessments and document clinical decision-making rather than relying on a signed agreement as a substitute for careful evaluation.

Risk Factors

Understanding suicide risk factors is essential for both clinical practice and the EPPP. No single risk factor is sufficient to predict suicide — rather, risk accumulates as multiple factors converge. Think of risk assessment as building a mosaic: each piece alone may seem incomplete, but together they reveal a clearer picture. The following risk factors are the most well-established in the research literature, and you should know them thoroughly for the exam.

  • Previous suicide attempt -- the single strongest predictor of completed suicide
  • Psychiatric diagnosis -- over 90% of completers had a diagnosable disorder, with depression and substance use being the most common
  • Hopelessness -- a stronger predictor of suicidal intent than depression severity (Beck et al., 1985)
  • Demographic factors: Males complete suicide at approximately 4 times the rate of females; females attempt suicide approximately 3 times as often as males
  • Access to lethal means, family history of suicide, recent losses, social isolation
  • Age: Highest rates in adults 45-64 and those 85+

The gender paradox in suicide — men die by suicide far more often, yet women attempt it far more frequently — is one of the most important distinctions to remember. This disparity is largely explained by method lethality: men are more likely to use firearms, while women more often use overdose or other methods that allow greater opportunity for intervention. This is also why means restriction (such as safely storing firearms or limiting access to medications) is one of the most effective suicide prevention strategies available, and why clinicians should always ask about access to lethal means during a risk assessment.

Notice that hopelessness, not depression severity per se, is the stronger predictor of suicidal intent. Aaron Beck and colleagues demonstrated this finding in landmark research, and it carries important clinical implications: a client whose depression is moderate but who feels utterly trapped and hopeless about the future may be at greater risk than someone with severe depressive symptoms who still holds some optimism. This is why assessment tools like the Beck Hopelessness Scale can provide clinically valuable information beyond standard depression measures.

Protective Factors

Effective suicide risk assessment doesn't stop at cataloging what makes a person vulnerable — it also requires identifying what keeps them alive. Protective factors are the counterweights to risk, and understanding them helps clinicians develop more nuanced, individualized safety plans. A person with multiple risk factors but strong reasons for living, robust social connections, and active engagement in treatment may be at meaningfully lower risk than their risk factor profile alone would suggest.

  • Social connectedness and reasons for living
  • Effective clinical care for mental and substance use disorders
  • Restricted access to lethal means
  • Cultural and religious beliefs that discourage suicide

Keep in mind that protective factors do not eliminate risk — they buffer against it. A clinician should never dismiss elevated risk simply because a client identifies reasons for living or reports religious beliefs against suicide. Instead, protective factors should be woven into the clinical formulation alongside risk factors to arrive at a balanced, well-documented assessment of overall suicide risk. On the EPPP, expect questions that require you to weigh both sides of this equation rather than relying on any single indicator in isolation.

Clinical Pearl

The Joiner interpersonal theory of suicide (2005) identifies three factors necessary for a lethal suicide attempt: thwarted belongingness (feeling disconnected), perceived burdensomeness (feeling like a burden to others), and acquired capability (reduced fear of death through habituation to pain and self-harm). What makes Joiner's model particularly powerful — and frequently tested on the EPPP — is its explanation of why most people who desire suicide never attempt it. According to the theory, suicidal desire arises from the intersection of thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness, but desire alone is not sufficient. Only when a person has also developed acquired capability — through repeated exposure to painful or provocative experiences such as prior self-harm, combat exposure, childhood abuse, or even certain occupational risks — does the threshold for a lethal attempt get crossed. This three-factor model helps explain why previous suicide attempts are such a potent risk factor: each attempt may further habituate the individual to the fear and pain associated with self-injury, increasing acquired capability for future attempts.

EPPP Focus Points

  • MDD requires 5 of 9 symptoms for 2 weeks; must include depressed mood or anhedonia
  • DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion for MDD
  • PDD duration: 2 years in adults, 1 year in children/adolescents
  • Bipolar I requires at least one manic episode; depressive episodes are not required
  • Bipolar II requires at least one hypomanic + one major depressive episode; if mania ever occurs, diagnosis changes to Bipolar I
  • Mania lasts at least 7 days; hypomania at least 4 days
  • Hopelessness is a stronger predictor of suicide than depression severity
  • Previous suicide attempt is the strongest predictor of future completed suicide
  • Males complete suicide more often; females attempt more often
  • Atypical depression features: mood reactivity, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity
  • PMDD requires 5+ symptoms in the final week before menses; must include at least one core affective symptom
  • PMDD requires prospective daily ratings for 2 consecutive cycles for confirmation
  • PMDD prevalence: 1.8-5.8% of menstruating women; distinct from PMS by severity and functional impairment
