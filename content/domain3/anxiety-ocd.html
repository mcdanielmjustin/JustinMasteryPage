Overview of Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders are among the most prevalent psychiatric conditions, affecting approximately 31% of adults at some point during their lives (Kessler et al., 2005). The DSM-5 categorizes anxiety disorders as conditions characterized by excessive fear and anxiety along with related behavioral disturbances. Fear is the emotional response to a real or perceived imminent threat, while anxiety is the anticipation of a future threat.

Key Distinction: Fear vs. Anxiety

Fear involves surges of autonomic arousal necessary for fight-or-flight, thoughts of immediate danger, and escape behaviors. Anxiety involves muscle tension, vigilance in preparation for future danger, and cautious or avoidant behaviors. Both can overlap, but the distinction is clinically important for differential diagnosis.

Anxiety disorders differ from normal developmental anxiety by being excessive, persistent (typically lasting 6 months or more), and causing clinically significant distress or impairment. The DSM-5 reorganized the anxiety chapter by removing OCD and PTSD into their own chapters, though they share features with anxiety disorders.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Imagine a client who sits in your office and tells you she worries about everything — her job performance, her children's safety, whether she paid a bill on time, whether a mole on her arm looks different than it did last week. She knows the worry is excessive. She tries to stop, but the thoughts just keep cycling. She hasn't slept well in months, her neck and shoulders are locked in a permanent knot of tension, and she snaps at her partner over trivial things. She can't point to a single triggering event or a specific feared object — the worry simply saturates every domain of her life. This pervasive, free-floating pattern of apprehension is the hallmark of Generalized Anxiety Disorder, a condition characterized by persistent and excessive worry across a variety of domains including work, health, family, finances, and everyday matters. Unlike the focused dread we see in phobias or the episodic surges of panic disorder, GAD spreads its anxious net wide — and the person finds it extraordinarily difficult to rein the worry in.

As we discussed in the overview, anxiety disorders share the core feature of excessive, persistent fear and anxiety that causes significant distress or impairment. GAD exemplifies that definition in its purest form: the worry itself becomes the central problem, and it drags along at least three somatic or cognitive symptoms that compound the individual's suffering. Understanding GAD well prepares you not only for exam questions but also for clinical practice, because this disorder shows up frequently — and rarely travels alone.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

The DSM-5 lays out a structured set of criteria that capture both the cognitive and physiological dimensions of GAD. Pay close attention to the temporal requirement and the specific symptom threshold, as these details commonly appear on licensing examinations.

  1. Criterion A: Excessive anxiety and worry occurring more days than not for at least 6 months, about a number of events or activities.
  2. Criterion B: The individual finds it difficult to control the worry.
  3. Criterion C: The anxiety and worry are associated with three or more of the following (only one required in children): restlessness or feeling keyed up, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.
  4. Criterion D: The disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment.
  5. Criterion E: Not attributable to substances or another medical condition.
  6. Criterion F: Not better explained by another mental disorder.

A few clinical details deserve emphasis here. First, notice that Criterion A demands worry across multiple events or activities — a person who worries exclusively about contamination or social evaluation likely meets criteria for a different diagnosis. Second, the "three or more" somatic/cognitive symptom threshold in Criterion C drops to just one symptom for children, reflecting the developmental reality that younger individuals may not yet articulate or manifest the full adult symptom profile. Third, muscle tension stands out among the Criterion C symptoms as particularly characteristic of GAD; many clients present initially to primary care physicians with chronic neck pain, headaches, or jaw clenching before anyone identifies the underlying anxiety disorder.

Clinical Pearl

GAD has the highest comorbidity rate of any anxiety disorder. Approximately 90% of individuals with GAD have at least one comorbid disorder, most commonly MDD. The intolerance of uncertainty model (Dugas et al., 1998) posits that individuals with GAD have difficulty tolerating ambiguous situations, which fuels the worry cycle.

That staggering 90% comorbidity figure deserves a moment of reflection. In clinical practice, "pure" GAD — without depression, another anxiety disorder, or a substance use problem alongside it — is the exception rather than the rule. This means that when you identify GAD, your next step should always involve a thorough assessment for co-occurring conditions, especially major depressive disorder. The intolerance of uncertainty model offers a particularly useful clinical lens: picture a client who cannot leave any question unanswered, who Googles every minor health symptom at 2 a.m., and who demands reassurance from loved ones about outcomes no one can predict. That intolerance of "not knowing" drives a relentless search for certainty that, paradoxically, generates more worry rather than less. Therapeutic approaches informed by this model — such as those developed by Dugas and colleagues — help clients gradually increase their tolerance for ambiguity, often through behavioral experiments that expose them to deliberately uncertain situations.

Epidemiology

GAD affects approximately 5.7% of adults over their lifetime, making it one of the more common anxiety disorders encountered in both outpatient mental health and primary care settings. Women receive the diagnosis roughly twice as often as men, a gender disparity consistent with most anxiety disorders. Unlike many anxiety conditions that emerge in childhood or adolescence, GAD tends to develop gradually, with a median age of onset around 30 years — later than most other anxiety disorders. Clinicians should keep this timeline in mind: a 35-year-old presenting with a years-long pattern of diffuse worry fits the typical GAD profile, whereas the same presentation in a 12-year-old warrants careful differential diagnosis.

Once established, GAD typically follows a chronic, waxing-and-waning course. Clients often describe periods where the worry becomes more manageable — perhaps during a vacation or a stretch of relative stability — only to intensify again when new stressors emerge. This fluctuating trajectory can mislead both clients and clinicians into believing the problem has resolved, when in reality the underlying vulnerability persists. Recognizing this pattern helps set appropriate treatment expectations: the goal is not just symptom relief during a "good phase" but durable skills and, when indicated, sustained pharmacological support that prevents the next escalation.

Clinical Note

In children and adolescents, the presentation of GAD differs somewhat from the adult form. Children with GAD are most frequently preoccupied with concerns about academic and athletic competence — worrying excessively about grades, test performance, or their ability in sports. They also commonly express fears about catastrophic events and natural disasters such as earthquakes, tornadoes, or wars. Unlike adults, who more often worry about finances, health, or job performance, children tend to focus on areas where they are being evaluated or where they perceive uncontrollable external threats. Only one somatic or cognitive symptom from Criterion C is required for diagnosis in children, compared to three for adults. Children with GAD frequently seek excessive reassurance from parents and teachers and may present as "little perfectionists" who redo assignments or become visibly distressed about minor mistakes. Recognizing these age-specific worry themes is essential for accurate identification, as children may not label their experience as "worry" and instead present with somatic complaints such as stomachaches or headaches.

Onset of separation anxiety disorder is frequently precipitated by identifiable life stressors that disrupt the child's sense of security. Common triggers include the death of a relative or family pet, a change in schools, parental divorce, a move to a new home, or an illness in the family. For example, a child who had previously attended school without difficulty may begin refusing to leave home and exhibit intense distress about being apart from her parents following the loss of a beloved pet. The experience of loss or disruption activates fears about the permanence of attachment relationships, making the child hypervigilant about potential threats to remaining attachment figures. Clinicians should routinely assess for recent psychosocial stressors when separation anxiety symptoms emerge or intensify, as identifying the precipitant can guide both case conceptualization and therapeutic intervention.

Specific Phobias

Specific phobia involves marked and disproportionate fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation that is actively avoided or endured with intense distress. The DSM-5 identifies five specifier subtypes:

  • Animal type (e.g., spiders, dogs, insects) -- typically onset in childhood
  • Natural environment type (e.g., heights, storms, water)
  • Blood-injection-injury (BII) type -- uniquely associated with a vasovagal response (fainting)
  • Situational type (e.g., airplanes, elevators, enclosed places)
  • Other type (e.g., choking, vomiting, loud sounds)

EPPP Example

A patient faints at the sight of blood during a medical procedure. Unlike other phobias that involve sustained sympathetic arousal, BII phobia involves an initial sympathetic spike followed by a parasympathetic rebound, producing bradycardia and hypotension. This is why applied tension (Ost & Sterner, 1987) is the treatment of choice for BII phobia, rather than standard relaxation techniques.

Specific phobias are the most common anxiety disorder, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 12.5%. They are often acquired through direct traumatic experience, observation, or informational transmission (Rachman, 1977).

Social Anxiety Disorder

Imagine a graduate student who has thoroughly prepared a case presentation for her practicum seminar. She knows the material cold — she could explain her conceptualization in her sleep. Yet as she walks toward the front of the room, her heart pounds, her face flushes, and her mind goes blank. She is certain that every person in the room can see her hands trembling, that they are judging her as incompetent. She stumbles through her presentation, then spends the rest of the day replaying every perceived misstep. This is not ordinary stage fright. This is Social Anxiety Disorder — and it is one of the most prevalent and impairing anxiety conditions clinicians encounter.

Having just explored how specific phobias involve intense fear tied to circumscribed objects or situations, we now turn to a disorder where the feared stimulus is fundamentally interpersonal: other people's judgment. Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD), previously known as social phobia, involves marked and persistent fear or anxiety about social situations in which the individual may be scrutinized by others. The core dread is not simply of the situation itself — it is of acting in a way that will be negatively evaluated, leading to humiliation, embarrassment, or rejection. Unlike the person with a specific phobia of heights who can simply avoid balconies, the person with SAD faces their feared stimulus every time they enter a classroom, attend a meeting, or make small talk at a gathering.

What makes SAD particularly insidious is its self-reinforcing quality. A person who fears negative evaluation begins monitoring themselves intensely during social interactions — scrutinizing their own words, facial expressions, and body language. This inward focus paradoxically makes them less attuned to actual social cues (like the friendly nods of listeners) and more convinced that they are performing badly. They leave the interaction believing their worst fears were confirmed, which strengthens avoidance the next time. This internal feedback loop is central to understanding why the disorder persists even when others perceive the individual as perfectly competent.

Key Features

The DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for Social Anxiety Disorder capture a pattern that goes well beyond ordinary shyness or nervousness. Consider the difference between a new employee who feels butterflies before her first team meeting versus someone who has called in sick three times this month specifically to avoid speaking up in those meetings. The diagnostic features reflect this level of intensity and impairment:

  • The social situations almost always provoke fear or anxiety
  • Situations are avoided or endured with intense fear or anxiety
  • The fear is out of proportion to the actual threat posed
  • Duration of at least 6 months
  • Specify if performance only (limited to speaking or performing in public)

The six-month duration requirement is particularly important for the EPPP. This criterion helps clinicians distinguish SAD from transient social anxiety that might arise during a stressful life transition — such as starting a new job or adjusting to college — and the chronic, entrenched pattern that characterizes the disorder. Additionally, clinicians must rule out that the fear and avoidance are not better explained by another condition, such as body dysmorphic disorder (where the person fears scrutiny of a perceived physical flaw) or autism spectrum disorder (where social difficulties stem from different underlying mechanisms).

Cognitive models (Clark & Wells, 1995) emphasize that individuals with SAD shift attention inward to monitor their own behavior and appearance, creating a distorted self-image that perpetuates anxiety in social situations.

The Clark and Wells (1995) cognitive model deserves particular attention because it has profoundly shaped evidence-based treatment for SAD. According to this model, when a person with SAD enters a feared social situation, they activate a set of negative beliefs about themselves ("I'm boring," "People will see how anxious I am"). These beliefs trigger a shift in attention — instead of focusing outward on the conversation or audience, the person turns their attention inward, constructing a mental image of how they believe they appear to others. This self-image is almost always distorted and catastrophic: they picture themselves visibly sweating, stammering, or looking foolish. Because they are relying on this internal "camera" rather than on actual social feedback, they leave the situation convinced that their worst fears came true. This model explains why people with SAD can receive compliments on a presentation yet remain certain it went terribly — they trusted their internal self-monitoring over external evidence.

Clinically, this has direct treatment implications. Cognitive-behavioral interventions for SAD often include behavioral experiments designed to shift attention outward, video feedback to correct distorted self-imagery, and systematic testing of catastrophic predictions. For example, a therapist might ask a client to deliberately focus on their conversational partner's responses during a social interaction and then compare what actually happened to what they predicted would happen.

SAD has a lifetime prevalence of approximately 12% in the United States, making it one of the most common mental disorders. It typically begins in the mid-teens, often following a history of social inhibition or behavioral inhibition temperament — that quiet, watchful child who clings to a parent at birthday parties and takes a long time to warm up to new people. Research consistently shows that behavioral inhibition in early childhood is one of the strongest temperamental risk factors for developing SAD later in life, though not all inhibited children go on to develop the disorder. Onset after age 25 is relatively uncommon; when social anxiety appears for the first time in adulthood, clinicians should carefully consider whether it might be secondary to another condition, such as depression or a medical illness.

The performance only specifier was introduced in DSM-5 and distinguishes individuals whose fears are restricted to public speaking or performance contexts from those with the more generalized form. This distinction matters both clinically and on the EPPP. A person with the performance only specifier may function well in one-on-one conversations, small group interactions, and even casual social gatherings — their anxiety is triggered specifically when they must perform or speak before an audience. In contrast, the generalized form of SAD involves fear across a broad range of social interactions, including casual conversations, eating in front of others, or even making eye contact. The performance only subtype tends to have a later onset, may be less associated with comorbid conditions, and sometimes responds well to beta-blockers (such as propranolol) taken before a performance — a pharmacological strategy that is less effective for generalized SAD, where SSRIs and comprehensive CBT are typically first-line treatments.

Panic Disorder and Agoraphobia

Panic Disorder

Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks -- abrupt surges of intense fear or discomfort that reach a peak within minutes. At least one attack must be followed by one month or more of persistent concern about additional attacks or maladaptive behavioral changes related to the attacks.

Panic Attack Symptoms (4 or more required)

  1. Palpitations, pounding heart, or accelerated heart rate
  2. Sweating
  3. Trembling or shaking
  4. Sensations of shortness of breath or smothering
  5. Feelings of choking
  6. Chest pain or discomfort
  7. Nausea or abdominal distress
  8. Feeling dizzy, unsteady, light-headed, or faint
  9. Chills or heat sensations
  10. Paresthesias (numbness or tingling)
  11. Derealization or depersonalization
  12. Fear of losing control or "going crazy"
  13. Fear of dying

Clinical Pearl

The anxiety sensitivity model (Reiss & McNally, 1985) proposes that individuals with panic disorder have elevated fear of anxiety-related sensations themselves, believing these sensations have harmful consequences. Clark's (1986) cognitive model of panic emphasizes catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations as the core mechanism.

Agoraphobia

In DSM-5, agoraphobia is diagnosed as a separate disorder from panic disorder. It involves marked fear or anxiety about two or more of the following five situations: using public transportation, being in open spaces, being in enclosed places, standing in line or being in a crowd, and being outside of the home alone. The fear is that escape might be difficult or help unavailable in the event of panic-like symptoms.

Approximately one-third to one-half of individuals with panic disorder also develop agoraphobia. Notably, agoraphobia can occur without a history of panic disorder.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Having explored how panic disorder and agoraphobia can trap individuals in cycles of fear and avoidance, we now turn to a condition that creates its own distinctive prison — one built not from feared places, but from feared thoughts. Imagine a new mother who cannot stop picturing herself harming her infant, even though she loves the child deeply. Or a medical student who washes his hands until they crack and bleed, yet still feels contaminated. These individuals are not dangerous, nor are they "dirty." They are caught in the relentless grip of OCD, a disorder characterized by the presence of obsessions, compulsions, or both — intrusive mental events paired with desperate attempts to neutralize them.

Although OCD was reclassified into its own chapter in DSM-5 (Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders), reflecting growing recognition of its unique neurobiology and treatment profile, it remains a commonly tested topic alongside anxiety disorders on the EPPP. Understanding the distinction between obsessions and compulsions — and how they lock together into self-reinforcing cycles — is foundational to both clinical practice and exam preparation.

Obsessions vs. Compulsions

Obsessions are recurrent, persistent, intrusive, and unwanted thoughts, urges, or images that cause marked anxiety or distress. The individual attempts to ignore, suppress, or neutralize them — but the harder one tries to push an obsession away, the more forcefully it returns, much like trying not to think of a white bear. Compulsions are repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the individual feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules. They aim to prevent or reduce anxiety or avert a dreaded event, but they are not realistically connected to what they are designed to neutralize. A person may tap a doorframe exactly seven times to "prevent" a loved one from dying — fully aware the action makes no logical sense, yet unable to resist performing it.

What makes OCD so clinically distinctive — and so agonizing for those who suffer from it — is the ego-dystonic nature of the obsessions. These thoughts feel alien, horrifying, and fundamentally at odds with who the person believes themselves to be. The compassionate parent tormented by violent images toward a child, the devoutly religious person plagued by blasphemous thoughts — these individuals do not want these mental intrusions. The compulsions then emerge as desperate coping strategies, momentarily reducing anxiety but ultimately reinforcing the obsessional cycle. Each time a compulsion "works" (the anxiety briefly drops), the brain learns that the ritual was necessary, strengthening the urge to repeat it next time.

Common OCD Symptom Dimensions

OCD does not present as a single, uniform condition. Researchers have identified several symptom dimensions that cluster together, each pairing characteristic obsessions with corresponding compulsions. Recognizing these dimensions helps clinicians assess the full scope of a patient's symptoms and guides treatment planning. A client who presents with contamination fears and washing rituals, for instance, may respond to different exposure hierarchies than one consumed by intrusive thoughts of harming others.

Note that some compulsions are purely mental — silently counting, praying, or repeating phrases — and can be invisible to outside observers. A client may appear to be staring into space when, in reality, they are performing an elaborate mental ritual to neutralize a terrifying intrusive thought. Clinicians who only ask about behavioral rituals may miss an entire category of compulsive activity, so thorough assessment should always probe for covert mental acts.

DSM-5 Specifiers

The DSM-5 includes two important specifiers for OCD that carry significant clinical and prognostic implications. The first addresses how much awareness the person has that their OCD beliefs may not be true — a dimension that directly affects treatment engagement and outcome.

The insight specifier categorizes individuals along a continuum: good or fair insight (recognizing that OCD beliefs are definitely or probably not true), poor insight (thinking OCD beliefs are probably true), or absent insight/delusional beliefs (completely convinced the beliefs are true). Approximately 4% of individuals with OCD fall into the absent insight category. Consider a man who is utterly certain that failing to check the stove exactly twelve times will cause his house to burn down — he does not merely worry it might happen; he knows it will. This conviction can make treatment especially challenging because the person sees no reason to resist the compulsion. Poor or absent insight also raises important differential diagnostic questions, as clinicians must distinguish OCD with delusional beliefs from a primary psychotic disorder.

The tic-related specifier applies when the individual has a current or past history of a tic disorder, such as Tourette's disorder. This specifier flags a clinically meaningful subtype: tic-related OCD typically presents with an earlier age of onset, shows a more frequent male predominance, and features higher rates of symmetry and ordering compulsions. Research suggests that tic-related OCD may involve somewhat different neural circuitry — particularly striatal pathways — and may show a somewhat different treatment response, with some evidence that augmentation with antipsychotic medications can benefit this subgroup when SSRIs alone prove insufficient.

EPPP Example

Distinguishing OCD from OCPD: In OCD, obsessions are ego-dystonic (experienced as unwanted and distressing). The person fights against the intrusive thoughts and recognizes them as irrational — a mother horrified by violent images of her child does not want those thoughts and finds them deeply disturbing. In OCPD, the preoccupation with orderliness and perfectionism is ego-syntonic (consistent with the person's self-image). A supervisor with OCPD who insists every report follow an exacting format sees this rigidity as a virtue, not a symptom. Individuals with OCPD rarely seek treatment for these traits because they see them as desirable. This ego-dystonic versus ego-syntonic distinction is one of the most frequently tested differentials on the EPPP.

As we move into the next section on etiological models, keep in mind that OCD's unique position — straddling the boundary between anxiety disorders and its own diagnostic category — reflects the complex interplay of cognitive, behavioral, and neurobiological factors that drive this condition. Understanding what causes anxiety and OCD will help you appreciate why certain treatments, particularly exposure and response prevention, target the very mechanisms that keep these disorders alive.

Etiological Models of Anxiety and OCD

Biological Models

  • Neurotransmitters: Dysregulation of GABA (inhibitory), serotonin (5-HT), and norepinephrine is implicated across anxiety disorders. OCD is particularly linked to serotonin dysfunction, supported by the efficacy of SSRIs and clomipramine (a serotonergic TCA).
  • Neuroanatomy: The amygdala plays a central role in fear conditioning. The cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit is implicated in OCD. Hyperactivity in the orbitofrontal cortex and caudate nucleus is a consistent finding in neuroimaging studies of OCD (Saxena et al., 1998).
  • Genetics: Heritability estimates for anxiety disorders range from 30-50%. First-degree relatives of individuals with OCD have a 4-5 times increased risk of developing OCD.

Behavioral Models

Mowrer's (1960) two-factor theory remains foundational. Fear is initially acquired through classical conditioning (Factor 1) and maintained through operant conditioning via negative reinforcement of avoidance behavior (Factor 2). This model provides the rationale for exposure and response prevention (ERP), the gold-standard behavioral treatment for OCD and many anxiety disorders.

Cognitive Models

Insufficient Top-Down Control Hypothesis

This neurobiological model of GAD proposes that chronic worry results from diminished prefrontal regulatory control over limbic and paralimbic structures involved in threat processing. Specifically, the hypothesis predicts that GAD is associated with reduced functional connectivity among the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). In healthy individuals, the prefrontal cortex exerts inhibitory (top-down) modulation of amygdala-driven fear responses, with the ACC serving a critical mediating role in conflict monitoring and emotional regulation. In individuals with GAD, weakened connectivity within this circuit means that amygdala reactivity to perceived threats goes insufficiently dampened, perpetuating a state of heightened vigilance and uncontrollable worry.

Neuroimaging evidence supports this model: functional MRI studies have demonstrated that individuals with GAD exhibit hypoactivation of the prefrontal cortex during emotion regulation tasks and hyperactivation of the amygdala in response to threat-related stimuli. The ACC, which normally facilitates the selection of appropriate regulatory strategies, shows aberrant activation patterns — sometimes overactive during ambiguous stimuli (reflecting chronic conflict detection) and sometimes underactive during deliberate emotion regulation efforts. This framework integrates cognitive models emphasizing intolerance of uncertainty with neuroscience, suggesting that the cognitive features of GAD have identifiable neural substrates. It also has treatment implications: both CBT and pharmacotherapy for GAD have been shown to partially normalize prefrontal-amygdala connectivity, supporting the clinical relevance of this circuit-level model.

  • Attentional bias: Individuals with anxiety disorders show preferential attention to threat-related stimuli (MacLeod et al., 1986).
  • Catastrophic misinterpretation: Clark's (1986) model of panic; Wells' (1995) metacognitive model of GAD.
  • Inflated responsibility: Salkovskis (1985) proposed that OCD arises from the belief that one has the power and duty to prevent harm, leading to neutralizing compulsions.
  • Thought-action fusion: The belief that thinking about an action is morally equivalent to performing it, or that thinking about an event makes it more likely to occur (Rachman, 1993).
Barlow's (2000) triple vulnerability model proposes that anxiety disorders arise from the interaction of generalized biological vulnerability, generalized psychological vulnerability (early experiences of unpredictability/uncontrollability), and specific psychological vulnerability (learning to focus anxiety on particular stimuli).

Evidence-Based Treatments for Anxiety and OCD

Psychotherapy

  • Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP): The gold-standard treatment for OCD. Involves systematic exposure to feared stimuli (obsession triggers) while preventing the compulsive response. Approximately 60-70% of patients show significant improvement. Based on the principle that anxiety habituates with prolonged exposure and that preventing compulsions breaks the negative reinforcement cycle.
  • Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT): First-line treatment for all anxiety disorders. For GAD, targets intolerance of uncertainty and worry beliefs. For social anxiety, involves cognitive restructuring of negative self-evaluations and behavioral experiments with video feedback. For panic disorder, involves interoceptive exposure (deliberately producing feared bodily sensations) and cognitive restructuring of catastrophic misinterpretations.
  • Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): Emerging evidence for anxiety disorders; emphasizes psychological flexibility, acceptance of internal experiences, and values-based action rather than symptom elimination.
  • Applied relaxation: Ost's method teaching rapid relaxation skills; effective for GAD.

Pharmacotherapy

  • SSRIs: First-line pharmacological treatment for all anxiety disorders and OCD. Higher doses are typically needed for OCD (e.g., fluoxetine 60-80 mg) compared to depression (20 mg). Onset of therapeutic effect is 4-6 weeks for anxiety, and up to 8-12 weeks for OCD.
  • SNRIs: Venlafaxine and duloxetine are effective for GAD and social anxiety disorder.
  • Benzodiazepines: Effective for acute anxiety relief but not recommended as first-line treatment due to risk of dependence, tolerance, and withdrawal. They do not treat OCD.
  • Clomipramine: A serotonergic TCA that is effective for OCD, sometimes used when SSRIs fail. Has more side effects than SSRIs.
  • Buspirone: A 5-HT1A partial agonist used for GAD; slow onset (2-4 weeks); no abuse potential; not effective for OCD or panic.

Clinical Pearl

For OCD specifically, the combination of ERP with an SSRI produces the best outcomes. The NICE guidelines recommend stepped care: (1) low-intensity CBT including ERP for mild OCD, (2) higher-intensity CBT with ERP or SSRI for moderate OCD, (3) combined CBT/ERP plus SSRI for severe OCD, and (4) clomipramine or augmentation with an antipsychotic for treatment-resistant cases. Approximately 40-60% of OCD patients are classified as treatment-resistant with first-line approaches.

Separation Anxiety Disorder

Separation Anxiety Disorder is characterized by developmentally inappropriate and excessive fear or anxiety concerning separation from attachment figures. While historically categorized as a childhood-onset disorder in DSM-IV, the DSM-5 moved it to the anxiety disorders chapter, recognizing that it can be diagnosed across the lifespan, including in adults.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

  1. Criterion A: Developmentally inappropriate and excessive fear or anxiety concerning separation from those to whom the individual is attached, as evidenced by at least three of the following:
    • Recurrent excessive distress when anticipating or experiencing separation from home or major attachment figures
    • Persistent and excessive worry about losing major attachment figures or about possible harm to them (e.g., illness, injury, disaster, death)
    • Persistent and excessive worry about experiencing an untoward event (e.g., getting lost, being kidnapped, having an accident) that causes separation from a major attachment figure
    • Persistent reluctance or refusal to go out, away from home, to school, to work, or elsewhere because of fear of separation
    • Persistent and excessive fear of or reluctance about being alone or without major attachment figures at home or in other settings
    • Persistent reluctance or refusal to sleep away from home or to go to sleep without being near a major attachment figure
    • Repeated nightmares involving the theme of separation
    • Repeated complaints of physical symptoms (e.g., headaches, stomachaches, nausea, vomiting) when separation from major attachment figures occurs or is anticipated
  2. Criterion B: The fear, anxiety, or avoidance is persistent, lasting at least 4 weeks in children and adolescents and typically 6 months or more in adults.
  3. Criterion C: The disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, academic, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
  4. Criterion D: The disturbance is not better explained by another mental disorder.

Key Distinction: Duration Requirement

The DSM-5 applies a differential duration threshold: 4 weeks for children and adolescents versus 6 months for adults. This reflects the recognition that brief separation fears are normative in childhood but more clinically significant when persistent in adulthood.

Epidemiology and Course

Separation anxiety disorder is the most common anxiety disorder in children under 12, with a prevalence of approximately 4% in children and 1-2% in adults. Onset typically occurs in childhood, though adult-onset cases are increasingly recognized. The disorder tends to fluctuate in severity and may re-emerge during periods of stress.

Clinical Pearl

Somatic complaints, particularly headaches and stomachaches, are a hallmark presentation in children with separation anxiety disorder, especially on school mornings or in anticipation of separation. These physical symptoms often lead to medical evaluation before the anxiety disorder is identified. In adults, separation anxiety frequently manifests as excessive concern about the well-being of children or a spouse and reluctance to be apart from them.

Differential Diagnosis

Separation anxiety disorder must be distinguished from agoraphobia (fear of being unable to escape, not specifically about separation from attachment figures), generalized anxiety disorder (worry about multiple topics, not limited to separation), and social anxiety disorder (avoidance of social scrutiny, not separation). In children, school refusal may stem from separation anxiety, social anxiety, or specific phobia of the school environment, and careful assessment is needed to determine the primary driving fear.

Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD)

Imagine a college student who spends three hours each morning examining her skin in the mirror, convinced that faint acne scars make her appear "grotesque" — even though her friends genuinely cannot see what she is describing. She arrives late to class, avoids social gatherings, and has consulted four dermatologists, none of whom found anything clinically notable. Her distress is real, her impairment is severe, and yet the perceived flaw that drives her suffering is essentially invisible to everyone around her. This painful disconnect between subjective experience and objective reality lies at the heart of Body Dysmorphic Disorder, a condition classified under Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders in the DSM-5.

Having explored separation anxiety disorder — where distress centers on losing proximity to attachment figures — we now shift to a very different kind of preoccupation. In Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD), the individual fixates on one or more perceived defects or flaws in physical appearance that others either do not notice at all or consider only slight. Despite the minimal or nonexistent nature of these appearance concerns, the person experiences profound distress and significant functional impairment, often spending hours each day trapped in repetitive checking, grooming, or comparing rituals. BDD's placement alongside OCD in the DSM-5 reflects the striking parallels between the two disorders: intrusive, distressing preoccupations drive compulsive behaviors that temporarily reduce anxiety but ultimately perpetuate the cycle.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

The DSM-5 organizes the diagnosis of BDD around four core criteria. Clinicians should pay careful attention to each one, because the boundaries between BDD and related conditions — particularly eating disorders and delusional disorder — hinge on these distinctions.

  1. Criterion A: Preoccupation with one or more perceived defects or flaws in physical appearance that are not observable or appear only slight to others.
  2. Criterion B: At some point during the course of the disorder, the individual has performed repetitive behaviors (e.g., mirror checking, excessive grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking) or mental acts (e.g., comparing his or her appearance with that of others) in response to the appearance concerns.
  3. Criterion C: The preoccupation causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
  4. Criterion D: The appearance preoccupation is not better explained by concerns with body fat or weight in an individual whose symptoms meet diagnostic criteria for an eating disorder.

Notice how Criterion B explicitly requires behavioral or mental responses to the appearance concern — not merely worry. This requirement highlights the obsessive-compulsive structure of BDD: the preoccupation functions like an obsession, and the checking, grooming, or comparing functions like a compulsion. Also note that Criterion D draws a critical diagnostic boundary: when a patient's appearance concerns focus specifically on body fat or weight and the clinical picture meets criteria for an eating disorder such as anorexia nervosa, the eating disorder diagnosis takes precedence rather than BDD.

DSM-5 Specifiers

The DSM-5 provides two important specifiers that help clinicians characterize the clinical presentation more precisely. These specifiers carry meaningful treatment and prognostic implications.

  • With muscle dysmorphia: The individual is preoccupied with the idea that his or her body build is too small or insufficiently muscular. This specifier applies even if the individual is also preoccupied with other body areas.
  • Insight specifier: With good or fair insight, with poor insight, or with absent insight/delusional beliefs.

The muscle dysmorphia specifier deserves particular attention on the EPPP. Picture a man who weighs 210 pounds of lean muscle yet perceives himself as "scrawny," wearing multiple layers of clothing to hide his frame and spending four or more hours daily at the gym. He may use anabolic steroids despite medical risks, driven by the unshakable conviction that he looks pathetically small. This presentation occurs almost exclusively in males and can easily go unrecognized because the culture often reinforces intensive exercise and muscular ideals. The insight specifier matters clinically because individuals with absent insight hold their appearance beliefs with delusional conviction — they are absolutely certain they look as terrible as they believe. Historically, clinicians diagnosed these individuals with delusional disorder, somatic type, but the DSM-5 now captures them under the BDD diagnosis with the "absent insight/delusional beliefs" specifier, recognizing that the underlying condition remains the same regardless of insight level.

Repetitive Behaviors in BDD

The repetitive behaviors in BDD are compulsive in nature and functionally analogous to OCD compulsions. Common behaviors include: mirror checking (or mirror avoidance — some individuals swing between compulsive checking and complete avoidance of reflective surfaces), excessive grooming, skin picking to remove perceived flaws (sometimes resulting in visible skin damage that paradoxically creates the very imperfections the person fears), camouflaging (with makeup, clothing, hats, posture), reassurance seeking about appearance from friends, family, or medical professionals, comparing appearance with others (in person or increasingly through social media), and excessive exercise (particularly in muscle dysmorphia). These behaviors consume significant time — often several hours per day — and prove extremely difficult to resist or control, much like the compulsions observed in OCD.

Epidemiology and Clinical Features

BDD affects approximately 2% of the general population, making it more common than many clinicians realize. Prevalence rates climb dramatically in settings where people actively seek appearance-related solutions — reaching 7-8% in dermatology clinics and cosmetic surgery practices. The disorder typically emerges during adolescence, with a mean age of onset around 16-17 years, a developmental period when identity formation and social comparison intensify. Without treatment, BDD usually follows a chronic and unremitting course, with symptoms waxing and waning but rarely resolving spontaneously.

The most commonly targeted areas of concern involve the skin (acne, scars, wrinkles, complexion), hair (thinning hair, excessive body hair, hairline), and nose (size, shape, symmetry), though individuals may fixate on virtually any body part — ears, teeth, jawline, hands, or genitalia. Many individuals with BDD focus on multiple areas simultaneously, and the specific focus may shift over time. A key clinical feature that distinguishes BDD from normal appearance dissatisfaction is the sheer amount of time consumed by the preoccupation — often three to eight hours per day — and the degree to which it disrupts daily functioning, relationships, and quality of life.

Clinical Pearl

BDD carries a significantly elevated suicide risk, with approximately 80% of individuals reporting lifetime suicidal ideation and 24-28% reporting suicide attempts — rates that rival or exceed those seen in major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder. Comorbidity compounds the clinical picture: approximately 75% of individuals with BDD meet criteria for comorbid major depressive disorder, and 30-40% also carry a diagnosis of OCD. Clinicians should always screen for suicidal ideation when BDD is suspected. Another critical point for both clinical practice and the EPPP: cosmetic procedures rarely improve BDD symptoms and may actually worsen them, often shifting the preoccupation to a new perceived flaw or intensifying dissatisfaction with the surgical result. Individuals with absent insight may present with delusional intensity and were historically diagnosed with "delusional disorder, somatic type," but DSM-5 allows the BDD diagnosis regardless of insight level — an important diagnostic update to remember.

Treatment

Effective treatment for BDD draws directly from the same principles that guide OCD intervention, reflecting their shared placement in the obsessive-compulsive spectrum. CBT with exposure and response prevention (ERP) stands as the first-line psychotherapy. In practice, exposure tasks involve systematically confronting the situations the individual avoids — going out in public without camouflage makeup, sitting under bright lighting, allowing others to see the "flawed" area — while response prevention targets the repetitive behaviors by having the person resist mirror checking, grooming rituals, and reassurance seeking. A therapist might ask a patient who spends 45 minutes applying concealer each morning to progressively reduce application time, then ultimately leave the house without it, tolerating the anxiety until it naturally diminishes.

On the pharmacological side, SSRIs serve as the first-line medication treatment for BDD, and clinicians typically prescribe them at doses higher than those used for depression — similar to the elevated doses effective for OCD. This dosing pattern reflects the serotonergic underpinnings shared across obsessive-compulsive spectrum disorders. Research consistently demonstrates that serotonergic medications produce significantly better outcomes than non-serotonergic alternatives for BDD, a finding that parallels the pharmacological response pattern seen in OCD. Combining CBT/ERP with SSRI treatment often yields the strongest results, particularly for individuals with severe symptoms or poor insight.

Hoarding Disorder

Having explored how Body Dysmorphic Disorder revolves around perceived flaws in appearance — with its ego-dystonic preoccupations and ritualistic checking — we now turn to a condition that centers on a very different kind of distress: the anguish of letting go. Imagine a retired schoolteacher whose three-bedroom home has become virtually uninhabitable. Stacks of newspapers, broken appliances, clothing still bearing price tags, and bags of unread mail fill every room from floor to ceiling. Only a narrow pathway connects the front door to the kitchen and bedroom. She knows, on some level, that the accumulation has become unmanageable — and yet each item feels essential, irreplaceable, imbued with potential usefulness or emotional significance. When her adult children attempt to clear a pathway through the living room, she becomes tearful and panicked, pleading with them to stop. This is the clinical reality of Hoarding Disorder.

Hoarding Disorder earned its place as a standalone diagnosis in the DSM-5, classified under Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders. For years, clinicians and researchers treated hoarding as merely a symptom or subtype of OCD, but a growing body of evidence revealed something more complex. Studies have demonstrated that hoarding involves distinct neurobiological signatures, unique cognitive patterns — particularly around decision-making and emotional attachment to objects — and behavioral features that clearly set it apart from OCD. These differences proved substantial enough to justify independent diagnostic status, a change that has meaningfully improved both clinical recognition and treatment planning.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

  1. Criterion A: Persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value.
  2. Criterion B: This difficulty is due to a perceived need to save the items and to distress associated with discarding them.
  3. Criterion C: The difficulty discarding possessions results in the accumulation of possessions that congest and clutter active living areas and substantially compromises their intended use. If living areas are uncluttered, it is only because of the interventions of third parties (e.g., family members, cleaners, authorities).
  4. Criterion D: The hoarding causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning (including maintaining a safe environment for oneself or others).
  5. Criterion E: The hoarding is not attributable to another medical condition (e.g., brain injury, cerebrovascular disease, Prader-Willi syndrome).
  6. Criterion F: The hoarding is not better explained by the symptoms of another mental disorder.

Notice how Criterion C introduces a critical functional threshold: the clutter must congest active living areas enough to substantially compromise their intended use. A collector who fills a dedicated storage unit with vintage records does not meet this criterion. But when a person can no longer cook in the kitchen, sleep in the bed, or sit on the couch because possessions have overtaken those spaces, the line into disorder has clearly been crossed. Also note the important caveat — if living areas appear uncluttered only because third parties (family members, landlords, or city authorities) have intervened, the criterion is still met.

DSM-5 Specifiers

  • With excessive acquisition: If difficulty discarding possessions is accompanied by excessive acquiring of items that are not needed or for which there is no available space.
  • Insight specifier: With good or fair insight, with poor insight, or with absent insight/delusional beliefs.

The "excessive acquisition" specifier deserves clinical attention because it captures a subgroup that actively brings new items into an already overwhelmed space — purchasing, collecting free items, or even stealing objects they do not need. The insight specifier, meanwhile, carries direct treatment implications. Many individuals with hoarding disorder present with poor or absent insight, genuinely believing that their accumulation is reasonable and that others simply fail to appreciate the value of their possessions. This stands in stark contrast to most individuals with OCD, who typically recognize their obsessions as irrational. A person who insists, with delusional conviction, that their collection of 4,000 empty yogurt containers will be "needed someday" presents a very different clinical challenge than someone who tearfully acknowledges, "I know this is out of control, but I just can't let anything go."

Key Distinction: Hoarding Disorder vs. OCD Hoarding

This distinction can make or break a differential diagnosis on the EPPP. Hoarding in the context of hoarding disorder is ego-syntonic — individuals genuinely value their possessions, see the saving as meaningful, and experience distress primarily at the thought of discarding them, not at the act of accumulating. In contrast, hoarding in the context of OCD is ego-dystonic — the accumulation feels unwanted and distressing, driven by intrusive obsessions such as fear of contamination preventing disposal, or a need for symmetry and completeness that compels the person to keep acquiring. A person with OCD-related hoarding might say, "I can't throw away this newspaper because touching it might contaminate my hands, and I'd rather just leave it there." A person with hoarding disorder might say, "I can't throw away this newspaper because it contains important information I might need someday." This distinction is critical for both differential diagnosis and treatment planning.

Epidemiology and Course

Hoarding disorder affects approximately 2-6% of the general population — a figure that surprises many clinicians, since it makes hoarding substantially more common than OCD. The disorder follows a characteristic developmental trajectory: symptoms typically emerge during adolescence (ages 11-15), often as mild attachment to objects or reluctance to discard, then become clinically significant by the mid-20s to 30s, and worsen progressively with each passing decade. By the time most individuals present for treatment, they are middle-aged or older, and the accumulation has reached crisis proportions. The disorder affects males and females at roughly equal rates in the community, though clinical samples skew predominantly female, likely reflecting differences in help-seeking behavior.

The real-world consequences of hoarding disorder extend far beyond psychological distress. Clinicians should recognize the significant health and safety risks that accompany severe hoarding: fire hazards from blocked exits and piled combustible materials, falls caused by navigating through cluttered pathways, unsanitary living conditions that attract pests and breed infection, and profound social isolation as individuals become too embarrassed to invite anyone into their homes. In extreme cases, municipal authorities intervene with forced cleanouts — interventions that, without concurrent psychological treatment, typically lead to rapid re-accumulation and intense emotional distress.

Clinical Pearl

Hoarding disorder remains one of the most treatment-resistant conditions in the OCD-related spectrum — a fact that every clinician should internalize. Standard OCD treatments, including ERP alone, show limited efficacy for hoarding because the core problem is not an anxiety-driven compulsion but rather a deep emotional attachment to objects, faulty beliefs about their value, and genuine skills deficits. The most effective approach combines CBT with motivational interviewing, which directly targets the profound ambivalence about change that characterizes most individuals with this disorder. Treatment addresses three key domains: cognitive distortions about possessions (emotional attachment, perceived utility, aesthetic value), behavioral avoidance of discarding, and practical skills deficits in organizing, decision-making, and categorizing. Clinicians who conduct home-based sessions achieve significantly better outcomes than those who rely exclusively on office-based treatment, because the real therapeutic work happens at the site of the accumulation — sorting through actual possessions, practicing discarding in vivo, and building organizational systems in real time. Neuroimaging research further underscores the distinctness of hoarding from OCD: individuals with hoarding disorder show abnormal activity in the anterior cingulate cortex and insula specifically during decision-making tasks about their own possessions, a pattern not seen in typical OCD presentations.

Trichotillomania (Hair-Pulling Disorder)

Trichotillomania, also known as hair-pulling disorder, is classified under Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders in the DSM-5. It is a body-focused repetitive behavior disorder characterized by the recurrent, compulsive pulling out of one's own hair, resulting in noticeable hair loss.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

  1. Criterion A: Recurrent pulling out of one's hair, resulting in hair loss.
  2. Criterion B: Repeated attempts to decrease or stop hair pulling.
  3. Criterion C: The hair pulling causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
  4. Criterion D: The hair pulling or hair loss is not attributable to another medical condition (e.g., a dermatological condition).
  5. Criterion E: The hair pulling is not better explained by the symptoms of another mental disorder (e.g., attempts to improve a perceived defect in appearance in BDD).

Pulling Styles

Trichotillomania typically involves two styles of pulling: focused pulling (deliberate pulling in response to a negative emotion or urge, with full awareness) and automatic pulling (pulling that occurs outside of awareness, often during sedentary activities such as reading, watching TV, or talking on the phone). Most individuals engage in a mixture of both styles. Note that while the DSM-IV required a rising tension-before and relief-after cycle, the DSM-5 removed this criterion, as research showed many individuals do not experience this pattern.

Epidemiology and Clinical Features

Trichotillomania has a prevalence of approximately 1-2% and is significantly more common in females than males (approximately 10:1 ratio in adults). The most common sites of pulling are the scalp, eyebrows, and eyelids, though any body area with hair may be targeted. Onset is most common around puberty (ages 10-13). A subset of individuals engage in trichophagia (eating pulled hair), which can lead to trichobezoars (hairballs) requiring surgical removal -- a rare but potentially life-threatening complication.

Clinical Pearl

Habit reversal training (HRT) is the first-line behavioral treatment for trichotillomania. HRT has three core components: (1) awareness training -- learning to identify pulling urges, triggers, and behaviors in real time; (2) competing response training -- engaging in a physically incompatible behavior (e.g., clenching fists, sitting on hands) when an urge to pull arises; and (3) social support -- enlisting others to provide encouragement and reinforcement. HRT is often embedded within a broader CBT framework. SSRIs have shown inconsistent results for trichotillomania, unlike their efficacy in OCD, and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) has shown some promise as an adjunctive pharmacological agent.

Comorbidity

Trichotillomania is frequently comorbid with excoriation (skin-picking) disorder, OCD, major depressive disorder, and other anxiety disorders. Co-occurrence with excoriation disorder is particularly common, reflecting the shared classification as body-focused repetitive behavior disorders.

Excoriation (Skin-Picking) Disorder

Having just explored trichotillomania and its classification as a body-focused repetitive behavior disorder, we now turn to its close relative — a condition so frequently co-occurring with hair pulling that their shared features illuminate something fundamental about how the brain gets locked into repetitive, self-damaging loops. Consider a college student who spends an hour each evening in front of the bathroom mirror, methodically scanning her face for tiny imperfections — a barely visible bump, a clogged pore, the edge of a healing scab. She picks, squeezes, and digs with her fingernails until she has created raw, bleeding wounds far worse than anything she started with. She knows she should stop. She has promised herself, repeatedly, that tonight will be different. But the urge builds like a pressure she cannot contain, and before she fully realizes it, she is picking again. She wears heavy foundation to classes, avoids social gatherings, and has missed job interviews because her face looks too damaged. This is not vanity or a bad habit — this is excoriation disorder.

Also known as skin-picking disorder or dermatillomania, excoriation disorder earned its place as a formal diagnosis for the first time in the DSM-5, where it sits alongside trichotillomania under the Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders chapter. Like trichotillomania, the DSM-5 categorizes it as a body-focused repetitive behavior disorder — recognizing that while these conditions share the compulsive quality of OCD, they involve repetitive behaviors directed at the body rather than ritualistic responses to obsessional thoughts. Understanding this disorder matters clinically because it is far more common than most practitioners realize, causes genuine physical harm, and responds to targeted interventions when correctly identified.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

The DSM-5 criteria for excoriation disorder mirror the structure you have already learned for trichotillomania, reinforcing their shared conceptual framework. The criteria establish a clear pattern: a repetitive behavior the person cannot stop, despite wanting to, that causes real harm and cannot be better attributed to another condition. Pay particular attention to Criterion E, which requires clinicians to carefully rule out several look-alike presentations before assigning this diagnosis.

  1. Criterion A: Recurrent skin picking resulting in skin lesions.
  2. Criterion B: Repeated attempts to decrease or stop skin picking.
  3. Criterion C: The skin picking causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
  4. Criterion D: The skin picking is not attributable to the physiological effects of a substance (e.g., cocaine) or another medical condition (e.g., scabies).
  5. Criterion E: The skin picking is not better explained by symptoms of another mental disorder (e.g., delusions or tactile hallucinations in psychotic disorder, attempts to improve perceived defect in BDD, stereotypies in stereotypic movement disorder, intention to harm oneself in nonsuicidal self-injury).

Notice that Criterion B — repeated attempts to stop — distinguishes excoriation disorder from casual or occasional skin picking, which many people do without distress. The individual with excoriation disorder experiences a distressing loss of control, recognizing the behavior as problematic yet finding themselves unable to resist it. This ego-dystonic quality parallels what we see in trichotillomania and, more broadly, across the OCD spectrum.

Behavioral Pattern

Like trichotillomania, excoriation disorder involves both focused picking (deliberate, in response to negative emotions or urges) and automatic picking (outside of awareness, during sedentary activities). Think of focused picking as the person who sits down at the mirror after a stressful day at work, driven by mounting tension and an urge to "fix" something on their skin — they experience a surge of relief or satisfaction with each extraction. Automatic picking, by contrast, looks like the student who absently runs her fingers along her arms while reading a textbook, picking at bumps and scabs without even noticing until she sees blood. Many individuals alternate between both styles, sometimes within the same episode. The picking may target healthy skin, minor skin irregularities (e.g., pimples, calluses, scabs), or lesions from previous picking — creating a self-perpetuating cycle where yesterday's wounds become today's targets. Common sites include the face, arms, and hands, though individuals may pick from virtually any accessible body area. Individuals may use fingernails, tweezers, pins, or other implements. A similar tension/relief cycle may be present, though it is not required for diagnosis.

Epidemiology and Clinical Features

Excoriation disorder affects approximately 1.4% of the general population — making it notably common among the OCD-spectrum disorders and far more prevalent than many clinicians expect. The condition disproportionately affects females, who represent approximately 75% of diagnosed cases, a gender ratio that mirrors what we see in trichotillomania. Onset can occur at any age but frequently begins in adolescence, often coinciding with or following the onset of acne or other dermatological conditions — a pattern that makes intuitive sense, as normal skin changes provide initial "targets" that trigger and reinforce the picking behavior in vulnerable individuals.

The course of excoriation disorder typically follows a chronic trajectory, with symptom severity that waxes and wanes over months and years, often intensifying during periods of heightened stress, boredom, or emotional distress. Clinicians should remain alert to the significant medical complications this disorder can produce: repeated picking causes tissue damage, permanent scarring, and creates entry points for serious infection. Some individuals develop disfiguring wounds that require medical attention. The psychosocial consequences can be equally devastating — individuals may avoid intimate relationships, withdraw from social activities, or restrict their career choices to avoid exposing damaged skin. The shame and secrecy surrounding the behavior often delay help-seeking by years or even decades.

Clinical Pearl

Treatment for excoriation disorder closely parallels that for trichotillomania, and for the EPPP, remembering the shared treatment approach across body-focused repetitive behavior disorders will serve you well. Habit reversal training (HRT) combined with CBT represents the first-line psychotherapy approach. The treatment package includes three core components: awareness training teaches clients to identify triggers, high-risk situations, and the earliest sensory cues that precede picking; competing response training helps them substitute physically incompatible behaviors (such as clenching fists, handling textured objects, or pressing palms flat on a surface) when urges arise; and stimulus control strategies modify the environment to reduce opportunities for picking — for example, covering or removing magnifying mirrors, wearing gloves during high-risk activities, or keeping hands occupied with fidget tools during sedentary tasks. SSRIs are sometimes used but evidence for their efficacy in excoriation disorder remains mixed — notably weaker than the evidence supporting their use in OCD proper. N-acetylcysteine has also shown preliminary efficacy as a glutamate-modulating agent, offering a novel pharmacological avenue that researchers continue to explore.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis of excoriation disorder requires careful clinical reasoning because several other conditions can produce repetitive skin picking — but with fundamentally different motivations and mechanisms. Clinicians must distinguish excoriation disorder from nonsuicidal self-injury, in which an individual deliberately harms their skin to regulate intense emotions such as anger, emptiness, or dissociation. The key distinction lies in motivation and behavioral quality: nonsuicidal self-injury involves intentional self-harm rather than a repetitive urge-driven habit, and the individual typically selects cutting, burning, or hitting rather than the picking-and-extracting pattern seen in excoriation disorder. Similarly, when skin picking occurs in the context of BDD, the behavior is driven by a desire to fix perceived appearance flaws — the person picks because they believe something about their skin looks abnormal, not because of a compulsive urge. In psychotic disorders, picking may stem from delusions of parasitosis (the fixed belief that bugs are crawling under the skin) or tactile hallucinations — a qualitatively different experience from the urge-based picking of excoriation disorder. Finally, substance-induced picking — particularly the formication (sensation of insects crawling on the skin) produced by stimulants like cocaine and methamphetamine — must always be considered. When skin picking occurs exclusively in the context of one of these other conditions, clinicians should not assign a separate diagnosis of excoriation disorder. This principle underscores a broader diagnostic rule across the DSM-5: the specific diagnosis that best captures the primary driving mechanism takes diagnostic precedence.

EPPP Focus Points

  • GAD requires worry on more days than not for at least 6 months plus 3+ associated symptoms (only 1 for children)
  • BII phobia is unique among phobias for its vasovagal/fainting response; treated with applied tension, not relaxation
  • Panic attacks are a specifier in DSM-5, not a separate disorder -- they can occur across multiple disorders
  • Agoraphobia is now a separate diagnosis from panic disorder in DSM-5
  • OCD obsessions are ego-dystonic; OCPD traits are ego-syntonic
  • ERP (Exposure and Response Prevention) is the first-line psychotherapy for OCD
  • Mowrer's two-factor theory: classical conditioning acquires fear; operant conditioning (negative reinforcement) maintains avoidance
  • The CSTC circuit (orbitofrontal cortex, caudate, thalamus) is the key neural pathway in OCD
  • SSRIs are first-line pharmacotherapy for all anxiety disorders and OCD; higher doses are typically needed for OCD
  • Social anxiety disorder's performance only specifier is new to DSM-5
  • Separation anxiety disorder was moved from childhood-onset disorders to the anxiety chapter in DSM-5; can be diagnosed in adults (6-month duration vs. 4 weeks in children); most common anxiety disorder in children under 12
  • Body dysmorphic disorder involves preoccupation with perceived flaws not observable to others; repetitive behaviors (mirror checking, reassurance seeking); high suicide risk; CBT with ERP is first-line treatment; cosmetic procedures are generally ineffective
  • Hoarding disorder is a standalone DSM-5 diagnosis distinct from OCD; hoarding in HD is ego-syntonic (valued) vs. ego-dystonic in OCD; CBT with motivational interviewing is first-line treatment; standard ERP alone has limited efficacy
  • Trichotillomania: DSM-5 removed the tension/relief criterion; habit reversal training (HRT) is first-line behavioral treatment, not ERP; SSRIs have inconsistent efficacy unlike in OCD
  • Excoriation disorder is new to DSM-5 as a formal diagnosis; shares treatment approach with trichotillomania (HRT + CBT); must differentiate from nonsuicidal self-injury, BDD-related picking, and substance-induced picking
