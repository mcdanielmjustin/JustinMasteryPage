General Diagnostic Criteria for Personality Disorders

Most psychiatric diagnoses describe conditions that feel foreign to the person experiencing them — the person with panic disorder knows something is wrong, and the person with depression can often remember a time before the darkness descended. Personality disorders are different. They describe patterns so deeply woven into a person's sense of self that the individual may not recognize them as problematic at all. A man who has alienated every colleague he has ever worked with may genuinely believe that the problem lies entirely with others. A woman whose explosive anger has ended three successive relationships may view each breakup as proof that she is simply surrounded by untrustworthy people. This is the clinical paradox at the heart of personality pathology: the very patterns causing the most damage are the ones the patient is least likely to identify as problems.

A personality disorder is an enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual's culture, is pervasive and inflexible, has an onset in adolescence or early adulthood, is stable over time, and leads to distress or impairment. The general criteria require the pattern to be manifested in two or more of the following areas:

  1. Cognition -- ways of perceiving and interpreting self, others, and events
  2. Affectivity -- range, intensity, lability, and appropriateness of emotional response
  3. Interpersonal functioning
  4. Impulse control

Key Concept: Ego-Syntonic Nature

Personality disorder traits are typically ego-syntonic -- the individual views them as consistent with their self-image and may not recognize them as problematic. This contrasts with most Axis I disorders, where symptoms are ego-dystonic. This ego-syntonic quality often leads to poor insight and resistance to treatment, as individuals may not perceive a need for change.

The DSM-5 retains the categorical model of 10 personality disorders organized into three clusters, while also including an Alternative DSM-5 Model for Personality Disorders (AMPD) in Section III for further study.

Cluster A: Odd or Eccentric

Imagine a client who walks into your office convinced that coworkers are secretly plotting against him — not because of any evidence, but because "you can just tell by the way they look at each other." Or consider the person who has lived alone for twenty years, not out of circumstance but out of genuine preference, feeling no loneliness and no desire for companionship. Or picture someone who arranges crystals around their apartment because they believe the stones ward off negative psychic energy, and who speaks in vague, tangential ways that leave you unsure what they actually mean. These three presentations — suspiciousness, social detachment, and eccentricity — capture the core of the Cluster A personality disorders.

Cluster A personality disorders share a genetic association with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Chapter 2) — think of them as occupying a continuum with psychotic disorders, sharing features in attenuated form without crossing into frank psychosis. The following visual aid captures the key distinctions among the three Cluster A disorders.

Paranoid Personality Disorder

Think of a colleague who never shares personal information at work, not because they are private by nature but because they genuinely believe that anything they reveal will be "used against them." When a friend forgets to return a phone call, this person does not assume the friend was busy — they assume betrayal. When a partner is five minutes late, infidelity becomes the most plausible explanation. This pervasive, corrosive mistrust captures the essence of Paranoid Personality Disorder.

The core feature is a pervasive pattern of distrust and suspiciousness of others such that their motives are interpreted as malevolent, beginning by early adulthood and present across a range of contexts. Individuals with this disorder characteristically suspect — without sufficient basis — that others are exploiting, harming, or deceiving them. They doubt the loyalty and trustworthiness of friends and associates, are reluctant to confide in others for fear that information will be weaponized, and read hidden demeaning or threatening meanings into benign remarks or events. They persistently bear grudges, are quick to perceive attacks on their character or reputation that are not apparent to others, and experience recurring, unjustified suspicions about the fidelity of a spouse or sexual partner.

Clinically, it is important to recognize that individuals with Paranoid Personality Disorder are not easy therapy clients. Their suspiciousness extends naturally to the therapeutic relationship — they may question your motives, doubt your confidentiality, or interpret neutral clinical observations as veiled criticisms. Building a working alliance requires extraordinary patience, transparency, and consistency. Rather than directly challenging paranoid beliefs, effective clinicians often work alongside the client's perspective while gently introducing alternative interpretations over time.

Differential Diagnosis

Paranoid PD vs. delusional disorder (persecutory type): In paranoid PD, the suspicions are not of delusional intensity -- they are exaggerated interpretations but not fixed, false beliefs. A person with Paranoid PD might say, "I think my boss is trying to undermine me," while maintaining some capacity to consider alternative explanations; a person with persecutory delusional disorder holds an unshakable conviction that cannot be swayed by contradictory evidence. Paranoid PD vs. paranoid schizophrenia: Paranoid PD lacks hallucinations, disorganized speech, and other psychotic features. The suspiciousness in Paranoid PD, while pervasive and distressing, remains within the realm of exaggerated but non-psychotic interpretation of reality.

Schizoid Personality Disorder

While the individual with Paranoid Personality Disorder desperately monitors social relationships — scanning for betrayal and threat — the person with Schizoid Personality Disorder appears to have opted out of the social world altogether. This is not the introvert who enjoys quiet evenings but still values close friendships, nor is it the socially anxious person who longs for connection but fears rejection. The individual with Schizoid PD genuinely does not seem to want or need human closeness.

The central feature is a pervasive pattern of detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of emotional expression in interpersonal settings. Individuals with this disorder neither desire nor enjoy close relationships, including being part of a family. They almost always choose solitary activities, have little if any interest in sexual experiences with another person, take pleasure in few activities, lack close friends or confidants other than first-degree relatives, appear indifferent to the praise or criticism of others, and show emotional coldness, detachment, or flattened affect.

Picture a software developer who works remotely, lives alone, spends weekends hiking solo or assembling model ships, and when asked at a family gathering whether they feel lonely, responds with genuine puzzlement — "Why would I?" This is not depression masquerading as isolation; there is no subjective distress about the lack of relationships. This distinction matters clinically: individuals with Schizoid PD rarely present for treatment on their own because they do not experience their pattern as problematic. They are more likely to be referred by family members or employers concerned about their social withdrawal. When they do appear in clinical settings, the therapist may notice a striking emotional flatness — not the blunted affect of schizophrenia, but a constricted emotional range that makes sessions feel oddly impersonal.

Schizotypal Personality Disorder

If Paranoid PD is defined by suspicion and Schizoid PD by detachment, then Schizotypal Personality Disorder combines social difficulty with something more striking — cognitive and perceptual oddities that hover at the edges of psychosis without fully crossing the line. This is the disorder that most clearly bridges the gap between personality pathology and the schizophrenia spectrum, and for the EPPP, understanding that bridge is essential.

The core feature is a pervasive pattern of social and interpersonal deficits marked by acute discomfort with, and reduced capacity for, close relationships, along with cognitive or perceptual distortions and eccentricities of behavior. Features include ideas of reference (believing that random events carry special personal significance), odd beliefs or magical thinking inconsistent with subcultural norms (e.g., believing one can sense future events or read others' thoughts), unusual perceptual experiences (such as sensing an absent person's presence), odd thinking and speech that is vague, metaphorical, or stereotyped, suspiciousness or paranoid ideation, inappropriate or constricted affect, behavior or appearance that strikes others as odd or eccentric, a lack of close friends or confidants outside first-degree relatives, and excessive social anxiety that does not diminish with familiarity — a crucial distinguishing feature, because it suggests the anxiety is driven by paranoid fears rather than by concerns about negative evaluation.

Consider a graduate student who wears mismatched, antiquated clothing, speaks in an oddly formal and circuitous way, believes she has telepathic abilities, and avoids study groups not because she is shy but because she senses "bad energy" from certain classmates. She does not hear voices or hold fixed delusions, but her inner world is populated by quasi-mystical experiences that make her seem profoundly different from her peers. Unlike Social Anxiety Disorder — where increased familiarity typically reduces discomfort — her unease around others persists or even intensifies over time because it is rooted in suspicion rather than fear of judgment.

Clinical Pearl

Schizotypal PD has the strongest genetic link to schizophrenia and is considered part of the schizophrenia spectrum. Family studies consistently show elevated rates of schizophrenia among relatives of individuals with Schizotypal PD, and vice versa. The disorder involves quasi-psychotic symptoms (ideas of reference, magical thinking, perceptual distortions) that fall short of true psychosis — think of them as "psychosis-lite," where reality testing is strained but not broken. In the ICD-10, schizotypal disorder is classified as a psychotic disorder rather than a personality disorder, reflecting how closely aligned it is with the schizophrenia spectrum. This classification difference between DSM-5 and ICD-10 is a frequently tested point on the EPPP and highlights the dimensional nature of psychiatric classification — the boundary between severe personality pathology and psychotic disorder is not as sharp as our diagnostic categories sometimes suggest.

Cluster B: Dramatic, Emotional, or Erratic

Where Cluster A operates in the cool register of detachment and eccentricity, Cluster B runs hot — relationships that blaze and burn out, emotions swinging from adoration to rage, self-images built on grandiosity masking fragility. These presentations evoke strong countertransference and are among the most heavily tested EPPP topics in personality pathology.

Antisocial Personality Disorder (ASPD)

A pervasive pattern of disregard for and violation of the rights of others, occurring since age 15. The individual must be at least 18 years old, and there must be evidence of conduct disorder before age 15.

  • Failure to conform to social norms with respect to lawful behaviors
  • Deceitfulness (lying, using aliases, conning for profit/pleasure)
  • Impulsivity or failure to plan ahead
  • Irritability and aggressiveness (repeated fights/assaults)
  • Reckless disregard for safety of self or others
  • Consistent irresponsibility (work, financial obligations)
  • Lack of remorse

ASPD vs. Psychopathy

Psychopathy (as measured by Hare's PCL-R) is a related but distinct construct. The PCL-R captures two factors: Factor 1 (interpersonal/affective traits: glibness, grandiosity, lack of empathy, shallow affect) and Factor 2 (antisocial lifestyle: impulsivity, irresponsibility, criminal versatility). Most individuals with psychopathy meet criteria for ASPD, but most individuals with ASPD do not meet criteria for psychopathy (approximately 50-80% of inmates meet ASPD criteria, but only about 15-25% score high on the PCL-R).

Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

A pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, and affects, and marked impulsivity. Five or more of nine criteria are required:

  1. Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment
  2. Pattern of unstable and intense interpersonal relationships (idealization/devaluation, "splitting")
  3. Identity disturbance: markedly unstable self-image or sense of self
  4. Impulsivity in at least two areas that are potentially self-damaging
  5. Recurrent suicidal behavior, gestures, threats, or self-mutilating behavior
  6. Affective instability due to marked reactivity of mood
  7. Chronic feelings of emptiness
  8. Inappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger
  9. Transient, stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms

Clinical Pearl

Linehan's (1993) biosocial theory proposes that BPD arises from the transaction between biological vulnerability to emotional dysregulation and an invalidating environment (one that erratically, inappropriately, or negligently responds to private experiences). Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) is the best-supported treatment for BPD and targets emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, and mindfulness. Kernberg's object relations approach emphasizes splitting as the primary defense mechanism in BPD.

Clinical Vignette: Borderline Personality Disorder

A 28-year-old woman presents to the emergency department after superficial lacerations to her forearms following a heated phone call with her boyfriend. She describes him as "the most wonderful person alive" during the intake, but by the end of the evaluation — after he does not return her call — she refers to him as "a monster who never cared." She reports chronic feelings of emptiness, a pattern of intense but short-lived relationships, and three prior emergency visits for self-harm, each triggered by perceived abandonment. Her emotional shifts are rapid and extreme: within a single interview, she transitions from tearful desperation to cold anger to warm gratitude toward the treating clinician. This presentation — the frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, the idealization-devaluation cycle, the identity disturbance, and the impulsive self-harm — illustrates the core features of BPD and the importance of recognizing the pattern early to avoid diagnostic confusion with mood disorders or acute stress reactions.

Histrionic Personality Disorder

A pervasive pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking. Features include discomfort when not the center of attention, inappropriately seductive or provocative behavior, shallow and rapidly shifting emotions, use of physical appearance to draw attention, impressionistic speech lacking detail, self-dramatization, suggestibility, and considering relationships more intimate than they are.

The DSM-5 acknowledges considerable diagnostic overlap between antisocial personality disorder and histrionic personality disorder, which can create differential diagnostic challenges. Both disorders share core features of impulsivity, manipulativeness, superficiality in relationships, and seductive behavior. However, the underlying motivations and broader clinical presentations differ substantially. In ASPD, manipulation serves instrumental goals such as obtaining power, material gain, or dominance over others, and is accompanied by disregard for rights and welfare of others. In histrionic PD, manipulative and seductive behaviors are driven primarily by the desire to be the focus of attention and to elicit emotional responses from others. Additionally, ASPD is marked by aggression, deceitfulness, and a pattern of legal violations, whereas histrionic PD centers on theatrical emotionality, suggestibility, and exaggerated expression of feelings.

Shared Feature ASPD Presentation Histrionic PD Presentation
Impulsivity Reckless, risk-taking; failure to plan ahead Rapidly shifting emotions; acting on immediate desires for attention
Manipulativeness Conning others for profit or personal power Using emotional displays or seduction to maintain attention
Seductiveness Instrumental; exploitative toward others Inappropriately provocative; driven by need to be noticed
Superficiality Glib, shallow charm used to deceive Impressionistic speech; considers relationships closer than they are

Clinical Note

When both sets of criteria are met, the DSM-5 permits dual diagnosis. Historically, ASPD has been diagnosed more frequently in males and histrionic PD more frequently in females, raising concerns about gender bias in clinical application. Clinicians should base diagnostic decisions on behavioral criteria rather than gender-based assumptions about presentation.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder

A pervasive pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration, and lack of empathy. Features include grandiose sense of self-importance, preoccupation with fantasies of success/power/beauty, belief in being special, requiring excessive admiration, sense of entitlement, interpersonal exploitation, lack of empathy, envy of others or belief that others are envious, and arrogant behaviors/attitudes.

EPPP Example

Kohut's self psychology conceptualizes narcissistic pathology as resulting from empathic failures by early caregivers (selfobjects), leading to a fragmented self. Treatment involves providing empathic mirroring and idealizing transferences. Kernberg, in contrast, views narcissistic personality as a pathological grandiose self that defends against underlying aggression and envy, requiring confrontation of defenses in treatment.

EPPP Focus Points

  • Personality disorders are ego-syntonic; most clinical disorders are ego-dystonic
  • Cluster A (odd/eccentric): Paranoid, Schizoid, Schizotypal — genetically linked to schizophrenia spectrum
  • Cluster B (dramatic/erratic): Antisocial, Borderline, Histrionic, Narcissistic
  • Schizotypal PD has the strongest genetic link to schizophrenia; classified as psychotic disorder in ICD-10
  • ASPD requires age 18+ with evidence of conduct disorder before age 15
  • Psychopathy (PCL-R) is narrower than ASPD; Factor 1 (interpersonal/affective) vs. Factor 2 (antisocial lifestyle)
  • BPD core features: splitting, abandonment fears, identity disturbance, affective instability
  • DBT (Linehan) is the gold-standard treatment for BPD; biosocial theory: biological vulnerability + invalidating environment
  • Kohut (empathic mirroring) vs. Kernberg (confrontation of defenses) for narcissistic pathology
  • ASPD and Histrionic PD share impulsivity, manipulativeness, and superficiality but differ in underlying motivation
