Overview of Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders

A lawyer who opens a bottle of wine every evening "to relax" now finds herself needing two bottles to achieve the same effect — and her hands tremble when she tries to go without. A college student who started using prescription stimulants to study for finals is now buying them off the street because he cannot focus without them. A retired postal worker visits the casino three times a week, has drained her retirement savings, and hides the bank statements from her husband. These three individuals present very differently, yet each illustrates how substances and reinforcing behaviors can hijack the brain's reward circuitry, transforming voluntary use into compulsive pursuit despite devastating consequences.

The DSM-5 made significant changes to the diagnosis of substance-related disorders. The most notable change was the elimination of the abuse/dependence distinction from DSM-IV, replacing it with a single substance use disorder (SUD) diagnosis on a continuum of severity. The DSM-5 also added gambling disorder as the first non-substance behavioral addiction and removed the legal problems criterion while adding craving.

DSM-5 Substance Use Disorder Criteria

A problematic pattern of substance use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, manifested by at least 2 of 11 criteria within a 12-month period. Severity: Mild (2-3 criteria), Moderate (4-5 criteria), Severe (6+ criteria). The 11 criteria cluster into four groups: impaired control, social impairment, risky use, and pharmacological criteria.

The 11 SUD Diagnostic Criteria

Impaired Control (Criteria 1-4)

  1. Taking the substance in larger amounts or over a longer period than intended
  2. Persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down or control use
  3. Great deal of time spent obtaining, using, or recovering from the substance
  4. Craving or a strong desire to use the substance (new in DSM-5)

Social Impairment (Criteria 5-7)

  1. Recurrent use resulting in failure to fulfill major role obligations
  2. Continued use despite persistent social or interpersonal problems
  3. Important activities given up or reduced because of use

Risky Use (Criteria 8-9)

  1. Recurrent use in situations in which it is physically hazardous
  2. Continued use despite knowledge of having a physical or psychological problem caused or exacerbated by the substance

Pharmacological Criteria (Criteria 10-11)

  1. Tolerance: Need for markedly increased amounts to achieve desired effect, or markedly diminished effect with continued use of the same amount
  2. Withdrawal: The characteristic withdrawal syndrome for the substance, or the substance is taken to relieve or avoid withdrawal symptoms

Clinical Pearl

Tolerance and withdrawal are neither necessary nor sufficient for an SUD diagnosis. A person taking prescribed opioids after surgery may develop tolerance and withdrawal but would not necessarily have an SUD. Conversely, a person can meet criteria for SUD (e.g., 2-3 social/behavioral criteria) without exhibiting tolerance or withdrawal. The DSM-5 notes that tolerance and withdrawal criteria should not be counted when substances are taken solely under appropriate medical supervision.

Alcohol Use Disorder

Alcohol causes more medical, social, and economic harm worldwide than any other substance. Its legal status and cultural embeddedness mean the transition from recreational use to clinical disorder often goes unrecognized for years.

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is the most commonly diagnosed SUD. Lifetime prevalence in the United States is approximately 29% (Grant et al., 2015). Genetic factors account for approximately 50-60% of the variance in risk for AUD.

Alcohol Withdrawal

Alcohol withdrawal occurs after cessation or reduction of heavy, prolonged use. Symptoms begin within 6-24 hours after the last drink and can include:

  • Autonomic hyperactivity: sweating, tachycardia, elevated blood pressure
  • Hand tremor
  • Insomnia, nausea/vomiting
  • Psychomotor agitation, anxiety
  • Transient visual, tactile, or auditory hallucinations or illusions
  • Generalized tonic-clonic seizures (typically 12-48 hours post-cessation)

Clinical Note: Distinguishing Alcohol Withdrawal from Other Conditions

When evaluating a patient for alcohol withdrawal, clinicians should carefully differentiate its symptom profile from other substance-related presentations. The core features of alcohol withdrawal — autonomic hyperactivity, tremor, agitation, anxiety, seizures, and perceptual disturbances — reflect CNS hyperexcitability following removal of alcohol's GABAergic inhibitory effects. Notably, nystagmus (involuntary rhythmic eye movements) is not a characteristic feature of the DSM-5 alcohol withdrawal syndrome. Nystagmus is instead a hallmark of alcohol intoxication and is also prominently associated with Wernicke's encephalopathy (as part of the ophthalmoplegia component of the classic triad) and with phencyclidine (PCP) intoxication. On the EPPP, questions may present a list of symptoms and ask which is least consistent with alcohol withdrawal — nystagmus is a strong distractor because of its association with other alcohol-related conditions, but it does not appear among the DSM-5 withdrawal criteria. This distinction is clinically meaningful: the presence of nystagmus in a patient presumed to be in withdrawal should prompt evaluation for concurrent Wernicke's encephalopathy or other etiologies rather than being attributed to the withdrawal syndrome itself.

Delirium Tremens (DTs)

Delirium tremens is the most severe form of alcohol withdrawal, occurring in approximately 5% of individuals undergoing withdrawal, typically 48-72 hours after the last drink. Features include profound confusion, vivid hallucinations (often visual -- classically insects or small animals), autonomic instability, agitation, and fever. DTs carry a mortality rate of up to 5% without treatment. Treatment involves benzodiazepines (especially long-acting agents like chlordiazepoxide or diazepam).

Alcohol-Related Medical Conditions

  • Wernicke's encephalopathy: Acute condition caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency. Classic triad: confusion, ataxia, and ophthalmoplegia (eye movement abnormalities). A medical emergency requiring IV thiamine.
  • Korsakoff's syndrome: Chronic condition following untreated Wernicke's. Characterized by severe anterograde amnesia (inability to form new memories) and confabulation. Often irreversible.
  • Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders: Leading preventable cause of intellectual disability. Features include facial dysmorphology, growth deficiency, and CNS dysfunction.

An additional point of diagnostic significance is that when Korsakoff syndrome develops as a consequence of chronic alcohol misuse, the DSM-5 classifies this condition as alcohol-induced major neurocognitive disorder, amnestic-confabulatory type. This classification reflects the fact that the persistent memory impairment—marked by profound difficulty encoding new information alongside a tendency to fill memory gaps with fabricated or distorted accounts—constitutes a form of major neurocognitive decline directly attributable to the neurotoxic effects of sustained alcohol consumption and the resulting thiamine (B1) depletion. Clinicians should note that while the Wernicke-Korsakoff sequence is the most commonly encountered etiology, thiamine deficiency from other causes (e.g., malnutrition, hyperemesis gravidarum, gastric bypass surgery) can produce an identical clinical picture. For EPPP purposes, recognizing the DSM-5 diagnostic label and understanding that Korsakoff syndrome represents an irreversible endpoint rather than an acute, treatable condition is essential.

Opioid Use Disorder

While alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening through seizures, the opioid crisis has claimed lives primarily through a different mechanism: respiratory depression during overdose. The trajectory from a legitimate prescription for back pain to heroin use to a fatal fentanyl overdose has become tragically common, making opioid use disorder one of the most urgent public health challenges of the current era and a critical topic for the EPPP.

Opioid use disorder involves problematic use of opioids (heroin, prescription painkillers such as oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl, or morphine). Opioids act on mu, delta, and kappa receptors, with mu receptor activation producing analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, and physical dependence.

Opioid Withdrawal

Opioid withdrawal is intensely uncomfortable but generally not life-threatening (unlike alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal). Symptoms emerge within hours of last use and include:

  • Dysphoric mood, nausea/vomiting, muscle aches
  • Lacrimation (tearing), rhinorrhea (runny nose)
  • Pupillary dilation, piloerection ("goosebumps"), sweating
  • Diarrhea, yawning, fever, insomnia

EPPP Example

Pharmacological treatments for opioid use disorder: Methadone (full mu-opioid agonist; reduces cravings and withdrawal; dispensed through certified clinics). Buprenorphine (partial mu-opioid agonist; lower abuse potential than methadone; can be prescribed in office settings; often combined with naloxone as Suboxone). Naltrexone (opioid antagonist; blocks euphoric effects; available as daily oral or monthly injectable [Vivitrol]; requires full detoxification before initiation to avoid precipitated withdrawal).

Clinical Note

The symptom profile of opioid withdrawal closely resembles that of a severe case of influenza. Shared features include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, diffuse muscle aches, fever, chills, and general malaise. This overlap is clinically significant for two reasons: first, individuals in early withdrawal may present to emergency departments or primary care settings believing they have the flu, potentially delaying appropriate substance use treatment; second, clinicians who are not attuned to this similarity may fail to screen for opioid dependence in patients presenting with flu-like complaints, particularly when the presentation occurs outside of typical influenza season. The mnemonic of opioid withdrawal as a "flu-like" syndrome is a useful heuristic for the EPPP, helping to distinguish it from the autonomic hyperactivity and seizure risk characteristic of alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal.

Opioid Overdose Management

Over 80,000 Americans died from opioid overdoses in 2021 — roughly one death every six and a half minutes. Where opioid withdrawal is intensely uncomfortable but rarely fatal, overdose kills rapidly. Recognizing the overdose triad and understanding naloxone administration are essential clinical competencies.

The Opioid Overdose Triad

A young adult slumped in a chair, breathing four or five times per minute, pupils constricted to pinpoints, unresponsive to sternal rubbing. This characteristic triad is virtually diagnostic:

  1. Respiratory depression (slow, shallow, or absent breathing) -- the primary cause of death in opioid overdose
  2. Miosis (pinpoint pupils) -- highly characteristic of opioid toxicity
  3. Decreased consciousness (ranging from sedation to coma)

Of these three signs, respiratory depression is by far the most dangerous. Opioids act on mu-receptors in the brainstem's respiratory centers, suppressing the body's automatic drive to breathe. Death from overdose is almost always death from suffocation — the brain simply stops sending the signal to inhale. This is why rapid intervention focuses first and foremost on restoring adequate breathing, whether through rescue breathing or pharmacological reversal. The pinpoint pupils, meanwhile, serve as an invaluable diagnostic clue: most other causes of unresponsiveness — such as benzodiazepine overdose, head trauma, or hypoglycemia — do not produce miosis, making this sign a reliable indicator that opioids are involved.

Naloxone (Narcan)

The discovery that opioid overdose could be pharmacologically reversed was one of the most important breakthroughs in emergency medicine. Naloxone (brand name Narcan) is a pure opioid antagonist that rapidly reverses the effects of opioid overdose by competitively binding to mu-opioid receptors, displacing the opioid molecules that are suppressing respiration and consciousness. Think of it like a master key that fits perfectly into the same lock the opioid is occupying — naloxone slides in, pushes the opioid out, and blocks its effects — but crucially, naloxone itself produces no opioid activity. It unlocks the door without opening it. Key clinical features include:

  • Onset of action within 2-5 minutes when administered intravenously (slightly longer intranasally or intramuscularly)
  • Duration of action is 30-90 minutes, which is often shorter than the duration of action of the opioid itself -- therefore, repeated doses may be necessary to prevent recurrence of respiratory depression
  • Administration can precipitate acute opioid withdrawal in dependent individuals, but this is not life-threatening
  • Available in intranasal, intramuscular, intravenous, and subcutaneous formulations; widely available without a prescription in many states

The mismatch between naloxone's relatively short duration and the longer duration of many opioids — particularly fentanyl and methadone — is one of the most clinically critical points to remember. A person who has been successfully revived with naloxone can slip back into respiratory depression thirty to sixty minutes later as the naloxone wears off while the opioid remains active in the body. This is why every person who receives naloxone must be transported to an emergency department for continued monitoring, even if they appear fully alert after the initial dose. Clinicians should also be aware that when naloxone precipitates withdrawal in a dependent individual, the person may wake up agitated, confused, and even combative — an understandable but sometimes startling response that first responders must be prepared to manage with de-escalation rather than additional sedation.

Clinical Pearl

Good Samaritan laws have been enacted in most U.S. states to encourage bystanders to call emergency services and administer naloxone during an overdose without fear of legal prosecution for drug-related offenses. These laws are an important public health tool in the opioid epidemic. Psychologists should be familiar with naloxone access and Good Samaritan protections in their jurisdiction. In clinical practice, this means that when working with clients who use opioids — or their family members — psychologists can proactively discuss naloxone availability and reassure concerned loved ones that calling 911 during an overdose will not result in criminal charges in most jurisdictions. Some clinicians incorporate naloxone education and distribution into harm-reduction safety planning, ensuring that every client at risk for overdose, and their close contacts, know how to recognize the overdose triad and administer naloxone before emergency services arrive.

Cannabis, Stimulants, and Other Substances

Cannabis Use Disorder

Cannabis use disorder is recognized in DSM-5 with the same 11-criterion framework. Cannabis withdrawal is now an official diagnosis in DSM-5, occurring within approximately 1 week of cessation of heavy use. Symptoms include irritability/anger, nervousness/anxiety, sleep difficulty, decreased appetite/weight loss, restlessness, depressed mood, and at least one physical symptom (abdominal pain, shakiness, sweating, fever, chills, headache).

Stimulant Use Disorder

Includes cocaine, amphetamines, and other stimulant-type substances. Stimulants increase dopamine in the mesolimbic reward pathway. Stimulant withdrawal ("crash") is characterized by dysphoric mood, fatigue, vivid unpleasant dreams, insomnia or hypersomnia, increased appetite, and psychomotor retardation or agitation.

Hallucinogen-Related Disorders

Hallucinogens encompass a broad category of substances including LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), psilocybin, mescaline, MDMA, and phencyclidine (PCP). These substances produce profound alterations in perception, mood, and cognition. While acute intoxication effects are generally time-limited, one clinically significant condition can persist long after substance use has ceased.

Hallucinogen Persisting Perception Disorder (HPPD)

HPPD involves the re-experiencing of perceptual disturbances that were originally encountered during hallucinogen intoxication, occurring after the substance has been fully eliminated from the body. These spontaneous recurrences — sometimes called "flashbacks" — are predominantly visual in nature. Common visual phenomena include geometric hallucinations, trails behind moving objects ("tracers"), halos around lights, afterimages that persist longer than normal, intensified colors, macropsia or micropsia (objects appearing larger or smaller), and flickering in the peripheral visual field. These perceptual disruptions cause clinically significant distress or functional impairment and are not attributable to another medical condition (e.g., epilepsy, migraine aura) or psychiatric disorder. HPPD can emerge weeks, months, or even years after the last use of a hallucinogenic substance, and episodes may be triggered by stress, fatigue, entry into dark environments, or use of other substances such as cannabis.

Clinical Note

HPPD should be distinguished from psychotic disorders and from normal brief flashback experiences. In HPPD, reality testing remains intact — the individual recognizes that the perceptual disturbances are drug-related effects rather than veridical perceptions. This preserved insight differentiates HPPD from hallucinogen-induced psychotic disorder, in which the person may lack awareness that the experiences are substance-related. PCP (phencyclidine) is particularly notable for producing psychotic-like symptoms — including paranoia, aggression, and analgesia — that can closely mimic schizophrenia, and PCP intoxication is associated with vertical and horizontal nystagmus, a distinguishing physical sign.

Tobacco (nicotine) withdrawal deserves additional clinical attention given the high prevalence of tobacco use disorders. The DSM-5 recognizes tobacco withdrawal as a diagnosable condition that emerges within 24 hours of abrupt cessation or significant reduction in nicotine intake after a period of daily use lasting at least several weeks. The characteristic symptom profile includes irritability, frustration, or anger; anxiety; difficulty concentrating; increased appetite and weight gain; restlessness; depressed mood; and insomnia. These symptoms typically peak within the first week and may persist for several weeks, though cravings can continue for months. While tobacco withdrawal is not medically dangerous, the dysphoria and cognitive disruption it produces are major contributors to relapse, which is why pharmacological aids such as nicotine replacement therapy, bupropion, and varenicline are frequently employed to attenuate withdrawal severity and support cessation efforts.

Tolerance, Dependence, and Key Concepts

Having surveyed the major substance classes — from alcohol and opioids to cannabis, stimulants, and tobacco — you've likely noticed that certain concepts keep appearing across categories: tolerance, dependence, withdrawal, compulsive use. These terms are foundational to understanding substance use disorders, yet they are frequently confused — not just by the general public, but by healthcare professionals, insurance companies, and even some clinicians. The confusion carries real consequences. A patient on long-term opioid therapy for chronic pain who develops physical dependence may be incorrectly labeled as "addicted," leading to abrupt medication discontinuation and unnecessary suffering. Conversely, a patient showing clear behavioral signs of a substance use disorder may be undertreated because they haven't yet developed obvious physical withdrawal symptoms. Clarifying these distinctions is essential for accurate diagnosis, ethical treatment, and effective communication on the EPPP and in clinical practice.

Consider a patient who has been prescribed benzodiazepines for a documented anxiety disorder for the past two years. Over time, her physician has needed to increase the dose to maintain the same anxiolytic effect. If the medication is abruptly discontinued, she experiences tremors, insomnia, and heightened anxiety. Does she have a substance use disorder? Not necessarily — and understanding why requires carefully distinguishing three concepts that are related but not synonymous: tolerance, physical dependence, and addiction.

Tolerance vs. Dependence vs. Addiction

Tolerance: Needing more of a substance to achieve the same effect, or diminished effect with the same amount. This is a neuroadaptive process in which the brain adjusts to the repeated presence of a substance by downregulating receptors or altering neurotransmitter release. Physical dependence: A physiological state in which withdrawal symptoms occur upon cessation; can occur with appropriate medical use and does not necessarily indicate a SUD. The benzodiazepine patient described above illustrates this well — her body has adapted to the drug's presence, and abrupt removal creates a rebound state. Addiction (SUD): A behavioral pattern of compulsive substance use despite adverse consequences, involving impaired control, social impairment, and risky use. The DSM-5 avoids the term "addiction" in diagnostic labels to reduce stigma, using "substance use disorder" with severity specifiers instead. What distinguishes a SUD from mere physical dependence is the presence of behavioral and psychological features — craving, loss of control, continued use despite harm, and neglect of important life roles.

Why does this distinction matter so much clinically? Imagine two patients, both physically dependent on opioids. Patient A takes prescribed oxycodone exactly as directed following a spinal surgery, has developed tolerance and would experience withdrawal if the medication were stopped abruptly, but has never escalated the dose without physician guidance, never sought opioids from other sources, and continues to function well at work and home. Patient B, by contrast, began with a similar prescription but now takes far more than prescribed, has visited multiple emergency departments seeking additional prescriptions, has lost her job due to impaired functioning, and continues using despite a near-fatal overdose. Both patients are physically dependent; only Patient B meets criteria for an opioid use disorder. Collapsing these categories together — treating dependence as synonymous with addiction — leads to clinical errors in both directions.

Cross-Tolerance and Cross-Dependence

Once you understand that tolerance and dependence are neuroadaptive processes tied to specific receptor systems, a logical next question emerges: what happens when two different substances act on the same receptor system? This is where the clinically important phenomena of cross-tolerance and cross-dependence come into play, and understanding them is essential for both the EPPP and safe clinical practice.

Cross-tolerance occurs when tolerance to one substance extends to another substance within the same pharmacological class. Because alcohol and benzodiazepines both enhance inhibitory neurotransmission through GABA-A receptors, a person with chronic heavy alcohol use will often require higher-than-typical doses of benzodiazepines to achieve a therapeutic effect — their brain has already downregulated the very receptor system these medications target. This phenomenon has direct implications for surgical anesthesia, emergency sedation, and anxiety treatment in patients with alcohol use histories.

Cross-dependence is the related but distinct phenomenon in which one substance can suppress withdrawal symptoms from another substance in the same pharmacological class. This principle is the foundation for one of the most common protocols in addiction medicine: using long-acting benzodiazepines such as chlordiazepoxide or diazepam to manage alcohol withdrawal. Because both substances act on GABA-A receptors, the benzodiazepine can substitute for alcohol's neurochemical effects, preventing the dangerous hyperexcitability — including seizures and delirium tremens — that characterizes acute alcohol withdrawal. Similarly, methadone (a long-acting opioid agonist) can suppress withdrawal in a patient dependent on short-acting opioids like heroin, precisely because of cross-dependence within the opioid receptor system. Recognizing these pharmacological relationships is critical for understanding both the rationale behind medically assisted detoxification and the risks of polysubstance use among patients who combine drugs within the same class.

Neurobiology of Addiction

Why does a person continue using a substance that has cost them their job, their marriage, and their health? The answer lies not in moral weakness but in the neurobiology of reward, learning, and compulsion. Understanding how substances hijack the brain's motivational circuitry transforms addiction from a puzzle of willpower into a comprehensible — and treatable — brain disorder.

The Mesolimbic Dopamine Pathway

The mesolimbic dopamine pathway (ventral tegmental area to nucleus accumbens) is the primary neural circuit involved in reward and reinforcement. All substances of abuse, despite their different mechanisms of action, ultimately increase dopamine transmission in the nucleus accumbens. This "final common pathway" explains why diverse substances can all produce addiction (Koob & Volkow, 2016).

  • Stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines): Directly increase dopamine by blocking reuptake or increasing release
  • Opioids: Disinhibit dopamine neurons by inhibiting GABAergic interneurons in the VTA
  • Alcohol: Enhances GABA and inhibits glutamate; also indirectly increases dopamine release
  • Nicotine: Stimulates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on dopamine neurons
  • Cannabis: Activates CB1 receptors, which indirectly modulate dopamine release

Neuroadaptation and Allostasis

With chronic substance use, the brain undergoes neuroadaptation: the reward system becomes downregulated (fewer dopamine receptors, reduced dopamine release), leading to tolerance and a new hedonic set point. Koob's allostatic model proposes that addiction involves a shift from positive reinforcement (seeking pleasure) to negative reinforcement (avoiding withdrawal and dysphoria). The extended amygdala and stress systems (CRF, norepinephrine) become hyperactivated, driving the compulsive drug-seeking characteristic of addiction.

EPPP Example

The opponent-process theory (Solomon & Corbit, 1974) explains drug tolerance and withdrawal through opposing emotional processes. The initial positive effect (a-process) triggers a compensatory negative effect (b-process). With repeated use, the b-process strengthens, requiring more substance to achieve the same positive effect (tolerance) and producing a powerful negative state when the substance is absent (withdrawal). This model applies beyond substance use to understanding any habituation to emotional stimuli.

Technology-Based Interventions for Substance Use

Advances in digital health have introduced novel delivery methods for substance use interventions, particularly for smoking cessation. A growing body of research demonstrates that text messaging interventions represent an effective modality for helping individuals quit tobacco use. Text-based programs typically deliver automated, scheduled messages that provide encouragement, coping strategies, and relapse prevention tips directly to a person's mobile phone over the course of several weeks or months.

Meta-analytic evidence indicates that text messaging programs can function as effective stand-alone treatments for smoking cessation, producing significant improvements in quit rates compared to minimal intervention or no-treatment controls. Furthermore, when text messaging is combined with other established smoking cessation approaches—such as nicotine replacement therapy, pharmacotherapy, or counseling—the combined intervention yields better outcomes than either approach delivered in isolation. This additive benefit suggests that text messaging serves as a valuable adjunct that enhances engagement, provides continuous between-session support, and reinforces motivation throughout the quit process.

These findings are consistent with the stages of change framework, as text messaging can be tailored to an individual's readiness for change—delivering consciousness-raising content during contemplation, action-oriented tips during active quit attempts, and relapse prevention strategies during maintenance. The accessibility, low cost, and scalability of text-based interventions make them particularly promising for reaching underserved populations who may face barriers to traditional face-to-face treatment.

Example

SmokefreeTXT, a program developed by the National Cancer Institute, sends automated text messages to individuals who set a quit date. Messages include motivational content, strategies for managing cravings, and tips for handling high-risk situations. Research on this and similar programs has shown that participants who receive text support are approximately 1.5 to 2 times more likely to achieve sustained abstinence compared to those who do not receive such messages.

Clinical Pearl

Motivational interviewing (MI) (Miller & Rollnick, 2013) is the therapeutic approach most closely aligned with the stages of change model. MI uses a collaborative, empathic, non-confrontational approach to strengthen a person's own motivation for change. Core principles include expressing empathy, developing discrepancy, rolling with resistance, and supporting self-efficacy. The OARS technique (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries) is fundamental to MI practice.

Gambling Disorder

Gambling disorder is the first and only non-substance behavioral addiction included in the DSM-5, placed in the Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders chapter. This placement reflects growing evidence that gambling disorder shares significant neurobiological, genetic, and clinical features with substance use disorders.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

Persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behavior leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, as indicated by 4 or more of 9 criteria within a 12-month period:

  1. Needs to gamble with increasing amounts of money to achieve the desired excitement (tolerance)
  2. Is restless or irritable when attempting to cut down or stop gambling (withdrawal)
  3. Has made repeated unsuccessful efforts to control, cut back, or stop gambling (loss of control)
  4. Is often preoccupied with gambling (e.g., reliving past gambling experiences, planning the next venture, thinking of ways to obtain money)
  5. Often gambles when feeling distressed (e.g., helpless, guilty, anxious, depressed)
  6. After losing money gambling, often returns another day to get even ("chasing" losses)
  7. Lies to conceal the extent of involvement with gambling
  8. Has jeopardized or lost a significant relationship, job, or educational/career opportunity because of gambling
  9. Relies on others to provide money to relieve desperate financial situations caused by gambling

Parallels with Substance Use Disorders

The diagnostic criteria for gambling disorder closely parallel the 11 SUD criteria, including tolerance (needing more to achieve excitement), withdrawal (restlessness when stopping), loss of control, and continued engagement despite negative consequences. This parallel is deliberate and reflects the shared underlying pathology.

Neurobiology

Neuroimaging and neuropharmacological studies have demonstrated that gambling disorder shares key neurobiological features with substance addiction. The dopamine reward system -- particularly the mesolimbic pathway from the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens -- is activated during gambling in ways that closely parallel activation seen with substance use. Individuals with gambling disorder show reduced striatal dopamine receptor availability, impaired prefrontal cortex function (contributing to poor decision-making), and heightened responsivity to gambling-related cues.

Prevalence and Clinical Features

Prevalence of gambling disorder is estimated at 0.4-1% of adults. Males are affected more frequently than females, though the gender gap is narrowing. Gambling disorder has high comorbidity with substance use disorders, mood disorders, and personality disorders (particularly antisocial personality disorder). Severity specifiers follow the SUD model: mild (4-5 criteria), moderate (6-7 criteria), severe (8-9 criteria).

EPPP Example

Internet gaming disorder was not included as an official DSM-5 diagnosis but was placed in Section III ("Conditions for Further Study"). Gambling disorder remains the only recognized behavioral addiction in the DSM-5. The decision to include gambling disorder was based on decades of evidence showing that it parallels substance addiction in clinical presentation, comorbidity, neurobiology, physiology, and treatment response.

  • Stages of Change: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance
  • Motivational interviewing is non-confrontational, uses OARS technique, aligned with stages of change
  • Opioid overdose triad: respiratory depression + miosis (pinpoint pupils) + decreased consciousness
  • Naloxone (Narcan): opioid antagonist; onset 2-5 minutes IV; may need repeated doses; Good Samaritan laws protect bystanders
  • Gambling disorder: first non-substance behavioral addiction in DSM-5; 4+ of 9 criteria in 12 months
  • Gambling disorder criteria parallel SUD (tolerance, withdrawal, loss of control); neurobiology involves dopamine reward system; prevalence 0.4-1% adults
