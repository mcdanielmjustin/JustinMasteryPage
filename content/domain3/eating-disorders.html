Overview of Feeding and Eating Disorders

For millions of individuals, eating becomes a battleground — rigid control, chaotic bingeing, terrifying loss of autonomy, or all three. These are not lifestyle choices; they are serious psychiatric disorders carrying the highest mortality rates in the DSM, capable of destroying cardiovascular function, bone density, fertility, and life itself.

The DSM-5 feeding and eating disorders chapter covers conditions characterized by persistent disturbances in eating behavior that alter food consumption or absorption and cause significant impairment in physical health or psychosocial functioning. Anorexia nervosa, as we will see, carries particular lethality.

Key Concept: Transdiagnostic Model

Fairburn's (2003) transdiagnostic model proposes that eating disorders share a core psychopathology: the over-evaluation of eating, shape, and weight, and their control. This model is the basis for enhanced cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT-E), which can be applied across eating disorder diagnoses.

Anorexia Nervosa (AN)

The transdiagnostic over-evaluation of shape and weight reaches its most physically dangerous expression in anorexia nervosa. A 16-year-old cross-country runner begins skipping meals after her coach praises her lean frame. She cuts entire food groups, exercises through injuries. By the time she reaches the ED with a resting heart rate of 38 bpm at 85 pounds, she still believes her thighs are too large. Anorexia nervosa is characterized by restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight, intense fear of gaining weight, and a profound disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced.

The pursuit of thinness often feels ego-syntonic — patients frequently resist intervention. This entanglement of biology, cognition, and identity makes AN among the most difficult psychiatric conditions to treat and, as we will see, the most lethal.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

Three core criteria capture behavioral restriction, motivational disturbance, and cognitive-perceptual disruption.

  1. Criterion A: Restriction of energy intake relative to requirements, leading to significantly low body weight in the context of age, sex, developmental trajectory, and physical health
  2. Criterion B: Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, or persistent behavior that interferes with weight gain, even though at a significantly low weight
  3. Criterion C: Disturbance in the way body weight or shape is experienced, undue influence of body weight/shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of the current low body weight

All three criteria must converge. A patient at dangerously low weight who recognizes the problem and wants to gain weight would not meet Criterion C — distinguishing AN from weight loss due to depression (Chapter 3), medical illness, or food insecurity.

Subtypes

Subtypes are assigned based on the patient's behavior over the preceding three months and carry different medical risk profiles.

  • Restricting type: Weight loss achieved primarily through dieting, fasting, and/or excessive exercise. No recurrent binge-eating or purging in the past 3 months.
  • Binge-eating/purging type: Recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging (self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics, or enemas) during the past 3 months.

The binge-eating/purging subtype carries higher rates of impulsivity, substance use, and mood instability, plus additional medical risks from purging (esophageal tears, dental erosion). The critical differential from bulimia nervosa is body weight: AN patients are at significantly low weight, while BN patients (next section) maintain normal or above-normal weight.

Clinical Pearl

DSM-5 made several important changes to AN criteria: (1) removed the specific BMI threshold and the word "refusal" to eat, replacing it with "restriction"; (2) removed the amenorrhea criterion, recognizing that AN occurs in males and post-menopausal women; (3) Criterion B no longer requires explicit acknowledgment of fear of weight gain -- persistent behavior interfering with weight gain suffices.

These revisions matter for the EPPP. Replacing "refusal" with "restriction" removed stigmatizing language. Dropping amenorrhea broadened access (males and post-menopausal women now qualify). The Criterion B change allows diagnosis based on observable weight-interfering behavior rather than requiring the patient to verbally endorse fear of weight gain — important because many deny it.

Medical Complications

Perhaps the single most critical fact to know about anorexia nervosa is this: AN carries the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder, approximately 5-10% per decade of illness. To put this in perspective, a young woman diagnosed at age 18 faces a sobering probability of death by her late twenties simply from the medical ravages of sustained starvation or from suicide, which accounts for a significant proportion of AN-related deaths. The lethality of this disorder underscores why clinicians must take it seriously even when patients minimize their symptoms.

The medical complications of anorexia nervosa span virtually every organ system. Cardiac complications — including bradycardia (dangerously slow heart rate), hypotension, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias caused by electrolyte imbalances — represent the most common cause of medical death. The body's attempt to conserve heat in the face of lost subcutaneous fat produces lanugo hair, a fine downy growth over the body that patients often find distressing. Chronic nutritional deprivation leads to osteoporosis, which can cause fractures in young women who should be at the peak of bone density. Clinicians should also be prepared for the paradoxical danger of treatment itself: refeeding syndrome occurs when nutritional restoration triggers dangerous electrolyte shifts — particularly hypophosphatemia — as the body rapidly transitions from a catabolic to an anabolic state. This potentially fatal complication requires careful medical monitoring during the early stages of weight restoration, with slow caloric increases and frequent electrolyte monitoring.

For the EPPP, remember the key medical findings associated with AN: bradycardia, hypotension, lanugo hair, osteoporosis, electrolyte imbalances, and the risk of refeeding syndrome during treatment. Also remember that deaths result from both medical complications and suicide — a reminder that psychological assessment of suicidality remains essential even when the primary clinical focus is nutritional rehabilitation.

Bulimia Nervosa (BN)

Where AN restricts intake openly enough to produce visible emaciation, bulimia nervosa hides behind a normal-weight exterior. A college student maintains a healthy appearance to friends and family. In private, she consumes thousands of calories in under an hour, feeling unable to stop, then forces herself to vomit before anyone notices. This cycle repeats several times a week, shrouded in secrecy and shame. Bulimia nervosa is defined by recurrent binge eating followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviors aimed at preventing weight gain.

BN's invisibility makes it insidious — normal or above-normal weight means the disorder can persist for years without detection. The emotional toll is enormous: binges provide momentary relief from negative emotions, replaced immediately by self-loathing that fuels compensatory behavior. DSM-5 Criteria

Note the revised frequency threshold from DSM-IV and the weight-based distinction from AN.

  1. Recurrent episodes of binge eating (eating a definitively large amount of food in a discrete period with a sense of lack of control)
  2. Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors to prevent weight gain (purging, fasting, excessive exercise)
  3. Both occur at least once per week for 3 months (reduced from twice per week in DSM-IV)
  4. Self-evaluation is unduly influenced by body shape and weight
  5. Does not occur exclusively during episodes of AN

The DSM-5 reduced the frequency threshold from twice per week to once per week, reflecting research that individuals bingeing and purging weekly experienced comparable distress and impairment. This broadened diagnostic access beyond the "other specified" category.

EPPP Example

A key differential: AN binge-eating/purging type vs. BN. The distinguishing factor is body weight. If the individual is at a significantly low body weight and meets AN criteria, the diagnosis is AN binge-eating/purging type, even if binge-purge cycles are present. BN is diagnosed when the individual is at or above minimally normal weight. Note that BN no longer has purging/nonpurging subtypes in DSM-5. This is a frequently tested distinction — when an exam vignette describes someone who binges and purges, always check the stated body weight before choosing between AN binge-eating/purging type and BN. The behaviors may look identical; the weight is what determines the diagnosis.

Medical complications of BN are numerous and often serve as the first clinical clues that the disorder is present. These include dental erosion from repeated exposure to stomach acid — often noticed first by dentists — and parotid gland enlargement, sometimes called "chipmunk cheeks," which results from the chronic stimulation of salivary glands during purging episodes. Russell's sign, a classic physical finding, refers to calluses or scarring on the dorsum of the hand and knuckles caused by repeated contact with the teeth during self-induced vomiting. More serious complications include esophageal tears (Mallory-Weiss syndrome), which can lead to life-threatening bleeding, and metabolic alkalosis from the loss of hydrochloric acid through vomiting. Electrolyte disturbances — particularly hypokalemia — can lead to cardiac arrhythmias, making regular metabolic monitoring an essential component of care for individuals with BN.

Treatment for bulimia nervosa has a stronger evidence base than for many other eating disorders. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is considered the gold-standard psychological treatment, targeting the distorted beliefs about weight and shape that maintain the binge-purge cycle. Among pharmacological interventions, fluoxetine (Prozac) is the only FDA-approved medication for BN and is typically prescribed at higher doses (60 mg) than those used for depression. For the EPPP, remember that BN tends to have a better prognosis than AN, though relapse rates remain significant, and comorbid conditions — particularly depression, anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders — are common and must be addressed in comprehensive treatment planning.

Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)

BED shares BN's binge-eating component but diverges in one critical way: no compensatory behaviors follow. A 42-year-old accountant eats rapidly and continuously after stressful days — not from hunger, but from an automatic, trance-like compulsion. She feels ashamed afterward but does not purge. This pattern repeats two to three times weekly for a year. Binge-Eating Disorder, elevated to a full DSM-5 diagnosis from the DSM-IV appendix, involves recurrent binge eating without regular purging, fasting, or excessive exercise.

Binge-Eating Episode Features (3 or more)

Episodes must include at least three of the following markers, reflecting both loss of control and subsequent negative affect:

  • Eating much more rapidly than normal
  • Eating until feeling uncomfortably full
  • Eating large amounts when not feeling physically hungry
  • Eating alone because of embarrassment
  • Feeling disgusted, depressed, or very guilty afterward

The mnemonic RFHAG helps: Rapid eating, uncomfortably Full, not Hungry, eating Alone, Guilt/disgust afterward. The secrecy and shame form a self-reinforcing cycle — eating alone to hide the behavior produces negative affect that triggers the next binge, distinguishing BED from occasional overeating.

Episodes must occur at least once per week for 3 months — the same threshold as BN, a useful exam anchor. Marked distress regarding binge eating must also be present, distinguishing BED from unconcerned holiday overeating.

From an epidemiological standpoint, BED is the most common eating disorder in the United States, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 2.8% — more common than Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa combined. Its gender ratio is also more balanced than the other eating disorders, affecting roughly three women for every two men, compared to the much higher female-to-male ratios seen in AN and BN. Age of onset tends to be later as well, often emerging in late adolescence or adulthood rather than early to mid-adolescence.

A common exam pitfall: BED and obesity are associated but not synonymous. Not all BED patients are obese, and most obese individuals do not meet BED criteria. Obesity is defined by BMI; BED is defined by behavioral and emotional features. Treatment targets binge eating directly (CBT, IPT, or pharmacotherapy) rather than weight loss per se — CBT reduces binge episodes effectively even without significant weight change.

Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

ARFID is a new diagnosis in DSM-5, replacing and expanding the DSM-IV diagnosis of "feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood." It involves an eating or feeding disturbance manifested by persistent failure to meet nutritional needs, associated with significant weight loss, nutritional deficiency, dependence on enteral feeding or supplements, or marked interference with psychosocial functioning.

Key Distinction

Unlike AN, ARFID does not involve distress about body shape or weight, nor fear of weight gain. The food avoidance may be based on sensory characteristics of food (texture, color, smell), concern about aversive consequences of eating (e.g., choking, vomiting), or apparent lack of interest in eating. ARFID can occur in children and adults.

Pica and Rumination Disorder

The DSM-5 feeding and eating disorders chapter includes two additional conditions that are commonly tested: Pica and Rumination Disorder. These may co-occur with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, or other conditions.

Pica

Pica is defined as the persistent eating of nonnutritive, nonfood substances for a period of at least 1 month. The diagnostic criteria require:

  • The eating behavior is developmentally inappropriate -- mouthing and ingesting nonfood items is not considered abnormal before approximately age 2
  • The behavior is not part of a culturally sanctioned or socially normative practice
  • If the behavior occurs in the context of another mental disorder (e.g., intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder), it is sufficiently severe to warrant independent clinical attention

Pica is associated with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), pregnancy, and iron deficiency. Substances commonly ingested include dirt, clay, chalk, paper, soap, cloth, hair, string, and paint chips. Medical complications can be serious and include bowel obstruction, intestinal perforation, lead poisoning (from paint chips), parasitic infections, and dental injury.

Pica during pregnancy is a well-documented phenomenon that occurs across diverse cultural contexts. Pregnant women with pica commonly ingest substances such as clay (geophagia), coffee grounds, laundry starch (amylophagia), ice (pagophagia), chalk, and baking soda. While some of these practices may have cultural roots, they can pose significant health risks including lead exposure, intestinal blockage, and interference with nutrient absorption. The association between pica in pregnancy and iron-deficiency anemia is particularly robust, though the causal direction remains debated — iron deficiency may drive cravings for nonfood items, or the ingested substances may impair iron absorption, creating a self-reinforcing cycle. Clinicians should routinely screen for pica during prenatal assessments, as patients may not spontaneously disclose these behaviors due to embarrassment.

Rumination Disorder

Rumination Disorder involves the repeated regurgitation of food for a period of at least 1 month. Regurgitated food may be re-chewed, re-swallowed, or spit out. The diagnostic criteria require:

  • The behavior is not attributable to an associated gastrointestinal or other medical condition (e.g., gastroesophageal reflux, pyloric stenosis)
  • The behavior does not occur exclusively during the course of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, or avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)
  • If occurring in the context of another mental disorder (e.g., intellectual disability), the symptoms are sufficiently severe to warrant independent clinical attention

Rumination disorder can occur across the lifespan, from infancy through adulthood. In infants, it may present with characteristic straining and arching movements. Treatment typically involves habit reversal training (learning incompatible behaviors such as diaphragmatic breathing after meals) and behavioral interventions. Diaphragmatic breathing -- breathing deeply using the diaphragm to create abdominal pressure that counteracts regurgitation -- has the strongest evidence base for this condition.

EPPP Example

Key distinctions: Pica involves eating nonfood substances (not regurgitation) and is not diagnosed before age 2. Rumination disorder involves regurgitation of previously ingested food (not nonfood substances). Both require at least 1 month duration and both can occur across the lifespan but must be distinguished from developmentally normal behaviors and medical conditions.

Biological Factors

  • Genetics: Heritability estimates for AN are approximately 50-80%, among the highest for any psychiatric disorder. BN shows heritability of approximately 55%. Twin studies suggest shared genetic vulnerability across eating disorder diagnoses (Bulik et al., 2006).
  • Neurotransmitters: Serotonin dysregulation is implicated in both AN and BN. Individuals with BN show reduced serotonergic activity, which may contribute to impulsivity and binge eating. The efficacy of fluoxetine (the only FDA-approved medication for BN) supports serotonergic involvement.
  • Neuroendocrine changes: Leptin, ghrelin, and other appetite-regulating hormones are disrupted in eating disorders, though many changes appear to be consequences rather than causes of disordered eating.

Psychological and Sociocultural Factors

  • Perfectionism and low self-esteem: Perfectionism is a robust risk factor, particularly for AN. The combination of high standards and self-critical evaluation creates vulnerability.
  • Internalization of the thin ideal: Stice's (2002) dual-pathway model proposes that sociocultural pressure for thinness leads to body dissatisfaction, which promotes both dietary restraint (risk for AN) and negative affect (risk for binge eating).
  • Cognitive rigidity: Individuals with AN show set-shifting difficulties and weak central coherence (tendency to focus on details rather than global picture), which may be trait markers rather than state-dependent features.
  • Trauma history: Childhood sexual abuse is a nonspecific risk factor, associated with increased rates of BN and BED in particular.

EPPP Example

Treatment approaches differ by disorder: CBT-E (enhanced cognitive-behavioral therapy) is the first-line treatment for BN and BED. For AN, family-based treatment (FBT/Maudsley approach) is the best-supported treatment for adolescents, empowering parents to take charge of refeeding. For adults with AN, no single therapy has demonstrated clear superiority, though CBT-E, MANTRA (Maudsley model of anorexia treatment), and specialist supportive clinical management have the most evidence. Fluoxetine at 60 mg/day is effective for BN but medications have limited efficacy for AN.
