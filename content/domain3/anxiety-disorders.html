Overview of Anxiety Disorders

With a lifetime prevalence of approximately 31% (Kessler et al., 2005), anxiety disorders are the most common class of psychiatric illness. Before examining individual diagnoses, clinicians need a foundational distinction: fear is the emotional response to a real or perceived imminent threat, while anxiety is the anticipation of a future threat. Both activate overlapping neural circuits, yet they drive different clinical presentations.

Key Distinction: Fear vs. Anxiety

Fear involves surges of autonomic arousal necessary for fight-or-flight, thoughts of immediate danger, and escape behaviors. Anxiety involves muscle tension, vigilance in preparation for future danger, and cautious or avoidant behaviors. Both can overlap, but the distinction is clinically important for differential diagnosis.

What separates a disorder from ordinary worry is persistence (typically 6+ months), excessiveness relative to actual threat, and clinically significant distress or impairment. The DSM-5 reorganized this domain by removing OCD and PTSD into their own chapters (covered in Chapters 6 and 7), though they share features with the anxiety disorders examined here.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Imagine a client who sits in your office and tells you she worries about everything — her job performance, her children's safety, whether she paid a bill on time, whether a mole on her arm looks different than it did last week. She knows the worry is excessive. She tries to stop, but the thoughts just keep cycling. She hasn't slept well in months, her neck and shoulders are locked in a permanent knot of tension, and she snaps at her partner over trivial things. She can't point to a single triggering event or a specific feared object — the worry simply saturates every domain of her life. This pervasive, free-floating pattern of apprehension is the hallmark of Generalized Anxiety Disorder, a condition characterized by persistent and excessive worry across a variety of domains including work, health, family, finances, and everyday matters. Unlike the focused dread we see in phobias or the episodic surges of panic disorder, GAD spreads its anxious net wide — and the person finds it extraordinarily difficult to rein the worry in.

GAD exemplifies the anxiety disorder construct in its purest form: the worry itself becomes the central problem, dragging along somatic and cognitive symptoms that compound the individual's suffering. It also rarely travels alone — a comorbidity pattern explored below.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

The criteria capture both cognitive and physiological dimensions of GAD. The temporal requirement and symptom threshold are high-yield EPPP targets.

  1. Criterion A: Excessive anxiety and worry occurring more days than not for at least 6 months, about a number of events or activities.
  2. Criterion B: The individual finds it difficult to control the worry.
  3. Criterion C: The anxiety and worry are associated with three or more of the following (only one required in children): restlessness or feeling keyed up, being easily fatigued, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance.
  4. Criterion D: The disturbance causes clinically significant distress or impairment.
  5. Criterion E: Not attributable to substances or another medical condition.
  6. Criterion F: Not better explained by another mental disorder.

A few clinical details deserve emphasis here. First, notice that Criterion A demands worry across multiple events or activities — a person who worries exclusively about contamination or social evaluation likely meets criteria for a different diagnosis. Second, the "three or more" somatic/cognitive symptom threshold in Criterion C drops to just one symptom for children, reflecting the developmental reality that younger individuals may not yet articulate or manifest the full adult symptom profile. Third, muscle tension stands out among the Criterion C symptoms as particularly characteristic of GAD; many clients present initially to primary care physicians with chronic neck pain, headaches, or jaw clenching before anyone identifies the underlying anxiety disorder.

Clinical Pearl

GAD has the highest comorbidity rate of any anxiety disorder. Approximately 90% of individuals with GAD have at least one comorbid disorder, most commonly MDD. The intolerance of uncertainty model (Dugas et al., 1998) posits that individuals with GAD have difficulty tolerating ambiguous situations, which fuels the worry cycle.

In clinical practice, "pure" GAD is the exception: when you identify it, always screen for co-occurring conditions, especially major depressive disorder (see Chapter 3). The intolerance of uncertainty model offers a practical clinical lens: picture a client who Googles every minor health symptom at 2 a.m. and demands reassurance about outcomes no one can predict. That intolerance of "not knowing" drives a relentless search for certainty that paradoxically generates more worry. Therapeutic approaches informed by this model help clients gradually increase their tolerance for ambiguity through behavioral experiments involving deliberately uncertain situations.

Epidemiology

GAD affects approximately 5.7% of adults over their lifetime, with a 2:1 female-to-male ratio consistent with most anxiety disorders. Unlike phobias or social anxiety, GAD tends to develop gradually around age 30 — later than most other anxiety conditions. A 35-year-old with a years-long pattern of diffuse worry fits the typical profile; the same presentation in a 12-year-old warrants careful differential diagnosis.

Once established, GAD follows a chronic, waxing-and-waning course. Worry eases during periods of stability, then intensifies when new stressors emerge — a fluctuation that can mislead clients and clinicians into believing the problem has resolved. Treatment goals should target durable skills and, when indicated, sustained pharmacological support, not just symptom relief during a "good phase."

Clinical Note

In children and adolescents, the presentation of GAD differs somewhat from the adult form. Children with GAD are most frequently preoccupied with concerns about academic and athletic competence — worrying excessively about grades, test performance, or their ability in sports. They also commonly express fears about catastrophic events and natural disasters such as earthquakes, tornadoes, or wars. Unlike adults, who more often worry about finances, health, or job performance, children tend to focus on areas where they are being evaluated or where they perceive uncontrollable external threats. Only one somatic or cognitive symptom from Criterion C is required for diagnosis in children, compared to three for adults. Children with GAD frequently seek excessive reassurance from parents and teachers and may present as "little perfectionists" who redo assignments or become visibly distressed about minor mistakes. Recognizing these age-specific worry themes is essential for accurate identification, as children may not label their experience as "worry" and instead present with somatic complaints such as stomachaches or headaches.

Onset of separation anxiety disorder is frequently precipitated by identifiable life stressors that disrupt the child's sense of security. Common triggers include the death of a relative or family pet, a change in schools, parental divorce, a move to a new home, or an illness in the family. For example, a child who had previously attended school without difficulty may begin refusing to leave home and exhibit intense distress about being apart from her parents following the loss of a beloved pet. The experience of loss or disruption activates fears about the permanence of attachment relationships, making the child hypervigilant about potential threats to remaining attachment figures. Clinicians should routinely assess for recent psychosocial stressors when separation anxiety symptoms emerge or intensify, as identifying the precipitant can guide both case conceptualization and therapeutic intervention.

Specific Phobias

Where GAD spreads worry diffusely, specific phobias concentrate fear into a single target — and that contrast is diagnostically useful when patients present with avoidance. Specific phobia involves marked, disproportionate fear or anxiety about a specific object or situation that is actively avoided or endured with intense distress. The DSM-5 identifies five specifier subtypes:

  • Animal type (e.g., spiders, dogs, insects) -- typically onset in childhood
  • Natural environment type (e.g., heights, storms, water)
  • Blood-injection-injury (BII) type -- uniquely associated with a vasovagal response (fainting)
  • Situational type (e.g., airplanes, elevators, enclosed places)
  • Other type (e.g., choking, vomiting, loud sounds)

EPPP Example

A patient faints at the sight of blood during a medical procedure. Unlike other phobias that involve sustained sympathetic arousal, BII phobia involves an initial sympathetic spike followed by a parasympathetic rebound, producing bradycardia and hypotension. This is why applied tension (Ost & Sterner, 1987) is the treatment of choice for BII phobia, rather than standard relaxation techniques.

Specific phobias are the most common anxiety disorder, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 12.5%. They are often acquired through direct traumatic experience, observation, or informational transmission (Rachman, 1977).

Social Anxiety Disorder

Imagine a graduate student who has thoroughly prepared a case presentation for her practicum seminar. She knows the material cold — she could explain her conceptualization in her sleep. Yet as she walks toward the front of the room, her heart pounds, her face flushes, and her mind goes blank. She is certain that every person in the room can see her hands trembling, that they are judging her as incompetent. She stumbles through her presentation, then spends the rest of the day replaying every perceived misstep. This is not ordinary stage fright. This is Social Anxiety Disorder — and it is one of the most prevalent and impairing anxiety conditions clinicians encounter.

In specific phobias, the feared stimulus can often be circumvented — avoid balconies, avoid dogs. Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) presents a fundamentally different challenge: the feared stimulus is other people's judgment, and it cannot be avoided without withdrawing from social life altogether. SAD, previously termed social phobia, involves marked, persistent fear of social situations in which the individual may be scrutinized. The core dread is not the situation itself but acting in a way that invites humiliation, embarrassment, or rejection.

SAD's self-reinforcing quality makes it especially insidious. Fearing negative evaluation, the individual monitors their own words, expressions, and body language so intensely that they become less attuned to actual social cues — the friendly nods, the engaged questions. They leave each interaction convinced their worst fears were confirmed, which strengthens avoidance the next time. This internal feedback loop (formalized in the Clark & Wells model below) explains why the disorder persists even when others perceive the individual as perfectly competent.

Key Features

The diagnostic criteria capture a pattern beyond ordinary shyness: not butterflies before a meeting, but calling in sick three times this month to avoid speaking up.

  • The social situations almost always provoke fear or anxiety
  • Situations are avoided or endured with intense fear or anxiety
  • The fear is out of proportion to the actual threat posed
  • Duration of at least 6 months
  • Specify if performance only (limited to speaking or performing in public)

The six-month requirement distinguishes SAD from transient social anxiety during life transitions. Clinicians must also rule out body dysmorphic disorder (fear of scrutiny tied to a perceived physical flaw — see Chapter 6) and autism spectrum disorder (social difficulties with different underlying mechanisms — see Chapter 1).

Cognitive models (Clark & Wells, 1995) emphasize that individuals with SAD shift attention inward to monitor their own behavior and appearance, creating a distorted self-image that perpetuates anxiety in social situations.

The Clark and Wells (1995) model explains this mechanism precisely. Entering a feared situation activates negative self-beliefs ("I'm boring," "People can see how anxious I am"), which shift attention inward. The person constructs a catastrophic mental image of how they appear — visibly sweating, stammering, looking foolish — rather than attending to actual social cues. Because they rely on this distorted internal "camera" instead of external feedback, they leave convinced their worst fears were confirmed, even when listeners were engaged and supportive.

Treatment follows directly from this model: CBT for SAD uses behavioral experiments that shift attention outward, video feedback to correct distorted self-imagery, and systematic testing of catastrophic predictions against actual outcomes.

SAD has a lifetime prevalence of approximately 12%, typically beginning in the mid-teens following a history of behavioral inhibition temperament — the quiet, watchful child who clings to a parent at birthday parties. Behavioral inhibition is one of the strongest temperamental risk factors for later SAD, though not all inhibited children develop the disorder. Onset after age 25 is uncommon and should prompt evaluation for secondary causes such as depression or medical illness.

The performance only specifier (new in DSM-5) distinguishes fears restricted to public speaking or performance from the generalized form. Performance-only individuals function well in conversations and small groups; the generalized form involves fear across casual interactions, eating in front of others, and even making eye contact. Clinically, the performance-only subtype tends toward later onset, fewer comorbidities, and may respond to beta-blockers (propranolol) taken before performances — whereas generalized SAD typically requires SSRIs and comprehensive CBT as first-line treatment.

Panic Disorder and Agoraphobia

GAD, specific phobias, and SAD all involve fear directed at identifiable targets. Panic disorder introduces a different clinical experience entirely: the fear arrives without warning, without a clear trigger, and with such overwhelming physiological intensity that many individuals genuinely believe they are dying. This terrifying unpredictability drives the avoidance behaviors that often culminate in agoraphobia.

Panic Disorder

Panic disorder is characterized by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks -- abrupt surges of intense fear or discomfort that reach a peak within minutes. At least one attack must be followed by one month or more of persistent concern about additional attacks or maladaptive behavioral changes related to the attacks.

Panic Attack Symptoms (4 or more required)

  1. Palpitations, pounding heart, or accelerated heart rate
  2. Sweating
  3. Trembling or shaking
  4. Sensations of shortness of breath or smothering
  5. Feelings of choking
  6. Chest pain or discomfort
  7. Nausea or abdominal distress
  8. Feeling dizzy, unsteady, light-headed, or faint
  9. Chills or heat sensations
  10. Paresthesias (numbness or tingling)
  11. Derealization or depersonalization
  12. Fear of losing control or "going crazy"
  13. Fear of dying

Clinical Pearl

The anxiety sensitivity model (Reiss & McNally, 1985) proposes that individuals with panic disorder have elevated fear of anxiety-related sensations themselves, believing these sensations have harmful consequences. Clark's (1986) cognitive model of panic emphasizes catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations as the core mechanism.

Agoraphobia

In DSM-5, agoraphobia is diagnosed as a separate disorder from panic disorder. It involves marked fear or anxiety about two or more of the following five situations: using public transportation, being in open spaces, being in enclosed places, standing in line or being in a crowd, and being outside of the home alone. The fear is that escape might be difficult or help unavailable in the event of panic-like symptoms.

Approximately one-third to one-half of individuals with panic disorder also develop agoraphobia. Notably, agoraphobia can occur without a history of panic disorder.

EPPP Focus Points

  • GAD requires worry on more days than not for at least 6 months plus 3+ associated symptoms (only 1 for children)
  • GAD has the highest comorbidity rate of any anxiety disorder; approximately 90% have a comorbid condition
  • Specific phobias are the most common anxiety disorder (12.5% lifetime prevalence)
  • BII phobia uniquely involves a vasovagal response (fainting); treated with applied tension, not relaxation
  • Social anxiety disorder: Clark and Wells (1995) model emphasizes inward attentional shift and distorted self-image
  • Panic disorder requires recurrent, unexpected panic attacks plus 1+ month of concern or behavioral change
  • DSM-5 made agoraphobia a separate diagnosis from panic disorder; requires fear of 2+ of 5 situations
  • Anxiety sensitivity (fear of anxiety sensations) is a key vulnerability factor for panic disorder
  • Separation anxiety disorder can now be diagnosed in adults (not just children) under DSM-5
  • The performance only specifier for SAD distinguishes circumscribed public speaking fear from generalized social anxiety
