Overview of Depressive and Bipolar Disorders

The DSM-5 separates mood disorders into two distinct chapters: Depressive Disorders and Bipolar and Related Disorders. This reorganization reflects growing evidence that bipolar disorders are more closely related to schizophrenia spectrum disorders in terms of genetics, symptomatology, and neurobiology. Mood disorders collectively represent one of the leading causes of disability worldwide (WHO, 2017).

Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD)

Disruptive Mood Dysregulation Disorder (DMDD) is a depressive disorder introduced in the DSM-5 to address concerns about the overdiagnosis of pediatric bipolar disorder. DMDD is characterized by severe, recurrent temper outbursts — verbal rages or physical aggression — that are grossly out of proportion to the situation in intensity or duration. These outbursts occur, on average, three or more times per week. Crucially, between outbursts, the child's baseline mood is persistently irritable or angry for most of the day, nearly every day, and this mood is observable by others (e.g., parents, teachers, peers).

To receive a DMDD diagnosis, the pattern of chronic irritability and temper outbursts must have been present for 12 or more months, with no period of 3 or more consecutive months without all symptoms. The outbursts must occur in at least two of three settings (home, school, with peers) and must be severe in at least one setting. The diagnosis is reserved for children between ages 6 and 18, with onset before age 10. DMDD cannot be diagnosed concurrently with oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), intermittent explosive disorder, or bipolar disorder. When a child meets criteria for both ODD and DMDD, only the DMDD diagnosis should be assigned.

The creation of DMDD reflects research demonstrating that children with chronic, non-episodic irritability are at elevated risk for developing unipolar depressive and anxiety disorders in adulthood — not bipolar disorder. This distinguishes DMDD from pediatric bipolar disorder, in which mood disturbance is episodic rather than chronic and persistent.

Clinical Note

DMDD was developed specifically to reduce the rate at which chronically irritable children were being diagnosed with bipolar disorder and consequently treated with mood stabilizers or atypical antipsychotics. On the EPPP, remember that DMDD emphasizes chronic irritability between outbursts — this persistent negative mood baseline is what differentiates it from bipolar disorder, where mood elevation or irritability occurs in discrete episodes.

Mood Episode Types

A major depressive episode involves at least 5 of 9 symptoms lasting 2 or more weeks, with depressed mood or anhedonia required. A manic episode involves abnormally elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy lasting at least 7 days (or any duration if hospitalization is required). A hypomanic episode meets the same symptom criteria as mania but lasts at least 4 consecutive days and does not cause marked impairment or psychotic features.

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

The relationship between depression and medical illness is well established, but certain medical populations carry especially elevated psychiatric comorbidity. Research on patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) has consistently demonstrated a high prevalence of co-occurring psychiatric conditions, with major depressive disorder representing the most frequently identified comorbid psychiatric diagnosis in this population. Estimates suggest that approximately 20-30% of CHD patients meet diagnostic criteria for MDD at some point during their illness course—a rate roughly three times higher than in the general population.

The co-occurrence of MDD and coronary heart disease is not merely coincidental. The relationship appears to be bidirectional: depression serves as an independent risk factor for the development and progression of cardiovascular disease, while the physiological and psychosocial burden of heart disease predisposes individuals to depressive episodes. Shared pathophysiological mechanisms include elevated inflammatory markers (e.g., C-reactive protein, interleukin-6), HPA axis dysregulation with chronic cortisol elevation, autonomic nervous system dysfunction characterized by reduced heart rate variability, and platelet hyperactivation. Behavioral factors also mediate the relationship: depressed cardiac patients show lower rates of medication adherence, exercise participation, dietary modification, and cardiac rehabilitation attendance.

Clinical Note

Depression screening in cardiac patients is now recommended by the American Heart Association as part of standard cardiovascular care. Untreated depression in CHD patients is associated with a 2- to 2.5-fold increased risk of subsequent cardiac events and mortality. SSRIs (particularly sertraline) and CBT have demonstrated both safety and efficacy in treating depression among cardiac patients, making early identification and intervention a critical component of comprehensive cardiovascular care.

Comorbidity of MDD and Substance Use Disorders

Major depressive disorder frequently co-occurs with substance use disorders, and understanding patterns of comorbidity is essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. Large-scale epidemiological surveys have consistently demonstrated that individuals who meet criteria for MDD have elevated lifetime rates of co-occurring substance use disorders.

Among the various substances associated with comorbid use disorders in individuals with MDD, alcohol use disorder accounts for the largest proportion of lifetime co-occurrence. In nationally representative samples, respondents diagnosed with major depression reported alcohol use disorder as their most common lifetime comorbid substance-related diagnosis, exceeding rates of cannabis, opioid, and other drug use disorders. This pattern reflects both the high base rate of alcohol use in the general population and the bidirectional relationship between depressive symptoms and alcohol consumption. Many individuals use alcohol as a form of self-medication for negative affect, while chronic heavy drinking disrupts serotonergic and GABAergic neurotransmission in ways that promote depressive episodes.

This comorbidity has significant clinical implications. Co-occurring alcohol use disorder worsens the course of depression, reduces treatment response, and substantially elevates suicide risk. Screening for alcohol misuse should be routine in any evaluation of depressive symptoms, and integrated treatment approaches addressing both conditions simultaneously yield better outcomes than sequential treatment of each disorder in isolation.

Example

A 42-year-old man presents with a 6-month history of depressed mood, anhedonia, insomnia, and difficulty concentrating. Further assessment reveals he has been drinking 4-5 alcoholic beverages nightly "to help him sleep" and previously met criteria for alcohol use disorder in his late 20s. This pattern—where alcohol use disorder represents the most frequently co-occurring substance-related condition among those with MDD—requires integrated treatment addressing both the depression and the problematic alcohol use to optimize outcomes.

The gender distribution of MDD shifts markedly across development. Prior to puberty, rates of depression are roughly equivalent in boys and girls, with some studies suggesting slightly higher rates in prepubertal boys. However, during adolescence — particularly between ages 13 and 15 — there is a substantial increase in depression prevalence among girls, and the gender gap begins to approach the approximately 2:1 female-to-male ratio that characterizes adult MDD. Multiple factors contribute to this divergence, including hormonal changes associated with puberty (particularly fluctuations in estrogen and progesterone), increased interpersonal stress sensitivity, higher rates of rumination in adolescent girls, and greater exposure to risk factors such as sexual harassment and body image dissatisfaction. This sex difference in depression prevalence persists throughout the reproductive years and narrows somewhat after menopause.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

Five or more of the following symptoms present during the same 2-week period, representing a change from previous functioning. At least one must be (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia):

  1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day (in children/adolescents, can be irritable mood)
  2. Anhedonia -- markedly diminished interest or pleasure in all or almost all activities
  3. Significant weight change (more than 5% in a month) or appetite change
  4. Insomnia or hypersomnia nearly every day
  5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation observable by others
  6. Fatigue or loss of energy
  7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive/inappropriate guilt
  8. Diminished ability to think or concentrate, or indecisiveness
  9. Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, or a suicide attempt

The mnemonic SIG E CAPS (Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor changes, Suicidality) is commonly used to remember these criteria.

Clinical Pearl

DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion that was present in DSM-IV. Previously, depressive symptoms within 2 months of losing a loved one were excluded from an MDD diagnosis. The DSM-5 now allows MDD to be diagnosed even in the context of bereavement, recognizing that bereavement can trigger a full depressive episode.

Neurotransmitter Theories

Sleep Architecture Abnormalities in Depression

Beyond neurochemical alterations, major depressive disorder is associated with well-documented disruptions in sleep architecture as measured by polysomnography. These disturbances are among the most reliable biological markers of depression and have been extensively studied:

Sleep Parameter Change in MDD Description
REM latency Decreased The interval from sleep onset to the first REM period is shortened, meaning individuals with depression enter REM sleep more quickly than healthy controls
REM density Increased Greater frequency of rapid eye movements during REM periods, reflecting more intense REM activity
Slow-wave sleep (Stages 3 & 4) Decreased Reduced deep, restorative sleep contributes to feelings of unrefreshing sleep and daytime fatigue
Sleep latency Prolonged Increased time needed to initially fall asleep, consistent with the insomnia commonly reported by depressed patients

Decreased REM latency is considered one of the hallmark biological findings in depression and has been proposed as a potential endophenotype for the disorder. The shift toward earlier and more intense REM sleep may reflect underlying cholinergic-aminergic imbalance. These findings also inform the rationale for sleep deprivation therapy, in which total or selective REM sleep deprivation can produce rapid but temporary improvement in depressive symptoms. Many antidepressant medications — particularly SSRIs and TCAs — suppress REM sleep and increase REM latency, which may partially mediate their therapeutic effects.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that depression is associated with decreased REM latency (faster onset of REM) and increased REM density — not the reverse. A common exam distractor is "increased REM latency," which is incorrect. Depressed individuals enter REM sleep sooner and experience more active REM periods than non-depressed individuals.

  • Monoamine hypothesis: Depression results from deficits in serotonin (5-HT), norepinephrine (NE), and/or dopamine (DA). Supported by the mechanism of action of SSRIs, SNRIs, and MAOIs.
  • HPA axis dysregulation: Chronic stress leads to elevated cortisol via the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Non-suppression on the dexamethasone suppression test is associated with depression, though it lacks diagnostic specificity.
  • Neuroplasticity model: Reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and hippocampal volume reductions are observed in chronic depression (Duman & Monteggia, 2006).

The seasonal pattern specifier — commonly referred to as seasonal affective disorder (SAD) — has been associated with dysregulation of melatonin, a hormone produced by the pineal gland that helps regulate circadian rhythms and sleep-wake cycles. During shorter daylight periods in fall and winter, melatonin secretion increases and its normal diurnal rhythm may become disrupted, contributing to the onset of depressive symptoms such as hypersomnia, carbohydrate craving, and low energy. This photobiological mechanism provides the rationale for bright light therapy (phototherapy), which is considered a first-line treatment for winter-pattern SAD. Typically administered at 10,000 lux for 20–30 minutes each morning, light therapy suppresses excess melatonin production, shifts circadian phase, and has demonstrated efficacy comparable to antidepressant medication in mild-to-moderate seasonal depression.

Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia)

Having explored the episodic nature of major depressive disorder — with its distinct episodes, specifiers, and seasonal patterns — we now turn to a form of depression that is defined not by its intensity but by its sheer persistence. Imagine a client who, when asked how long they've felt depressed, pauses and says, "I honestly can't remember a time when I didn't feel this way." They don't describe the dramatic plunge into darkness that characterizes a major depressive episode; instead, they describe a low-grade, ever-present gray fog that has hung over their life for years. They still go to work, still manage their responsibilities — but with a chronic sense of heaviness, self-doubt, and joylessness that has become so familiar they wonder if it's simply their personality. This is the clinical portrait of Persistent depressive disorder (PDD).

PDD consolidates two previously separate diagnoses from DSM-IV — dysthymic disorder and chronic major depressive disorder — into a single diagnostic category. The core diagnostic requirement is depressed mood for most of the day, for more days than not, persisting for at least 2 years in adults (or 1 year in children and adolescents). During that two-year window, the person must never have been symptom-free for more than two consecutive months. In addition to the chronic depressed mood, the individual must exhibit two or more of the following symptoms:

  • Poor appetite or overeating
  • Insomnia or hypersomnia
  • Low energy or fatigue
  • Low self-esteem
  • Poor concentration or difficulty making decisions
  • Feelings of hopelessness

Notice that the symptom list for PDD is shorter and somewhat different from the nine-symptom criterion set for MDD. You won't find psychomotor changes, suicidal ideation, or anhedonia listed among the PDD criteria — though the individual may certainly experience these. The threshold for diagnosis is deliberately lower in terms of symptom count (two additional symptoms rather than four), reflecting the fact that PDD captures a chronic, lower-intensity form of depression. What it lacks in acute severity, it makes up for in duration and cumulative burden. Research consistently shows that PDD is associated with significant functional impairment, reduced quality of life, and substantial economic costs — in some studies rivaling or exceeding the impairment seen in episodic MDD, precisely because the suffering is so unrelenting.

A critical clinical consideration is that PDD can coexist with major depressive episodes. Because PDD is defined by chronicity rather than a specific severity level, individuals with PDD may periodically dip into episodes that meet full MDD criteria, layering acute severity onto an already chronic baseline. When this happens, the distinction between the disorders blurs — but DSM-5 handles this through specifiers rather than separate diagnoses. The clinician can note whether PDD occurs "with persistent major depressive episode," "with intermittent major depressive episodes, with current episode," or "with intermittent major depressive episodes, without current episode." Additional specifiers — such as anxious distress, atypical features, and early or late onset — can further characterize the presentation, helping clinicians tailor treatment planning to the individual's specific symptom profile.

From a treatment perspective, the chronicity of PDD poses unique challenges. Many clients with PDD have lived with their symptoms for so long that they have developed deeply ingrained cognitive patterns — pervasive schemas of inadequacy, helplessness, and pessimism — that are resistant to brief interventions. This is one reason why the Cognitive Behavioral Analysis System of Psychotherapy (CBASP), developed by James McCullough specifically for chronic depression, has shown particular promise for this population. Pharmacotherapy, especially with SSRIs or SNRIs, is also commonly used, and combination treatment (medication plus psychotherapy) tends to yield the best outcomes for chronic depression.

EPPP Example

When a patient has PDD and also meets criteria for a major depressive episode, this is termed "double depression." The concept of double depression is clinically important and frequently tested on the EPPP. Picture a client who has experienced a persistent low mood and low self-esteem for three years — meeting PDD criteria — and then develops a superimposed episode with markedly worsened sleep, appetite loss, suicidal ideation, and inability to function at work. This individual now carries both diagnoses simultaneously. The prognosis for double depression is worse than for MDD alone, with higher relapse rates, greater functional impairment, and a more treatment-resistant course (Klein et al., 2006). For exam purposes, remember that the key distinguishing feature is the chronic depressive baseline: the major depressive episode emerges against a backdrop of long-standing, lower-grade depressive symptoms, rather than arising from a period of normal mood.

As you prepare for the EPPP, keep the essential contrasts clear in your mind: MDD is defined by discrete episodes lasting at least two weeks with five or more symptoms; PDD is defined by chronic depressed mood lasting at least two years with two or more additional symptoms. MDD captures the acute peaks of depression; PDD captures the relentless undertow. And when both coexist — double depression — the clinical picture is more severe, more impairing, and more difficult to treat than either condition alone. With this understanding of the full spectrum of unipolar depressive disorders, we are now ready to explore what happens when depression alternates with its polar opposite — the elevated, expansive, or irritable mood states that define the bipolar disorders.

Bipolar I and Bipolar II Disorders

Genetics and Heritability of Bipolar Disorder

Bipolar disorder is among the most heritable of all psychiatric conditions. Research using twin methodology has consistently demonstrated a strong genetic contribution, with heritability estimates approximating 90% according to the DSM-5-TR. Monozygotic (identical) twin concordance rates for bipolar disorder are substantially higher than dizygotic (fraternal) twin concordance rates, supporting the conclusion that genetic factors play a predominant role in disease liability. First-degree biological relatives of individuals with bipolar I or bipolar II disorder carry a significantly elevated risk — approximately 10 times greater than the general population — of developing the disorder themselves.

Despite this strong heritability, bipolar disorder does not follow a simple Mendelian inheritance pattern. Instead, it is considered a polygenic condition in which multiple genes of small effect interact with one another and with environmental factors. Stressful life events, disrupted circadian rhythms, and substance use can serve as precipitating triggers in genetically vulnerable individuals. The high heritability estimate also has clinical implications: thorough family psychiatric history is essential when evaluating patients who present with mood symptoms, particularly when distinguishing between unipolar depression and an initial depressive episode that may herald bipolar disorder.

Example

A 22-year-old presents with a severe depressive episode and no prior manic or hypomanic history. Her mother has Bipolar I disorder and her maternal uncle was also diagnosed with bipolar disorder. Given the approximately 90% heritability and elevated familial risk, the clinician should carefully screen for any subthreshold hypomanic symptoms and monitor closely for mood elevation, particularly if antidepressant medication is initiated.

Bipolar I Disorder

Bipolar I disorder requires the occurrence of at least one manic episode. The manic episode may have been preceded by or followed by hypomanic or major depressive episodes, but these are not required for diagnosis. During a manic episode, at least three of the following symptoms are present (four if mood is only irritable):

  1. Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity
  2. Decreased need for sleep (e.g., feels rested after 3 hours)
  3. More talkative than usual or pressure to keep talking
  4. Flight of ideas or subjective experience of racing thoughts
  5. Distractibility
  6. Increase in goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
  7. Excessive involvement in activities with high potential for painful consequences

The mnemonic DIG FAST (Distractibility, Indiscretion/Impulsivity, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Sleep deficit, Talkativeness) helps recall mania criteria.

Bipolar II Disorder

Bipolar II disorder requires at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode. Bipolar II is not a "milder" form of Bipolar I -- the depressive episodes are often more chronic and debilitating. If a full manic episode ever occurs, the diagnosis changes to Bipolar I.

Clinical Pearl

Bipolar II is frequently misdiagnosed as MDD because patients typically present during depressive episodes and may not recognize hypomania as pathological. Screening for a history of elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, and increased activity is essential. Antidepressant monotherapy in unrecognized Bipolar II can precipitate a manic switch.

Cyclothymic Disorder

Having explored Bipolar I and Bipolar II — conditions defined by full-blown manic, hypomanic, and major depressive episodes — we now turn to a mood disorder that lives in a subtler but no less disruptive space. Imagine a client who describes her emotional life as a perpetual roller coaster that never quite reaches the dramatic peaks or plunging drops of a classic bipolar presentation. Some weeks she feels energized and confident, taking on extra projects and socializing late into the night with little need for sleep. Then, seemingly without warning, she slides into days of low motivation, self-doubt, and fatigue — not severe enough to keep her in bed, but enough to make her cancel plans and fall behind at work. She has felt this way, off and on, for years. She has never experienced a full manic episode or a major depressive episode, yet the relentless oscillation has eroded her relationships, her career, and her sense of self. This pattern captures the essence of cyclothymic disorder.

Cyclothymic disorder involves a chronic, fluctuating mood disturbance characterized by numerous periods of hypomanic symptoms alternating with periods of depressive symptoms — none of which meet the full diagnostic criteria for either a hypomanic episode or a major depressive episode. To qualify for the diagnosis, this pattern must persist for at least 2 years in adults (or 1 year in children and adolescents), and during that window the individual must not have been symptom-free for more than 2 consecutive months at a time. The word "chronic" is critical here: cyclothymia is not a brief mood fluctuation but a persistent temperamental instability that colors nearly every aspect of daily functioning.

One of the most clinically important features of cyclothymic disorder is its longitudinal risk. Research indicates there is a 15–50% risk of eventually developing Bipolar I or Bipolar II disorder. This makes cyclothymia more than a standalone diagnosis — it can function as a prodromal or precursor condition, much like how persistent subthreshold symptoms in other domains can foreshadow full syndromal illness. Because of this elevated conversion risk, clinicians should monitor individuals with cyclothymia carefully over time, watching for escalation of hypomanic symptoms into full hypomania or mania, or deepening of depressive periods into major depressive episodes.

Cyclothymic disorder is frequently underdiagnosed for several reasons. First, the symptoms never reach the dramatic intensity that typically drives a person to seek treatment. Clients often describe themselves as "moody" or attribute their emotional variability to personality rather than a mood disorder. Second, because the depressive periods fall short of major depression, clinicians who are not specifically screening for mood cycling may overlook the pattern entirely. Third, the chronic nature of cyclothymia means many individuals have never known a stable emotional baseline — they may genuinely not realize that the constant ups and downs are pathological. In clinical practice, a careful timeline of mood episodes, constructed collaboratively with the client and ideally corroborated by family members, can be invaluable in uncovering the cyclothymic pattern that might otherwise remain hidden beneath vague complaints of emotional instability.

For the EPPP, remember the key distinguishing features: cyclothymic disorder requires at least 2 years of mood cycling (1 year for children and adolescents), symptom-free intervals cannot exceed 2 months, episodes never meet full criteria for hypomania or major depression, and the condition carries a significant risk of progression to Bipolar I or II. This last point — the conversion risk — is a frequently tested concept and underscores why cyclothymia belongs firmly within the bipolar spectrum rather than being dismissed as mere moodiness.

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD)

Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) is classified as a depressive disorder in the DSM-5. It involves the presence of 5 or more symptoms in the final week before the onset of menses, with symptoms beginning to improve within a few days after the onset of menses and becoming minimal or absent in the week post-menses.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

At least one of the following core affective symptoms must be present:

  1. Marked affective lability (e.g., mood swings, feeling suddenly sad or tearful, increased sensitivity to rejection)
  2. Marked irritability or anger or increased interpersonal conflicts
  3. Marked depressed mood, feelings of hopelessness, or self-deprecating thoughts
  4. Marked anxiety, tension, and/or feelings of being keyed up or on edge

Plus one or more of the following additional symptoms (to reach a total of at least 5):

  • Decreased interest in usual activities
  • Subjective difficulty in concentration
  • Lethargy, easy fatigability, or marked lack of energy
  • Marked change in appetite, overeating, or specific food cravings
  • Hypersomnia or insomnia
  • A sense of being overwhelmed or out of control
  • Physical symptoms such as breast tenderness or swelling, joint or muscle pain, a sensation of bloating, or weight gain

Clinical Pearl

PMDD diagnosis must be confirmed by prospective daily ratings for at least 2 consecutive symptomatic menstrual cycles. Retrospective self-report alone is insufficient because women often overestimate premenstrual symptom severity when recalling past cycles. This prospective confirmation requirement is unique among DSM-5 diagnoses.

Distinction from PMS

Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) is a common, less severe condition that affects up to 75% of menstruating women. PMDD is distinct from PMS in that PMDD causes significant functional impairment in social, occupational, or interpersonal domains, whereas PMS involves milder symptoms that do not substantially interfere with daily functioning. PMDD prevalence is estimated at 1.8-5.8% of menstruating women.

Treatment

  • SSRIs are the first-line pharmacological treatment for PMDD. Uniquely, SSRIs can be used either continuously (daily throughout the cycle) or only during the luteal phase (the approximately 2 weeks between ovulation and menses onset), with both approaches demonstrating efficacy.
  • Hormonal treatments (oral contraceptives, GnRH agonists) may be used to suppress ovulation and reduce symptom cycling, particularly when SSRIs are insufficient.

EPPP Example

PMDD is a new addition to the depressive disorders chapter in DSM-5. The key features to remember are: (1) symptoms must occur in the final week before menses and remit after onset; (2) at least one of four core affective symptoms is required; (3) prospective daily ratings over 2 cycles are required for confirmation; (4) SSRIs are effective even when used only during the luteal phase.

Lewinsohn's behavioral model of depression (1974) offers an operant conditioning perspective that complements the cognitive theories described above. According to Lewinsohn, depression develops when an individual experiences a low rate of response-contingent positive reinforcement. When a person's behaviors no longer produce rewarding outcomes — whether due to environmental changes (e.g., loss of a relationship, job loss), skill deficits in eliciting reinforcement from others, or a reduced capacity to experience pleasure — the frequency of adaptive behaviors decreases. This behavioral withdrawal leads to the dysphoric mood, passivity, and social isolation characteristic of depression. The depressed person may initially receive attention and sympathy from others, but over time these social reinforcers diminish as others become frustrated or avoidant, further reducing positive reinforcement and deepening the depressive cycle.

Lewinsohn identified three primary pathways through which reinforcement deficits arise: (1) the environment offers few available reinforcers, (2) the individual lacks the social skills necessary to obtain reinforcement, or (3) the individual has a diminished ability to enjoy experiences that were previously rewarding. This model directly informs Behavioral Activation (BA) therapy, which systematically increases engagement in pleasurable and mastery-oriented activities to restore adequate levels of positive reinforcement.

Example

After relocating to a new city for work, a 32-year-old woman loses her established social network. She stops attending group activities because initial attempts to connect with new people feel unrewarding. As her activity level drops, her mood worsens, and she becomes increasingly isolated — further reducing opportunities for positive social reinforcement. From Lewinsohn's perspective, the therapeutic goal would be to help her gradually re-engage in reinforcing activities and develop social skills to build new relationships.

The Cognitive Triad

1. Negative view of the self: "I am worthless, inadequate, or defective."
2. Negative view of the world/experience: "The world is unfair, and nothing works out for me."
3. Negative view of the future: "Things will never get better; there is no hope."

These negative views are maintained by cognitive distortions (systematic errors in thinking) such as arbitrary inference, selective abstraction, overgeneralization, magnification/minimization, personalization, and all-or-nothing thinking. The distortions arise from underlying depressogenic schemas -- stable cognitive structures formed through early experience that become activated by stressful life events.

Seligman's (1975) learned helplessness model proposes that depression results from perceived inability to control outcomes. The reformulated model (Abramson et al., 1978) added attributional style: internal, stable, and global attributions for negative events are depressogenic.

Treatment of Mood Disorders

Pharmacotherapy for Depression

  • SSRIs (fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram): First-line treatment; selectively block serotonin reuptake; generally well tolerated with side effects including sexual dysfunction, GI symptoms, and initial anxiety
  • SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine): Block both serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake; useful for comorbid pain conditions
  • TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine): Older antidepressants; effective but more side effects (anticholinergic effects, cardiac conduction delays); lethal in overdose
  • MAOIs (phenelzine, tranylcypromine): Require dietary restrictions (tyramine-free diet to avoid hypertensive crisis); generally reserved for treatment-resistant depression or atypical depression
  • Atypicals: Bupropion (norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor; no sexual side effects; lowers seizure threshold); mirtazapine (increases appetite, causes sedation)

Clinical Pearl

The black box warning on antidepressants warns of increased suicidal thinking and behavior in children, adolescents, and young adults (ages 18-24) during initial treatment. This does not mean antidepressants cause suicide -- the risk is that as energy and motivation return before mood improves, the individual may have increased capacity to act on suicidal thoughts. The net benefit of antidepressant treatment for depression far outweighs this risk.

Pharmacotherapy for Bipolar Disorder

  • Lithium: The gold standard mood stabilizer; effective for acute mania and maintenance; requires blood level monitoring (therapeutic range 0.6-1.2 mEq/L); risks include thyroid dysfunction, renal impairment, and teratogenicity (Ebstein's anomaly)
  • Anticonvulsants: Valproate (effective for acute mania and mixed states; teratogenic -- neural tube defects); carbamazepine; lamotrigine (particularly effective for bipolar depression prevention)
  • Atypical antipsychotics: Quetiapine, olanzapine, aripiprazole, lurasidone are used for acute mania and/or bipolar depression

Psychotherapy for Depression

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Interpersonal Therapy (IPT) have the strongest evidence base for treating depression. CBT targets cognitive distortions and behavioral activation. IPT focuses on interpersonal disputes, role transitions, grief, and interpersonal deficits. Behavioral Activation (BA) -- increasing engagement in positively reinforcing activities -- has emerged as an effective standalone treatment, with efficacy comparable to full CBT in some studies (Dimidjian et al., 2006).

Suicide Risk Assessment

Having explored the major pharmacological and psychotherapeutic treatments for mood disorders, we now turn to what is arguably the most urgent clinical issue in all of mental health practice: the assessment of suicide risk. Every clinician will encounter suicidal clients — and how well you assess, document, and respond to that risk can literally mean the difference between life and death. For the EPPP, this topic is not only clinically essential but also heavily tested, spanning questions about risk factors, protective factors, evidence-based interventions, legal and ethical obligations, and the critical distinction between outdated practices and current best-practice guidelines.

Consider this scenario: A 52-year-old man presents to your outpatient practice following a recent divorce. He reports pervasive hopelessness, has been drinking heavily, and mentions — almost in passing — that he recently purchased a firearm "for protection." He denies active suicidal ideation when you ask directly. Should you feel reassured? The answer, of course, is no. Suicide risk assessment requires you to look beyond a client's self-report and integrate demographic data, psychiatric history, behavioral warning signs, and situational factors into a comprehensive clinical picture. Let's examine the key components you need to know.

Clinical Note: No-Suicide Contracts

Historically, some clinicians have used no-suicide contracts (also called "contracts for safety") in which a client verbally or in writing agrees not to engage in self-harm. However, research consistently demonstrates that these agreements do not reduce the likelihood of suicidal behavior. A client in acute crisis may agree to such a contract to avoid hospitalization, to please the clinician, or out of social compliance — none of which reflect genuine safety. Furthermore, no-suicide contracts offer no legal protection to the therapist in the event of a client's suicide. Courts have not recognized these contracts as meeting the standard of care for suicide risk management. Current best practices instead emphasize the use of collaborative safety planning, which involves working with the client to identify warning signs, coping strategies, social supports, professionals to contact during crisis, and means restriction steps. The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention is a widely adopted evidence-based alternative that shifts the focus from a passive promise to an active, personalized set of strategies the client can use when suicidal urges arise. Clinicians should also conduct thorough, ongoing risk assessments and document clinical decision-making rather than relying on a signed agreement as a substitute for careful evaluation.

Clinical Note: No-Suicide Contracts

Historically, clinicians sometimes asked patients to sign a no-suicide contract (also called a "contract for safety"), in which the patient agrees not to engage in self-harm and to contact the clinician if suicidal urges arise. However, there is now broad professional consensus that these agreements lack empirical support as a suicide prevention strategy and may actually create a false sense of security for the clinician. No-suicide contracts have no legal standing, do not constitute adequate risk management, and may discourage deeper clinical exploration of suicidal ideation. Patients who are most at risk — those experiencing intense ambivalence, impulsivity, or psychotic symptoms — are precisely the individuals least able to honor such agreements.

Current best-practice guidelines recommend replacing no-suicide contracts with collaborative safety planning. A safety plan, developed jointly with the patient, identifies personal warning signs, internal coping strategies, social contacts and settings that provide distraction, individuals to contact for help, professionals and agencies to reach in crisis, and steps for restricting access to lethal means. The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing subsequent suicidal behavior and increasing treatment engagement. Unlike a no-suicide contract, a safety plan is a clinical tool that empowers the patient with concrete, actionable steps rather than relying on a promise that may be impossible to keep during a crisis.

Risk Factors

Understanding suicide risk factors is essential for both clinical practice and the EPPP. No single risk factor is sufficient to predict suicide — rather, risk accumulates as multiple factors converge. Think of risk assessment as building a mosaic: each piece alone may seem incomplete, but together they reveal a clearer picture. The following risk factors are the most well-established in the research literature, and you should know them thoroughly for the exam.

  • Previous suicide attempt -- the single strongest predictor of completed suicide
  • Psychiatric diagnosis -- over 90% of completers had a diagnosable disorder, with depression and substance use being the most common
  • Hopelessness -- a stronger predictor of suicidal intent than depression severity (Beck et al., 1985)
  • Demographic factors: Males complete suicide at approximately 4 times the rate of females; females attempt suicide approximately 3 times as often as males
  • Access to lethal means, family history of suicide, recent losses, social isolation
  • Age: Highest rates in adults 45-64 and those 85+

The gender paradox in suicide — men die by suicide far more often, yet women attempt it far more frequently — is one of the most important distinctions to remember. This disparity is largely explained by method lethality: men are more likely to use firearms, while women more often use overdose or other methods that allow greater opportunity for intervention. This is also why means restriction (such as safely storing firearms or limiting access to medications) is one of the most effective suicide prevention strategies available, and why clinicians should always ask about access to lethal means during a risk assessment.

Notice that hopelessness, not depression severity per se, is the stronger predictor of suicidal intent. Aaron Beck and colleagues demonstrated this finding in landmark research, and it carries important clinical implications: a client whose depression is moderate but who feels utterly trapped and hopeless about the future may be at greater risk than someone with severe depressive symptoms who still holds some optimism. This is why assessment tools like the Beck Hopelessness Scale can provide clinically valuable information beyond standard depression measures.

Protective Factors

Effective suicide risk assessment doesn't stop at cataloging what makes a person vulnerable — it also requires identifying what keeps them alive. Protective factors are the counterweights to risk, and understanding them helps clinicians develop more nuanced, individualized safety plans. A person with multiple risk factors but strong reasons for living, robust social connections, and active engagement in treatment may be at meaningfully lower risk than their risk factor profile alone would suggest.

  • Social connectedness and reasons for living
  • Effective clinical care for mental and substance use disorders
  • Restricted access to lethal means
  • Cultural and religious beliefs that discourage suicide

Keep in mind that protective factors do not eliminate risk — they buffer against it. A clinician should never dismiss elevated risk simply because a client identifies reasons for living or reports religious beliefs against suicide. Instead, protective factors should be woven into the clinical formulation alongside risk factors to arrive at a balanced, well-documented assessment of overall suicide risk. On the EPPP, expect questions that require you to weigh both sides of this equation rather than relying on any single indicator in isolation.

Clinical Pearl

The Joiner interpersonal theory of suicide (2005) identifies three factors necessary for a lethal suicide attempt: thwarted belongingness (feeling disconnected), perceived burdensomeness (feeling like a burden to others), and acquired capability (reduced fear of death through habituation to pain and self-harm). What makes Joiner's model particularly powerful — and frequently tested on the EPPP — is its explanation of why most people who desire suicide never attempt it. According to the theory, suicidal desire arises from the intersection of thwarted belongingness and perceived burdensomeness, but desire alone is not sufficient. Only when a person has also developed acquired capability — through repeated exposure to painful or provocative experiences such as prior self-harm, combat exposure, childhood abuse, or even certain occupational risks — does the threshold for a lethal attempt get crossed. This three-factor model helps explain why previous suicide attempts are such a potent risk factor: each attempt may further habituate the individual to the fear and pain associated with self-injury, increasing acquired capability for future attempts.

EPPP Focus Points

  • MDD requires 5 of 9 symptoms for 2 weeks; must include depressed mood or anhedonia
  • DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion for MDD
  • PDD duration: 2 years in adults, 1 year in children/adolescents
  • Bipolar I requires at least one manic episode; depressive episodes are not required
  • Bipolar II requires at least one hypomanic + one major depressive episode; if mania ever occurs, diagnosis changes to Bipolar I
  • Mania lasts at least 7 days; hypomania at least 4 days
  • Beck's cognitive triad: negative views of self, world, and future
  • Hopelessness is a stronger predictor of suicide than depression severity
  • Previous suicide attempt is the strongest predictor of future completed suicide
  • Males complete suicide more often; females attempt more often
  • Atypical depression features: mood reactivity, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, rejection sensitivity
  • PMDD requires 5+ symptoms in the final week before menses; must include at least one core affective symptom
  • PMDD requires prospective daily ratings for 2 consecutive cycles for confirmation
  • PMDD prevalence: 1.8-5.8% of menstruating women; distinct from PMS by severity and functional impairment
  • SSRIs for PMDD can be used continuously or during the luteal phase only
