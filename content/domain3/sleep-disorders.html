Sleep-Wake Disorders

Sleep occupies roughly one-third of a human lifetime, yet its disorders are among the most underdiagnosed conditions in clinical practice. A patient who has not slept well in months may attribute her fatigue to stress. A man whose wife complains about his thunderous snoring may dismiss it as a nuisance rather than a sign of airway obstruction. A college student who falls asleep in lectures — and once collapsed laughing in the hallway — may be told she simply needs more discipline. Each of these presentations masks a distinct sleep-wake disorder with different pathophysiology, different consequences, and different treatment pathways. Because sleep disturbances are frequently comorbid with (and can mimic or exacerbate) mood, anxiety, and psychotic disorders, every clinician needs fluency in this diagnostic territory.

Insomnia Disorder

Insomnia disorder involves dissatisfaction with sleep quantity or quality, associated with difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening with inability to return to sleep. The disturbance occurs at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months, despite adequate opportunity for sleep. It causes clinically significant distress or impairment.

Allison Harvey's cognitive model of insomnia highlights the role of sleep-related safety behaviors — counterproductive coping strategies that individuals adopt in an attempt to gain control over their sleep but that paradoxically maintain and worsen the disorder. These behaviors function much like safety behaviors in anxiety disorders: they prevent the individual from learning that their feared outcome (e.g., catastrophic consequences of poor sleep) is unlikely to occur, thereby blocking the disconfirmation of maladaptive beliefs about sleep. Common examples include monitoring the clock to track how long it takes to fall asleep, going to bed excessively early to "bank" sleep time, canceling daytime activities after a poor night's rest, napping during the day, and consuming extra caffeine to counteract fatigue. Clock-watching is particularly insidious because it increases pre-sleep anxiety and cognitive arousal — each glance at the clock triggers calculations about remaining sleep opportunity and amplifies worry about the consequences of insufficient rest.

Clinical Note

Within CBT-I, therapists systematically identify and target safety behaviors through behavioral experiments. For instance, a patient who watches the clock may be asked to turn it away from the bed and observe the effect on sleep quality. By eliminating these behaviors, patients can test and revise their catastrophic beliefs about sleeplessness (e.g., "If I don't fall asleep within 20 minutes, tomorrow will be ruined"), which reduces the hyperarousal and monitoring that perpetuate insomnia. Harvey's model thus complements Spielman's 3P framework by specifying the cognitive-behavioral mechanisms through which perpetuating factors operate.

Clinical Pearl

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment, more effective than pharmacotherapy in the long term. Key components include stimulus control (bed only for sleep and sex), sleep restriction, sleep hygiene education, cognitive restructuring, and relaxation training. Spielman's (1987) 3P model of insomnia identifies predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors.

Narcolepsy

Narcolepsy involves recurrent periods of irrepressible need to sleep, lapsing into sleep, or napping, occurring at least 3 times per week over the past 3 months, plus at least one of the following:

  • Cataplexy: Brief episodes of sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone triggered by laughter or joking (most specific symptom)
  • Hypocretin (orexin) deficiency: CSF hypocretin-1 levels less than 110 pg/mL (the definitive biomarker)
  • Sleep study findings: REM sleep latency of 15 minutes or less, or MSLT showing mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less with 2 or more sleep-onset REM periods

Narcolepsy Type 1 (with cataplexy/hypocretin deficiency) is caused by autoimmune destruction of hypocretin-producing neurons in the hypothalamus. Narcolepsy Type 2 is without cataplexy and with normal hypocretin levels.

Obstructive Sleep Apnea Hypopnea

Obstructive sleep apnea involves repeated episodes of upper airway obstruction during sleep, manifested by snoring, gasping, or breathing pauses. Diagnosed by polysomnography showing 5 or more obstructive apneas or hypopneas per hour of sleep (apnea-hypopnea index, or AHI, of 5 or more) with associated symptoms, or 15 or more regardless of symptoms. Risk factors include obesity, male sex, older age, and craniofacial abnormalities.

Differential Diagnosis Tip

All three conditions produce daytime fatigue, but the mechanism differs. Insomnia patients are fatigued because they cannot initiate or maintain sleep. Narcolepsy patients are excessively sleepy despite adequate nighttime sleep duration — they may fall asleep involuntarily during conversations or meals. Sleep apnea patients report unrefreshing sleep because repeated airway obstructions fragment their sleep architecture, reducing time in restorative slow-wave and REM stages. When a patient presents with "I'm always tired," these distinctions guide the diagnostic workup and prevent misattribution of daytime somnolence to depression or poor sleep hygiene.

Parasomnias

Having covered conditions that impair sleep initiation, maintenance, or daytime wakefulness, we now turn to a different category of sleep pathology — disorders in which the problem is not the quantity or quality of sleep but rather what happens during it. Parasomnias are abnormal behavioral, experiential, or physiological events occurring during sleep.

EPPP Example

Distinguishing sleep terrors from nightmares: Sleep terrors occur during NREM Stage N3 (deep sleep), involve intense autonomic arousal, and the individual is typically amnestic for the episode. Nightmares occur during REM sleep, the individual fully awakens with recall of the dream content, and there is less autonomic arousal. Sleep terrors are most common in children ages 4-12 and typically resolve spontaneously.

Circadian Rhythm Sleep-Wake Disorders

Having explored how parasomnias like sleep terrors and nightmares emerge from disruptions within specific sleep stages, we now turn to a fundamentally different kind of sleep problem — one rooted not in what happens during sleep, but in when sleep happens. Consider a 16-year-old high school student who lies in bed wide awake until 3 AM every night, not because of anxiety or poor sleep hygiene, but because her internal biological clock is dramatically out of sync with the schedule her school demands. Or picture a night-shift nurse who struggles with crushing fatigue during her midnight-to-8 AM shifts, yet cannot fall asleep when she gets home in the morning because her body insists it is time to be awake. These individuals are experiencing the clinical consequences of a misaligned circadian rhythm.

Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders involve a persistent or recurrent pattern of sleep disruption that arises primarily from alteration of the body's circadian system or from a misalignment between the endogenous circadian rhythm and the sleep-wake schedule required by the individual's physical environment or social obligations. The key distinction from other sleep-wake disorders is that the sleep itself may be perfectly normal in architecture and quality — the problem is that it occurs at the wrong time. When individuals are allowed to sleep on their own preferred schedule, they typically sleep well; it is the collision between their internal clock and external demands that produces the distress and functional impairment warranting a clinical diagnosis.

Types

The DSM-5 identifies several subtypes of circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorder, each reflecting a different mechanism by which the internal clock and the external world fall out of alignment. Understanding these subtypes is clinically important because they differ in their typical populations, underlying causes, and treatment approaches.

  • Delayed sleep-wake phase type: Habitual sleep-wake timing is delayed, usually by 2+ hours, relative to conventional times. Common in adolescents. The individual cannot fall asleep until very late (e.g., 2-6 AM) and has difficulty waking for school or work. This is the adolescent who is mislabeled as "lazy" for sleeping through morning classes — her circadian clock is biologically shifted later, a phenomenon partly driven by pubertal hormonal changes. When allowed to sleep on her own schedule (e.g., during summer break), she sleeps normally and feels rested.
  • Advanced sleep-wake phase type: Habitual sleep-wake timing is advanced. More common in older adults. Individuals fall asleep and wake very early (e.g., sleep at 6-8 PM, wake at 2-5 AM). Picture a retired grandfather who falls asleep during the evening news at 7 PM and is wide awake, frustrated, at 3 AM with nothing to do — this is the mirror image of the delayed type, and it tends to become more prevalent with aging as the circadian system shifts earlier.
  • Shift work type: Insomnia and/or excessive sleepiness due to a work schedule that overlaps the usual sleep period. The night-shift nurse described above exemplifies this type. Her body's circadian system is promoting wakefulness during her work hours and sleep during her off hours — exactly the opposite of what her schedule demands. This type carries significant health risks, including increased rates of cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and accidents.
  • Jet lag type: Sleepiness and alertness occurring at inappropriate times relative to local time after travel across time zones. A psychologist flying from New York to Tokyo may find herself wide awake at 2 AM local time and struggling to keep her eyes open during an afternoon conference — her circadian clock is still operating on Eastern Standard Time. Eastward travel (which requires advancing the clock) is generally harder to adapt to than westward travel.
  • Non-24-hour sleep-wake type: Pattern of sleep-wake cycles not synchronized to the 24-hour environment. Most common in blind individuals lacking light input to the suprachiasmatic nucleus. Because the endogenous circadian period is slightly longer than 24 hours (approximately 24.2 hours), individuals without photic entrainment gradually drift, falling asleep progressively later each day in a free-running pattern that cycles in and out of alignment with conventional schedules.

For the EPPP, remember that the delayed type is most strongly associated with adolescence, the advanced type with older adulthood, and the non-24-hour type with blindness. The shift work and jet lag types are situational — driven by external schedule demands rather than an intrinsic clock abnormality — but they can nonetheless produce clinically significant impairment requiring intervention.

Clinical Pearl

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus is the master circadian pacemaker — a tiny paired nucleus sitting just above the optic chiasm that orchestrates the body's roughly 24-hour biological rhythms. It is primarily entrained (synchronized) by light input via the retinohypothalamic tract, which is why light is the single most powerful tool for resetting circadian timing. Melatonin, produced by the pineal gland under SCN control, promotes sleep onset and is used therapeutically for circadian rhythm disorders. Its secretion is suppressed by light and increases in darkness, earning it the nickname "the hormone of darkness." Clinically, light therapy (bright light exposure in the morning) is the primary treatment for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, and strategically timed melatonin administration can complement this approach. Notably, bright light therapy is also a first-line treatment for seasonal affective disorder — a connection that underscores the profound influence of circadian mechanisms on both sleep and mood.

  • Sleep terrors = NREM/N3 with amnesia; nightmares = REM with dream recall
  • REM sleep behavior disorder is a prodromal marker for Parkinson's disease and other synucleinopathies
  • Pica: eating nonfood substances for 1+ month; not diagnosed before age ~2; associated with ID, ASD, pregnancy, iron deficiency
  • Rumination disorder: repeated regurgitation for 1+ month; treat with habit reversal and diaphragmatic breathing
