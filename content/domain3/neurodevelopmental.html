Overview of Neurodevelopmental Disorders

A seven-year-old boy cannot sit still through a single class period, no matter how much he wants to please his teacher. Down the hall, a girl in the same grade has memorized every dinosaur species ever classified but cannot hold a back-and-forth conversation with a classmate. In another classroom, a bright student reads aloud with painful slowness, transposing letters in words her peers decoded two years ago. These three children illustrate very different clinical pictures, yet they share a critical common thread: all are experiencing conditions rooted in atypical brain development that emerged early in life and now disrupt the developmental milestones their peers are meeting on schedule.

Neurodevelopmental disorders are a group of conditions with onset in the developmental period, typically manifesting early in development and producing impairments in personal, social, academic, or occupational functioning. These disorders frequently co-occur with one another. The DSM-5 neurodevelopmental chapter includes intellectual disabilities, communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, specific learning disorder, and motor disorders (including tic disorders).

Key Concept: Dimensional Approach

DSM-5 moved toward a more dimensional approach for neurodevelopmental disorders, using severity levels and specifiers rather than rigid categorical subtypes. This is most evident in ASD (which consolidated previously separate diagnoses) and intellectual disability (which now emphasizes adaptive functioning over IQ scores for severity classification).

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

Picture a college freshman who has always been described as "smart but lazy." She aced the material she found interesting, lost every planner she was given, and spent most lectures drifting into vivid daydreams while her peers took notes. Now, without the external structure of her parents' household, her grades are plummeting, her apartment is in chaos, and she is beginning to wonder whether something deeper than poor motivation has been at work all along. For many individuals, this late-dawning recognition marks the point at which ADHD finally comes into clinical focus.

ADHD is characterized by a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development. DSM-5 made several important changes to ADHD criteria compared to DSM-IV.

DSM-5 Criteria Changes

  • Age of onset changed from before age 7 to before age 12
  • For adults (age 17+), only 5 of 9 symptoms required (instead of 6)
  • ADHD can now be diagnosed comorbidly with ASD (previously excluded)
  • Examples of symptom manifestation in older adolescents and adults were added
  • Subtypes were relabeled as "presentations" to reflect their potential instability over time

Clinical Pearl

Symptoms must be present in two or more settings (home, school, work), must be present for at least 6 months, and must interfere with or reduce the quality of functioning. ADHD persists into adulthood in approximately 50-65% of cases, though hyperactive-impulsive symptoms tend to decline more than inattentive symptoms (Faraone et al., 2006). Executive function deficits (working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility) are central to Barkley's (1997) model of ADHD.

Etiology

  • Genetics: ADHD is highly heritable (approximately 74%). It is polygenic, with dopamine-related genes (DRD4, DAT1) implicated.
  • Neurobiology: Dysregulation of dopamine and norepinephrine in prefrontal-striatal circuits. Structural imaging shows reduced volume in prefrontal cortex, basal ganglia, and cerebellum.
  • Environmental risk factors: Prenatal tobacco and alcohol exposure, low birth weight, prematurity, and lead exposure.

Neuroimaging meta-analyses have further specified the structural brain differences in ADHD. A landmark mega-analysis by Hoogman et al. (2017), drawing from the ENIGMA consortium, identified reduced volume in five subcortical structures: the caudate nucleus, putamen, nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and hippocampus. These differences were most pronounced in children and tended to attenuate with age, suggesting a pattern of delayed brain maturation rather than permanent structural deficit. The caudate and putamen are core components of the basal ganglia involved in motor control, reward processing, and executive functions. The nucleus accumbens plays a central role in motivation and reward circuitry, which aligns with theoretical models emphasizing altered reward sensitivity in ADHD. Reductions in amygdala volume relate to emotional dysregulation frequently observed in the disorder, while hippocampal differences may contribute to working memory difficulties. Overall intracranial volume was also found to be smaller in individuals with ADHD compared to neurotypical controls.

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Where ADHD disrupts executive control, ASD affects a different neurodevelopmental dimension: social communication capacity and behavioral flexibility. A parent may first notice not hyperactivity but a toddler who does not point at airplanes, does not respond to his name, and lines up toy cars in perfect rows rather than racing them. These early signs hint at fundamentally reshaped social information processing.

Autism Spectrum Disorder represents one of the most significant diagnostic changes in DSM-5. The previous DSM-IV categories of autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder, and PDD-NOS were consolidated into the single diagnosis of ASD.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

ASD requires deficits in both of the following domains:

Domain A: Social Communication and Social Interaction (all 3 required)

  1. Deficits in social-emotional reciprocity (e.g., abnormal social approach, failure of back-and-forth conversation, reduced sharing of interests/emotions)
  2. Deficits in nonverbal communicative behaviors (e.g., poor eye contact, abnormalities in body language, deficits in understanding/use of gestures)
  3. Deficits in developing, maintaining, and understanding relationships (e.g., difficulty adjusting behavior to social contexts, difficulty sharing imaginative play, absence of interest in peers)

Domain B: Restricted, Repetitive Behaviors (at least 2 of 4 required)

  1. Stereotyped or repetitive motor movements, use of objects, or speech (echolalia, idiosyncratic phrases)
  2. Insistence on sameness, inflexible adherence to routines, ritualized patterns
  3. Highly restricted, fixated interests that are abnormal in intensity or focus
  4. Hyper- or hyporeactivity to sensory input or unusual interest in sensory aspects of the environment

DSM-5 Changes from DSM-IV

DSM-IV used a triad (social interaction, communication, restricted behaviors). DSM-5 collapsed this into a dyad: (1) social communication/interaction and (2) restricted/repetitive behaviors. The sensory reactivity criterion (B4) is new. Language delay is no longer part of the ASD criteria but can be noted as a specifier. Importantly, DSM-5 requires specifying severity levels for each domain.

Severity Levels

Face Processing Deficits in ASD

A well-documented neurocognitive feature of autism spectrum disorder is impaired face recognition (sometimes termed prosopagnosia-like difficulty). Typically developing individuals process faces holistically and preferentially engage the fusiform face area (FFA) in the fusiform gyrus of the temporal lobe when viewing human faces. Neuroimaging studies consistently show that individuals with ASD exhibit reduced activation of the FFA during face perception tasks and instead recruit object-processing regions, suggesting they process faces in a feature-by-feature manner rather than as integrated wholes.

These face processing deficits have far-reaching consequences for social functioning. The ability to recognize familiar individuals, decode emotional expressions, and follow gaze direction all depend on efficient face perception. When this foundational capacity is compromised, higher-order social skills — such as interpreting another person's mental state from their facial expression (a component of theory of mind) — become substantially more difficult. Eye-tracking research demonstrates that individuals with ASD spend less time fixating on the eye region of faces and more time attending to the mouth or peripheral features, which reduces access to critical emotional and communicative information. These findings have informed intervention approaches that explicitly teach facial emotion recognition using structured training programs, video modeling, and computer-based tools designed to draw attention to salient facial cues.

Example

A 10-year-old boy with ASD Level 1 consistently fails to recognize classmates outside of the school context (e.g., at a grocery store) and has difficulty identifying whether a peer is angry, sad, or teasing based on facial expression alone. His social skills group incorporates structured exercises in which he practices matching photographs of faces to emotion labels and identifying eye-region cues that distinguish genuine smiles from polite ones.

Prognostic Factors in ASD

Research consistently identifies functional language development by age 5 as one of the strongest predictors of long-term outcome in autism spectrum disorder. Children who acquire communicative speech — the ability to use language meaningfully in social contexts — before their fifth birthday tend to demonstrate significantly better adaptive functioning, educational achievement, and social integration in adolescence and adulthood. Other factors associated with more favorable prognosis include higher cognitive ability, absence of co-occurring intellectual disability, and early access to intensive behavioral intervention. Conversely, children who remain nonverbal or minimally verbal past age 5 are more likely to require substantial ongoing support across the lifespan. Clinicians should note that "functional language" refers not merely to the production of words but to the capacity to use language for communicative purposes such as requesting, commenting, and engaging in reciprocal exchanges. This prognostic marker underscores the clinical rationale for prioritizing early communication-focused interventions — including applied behavior analysis (ABA), naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions, and augmentative/alternative communication (AAC) systems — during the critical preschool years when neuroplasticity is greatest.

Specific Learning Disorder

Beyond the attention and social communication challenges discussed so far, neurodevelopmental pathology can also target the specific cognitive processes that underpin academic learning. A child with normal intelligence and adequate educational exposure may nonetheless struggle profoundly with reading, writing, or mathematics — not because of inattention or a language barrier, but because the neural pathways supporting these particular academic skills developed atypically.

Specific Learning Disorder (SLD) is a unified diagnosis in DSM-5 that replaced the separate DSM-IV categories of reading disorder, mathematics disorder, and disorder of written expression. It requires persistent difficulties learning keystone academic skills, with onset during school-age years.

Dyslexia is the most prevalent form of specific learning disorder, and among the subtypes of dyslexia, phonological dyslexia is the most frequently encountered. Phonological dyslexia is characterized by significant difficulty with the grapheme-to-phoneme conversion process—the ability to map written letters and letter combinations onto their corresponding sounds. Individuals with this subtype struggle to decode unfamiliar or novel words (nonwords) because they cannot effectively apply phonological rules, though they may read familiar words relatively well through whole-word recognition. This contrasts with surface dyslexia, in which individuals can apply phonological decoding rules but have difficulty recognizing words that violate standard pronunciation patterns (irregular words such as "yacht" or "colonel"). A third pattern, deep dyslexia, involves both phonological impairment and semantic errors during reading (e.g., reading "forest" as "trees"), and is typically associated with acquired brain injury rather than developmental etiology.

Clinical Note

Because phonological processing deficits underlie the most common reading difficulties, evidence-based interventions for dyslexia typically emphasize phonological awareness training, systematic phonics instruction, and repeated practice with decoding skills. Early identification through screening of phonological awareness in kindergarten and first grade allows for timely intervention during the period when reading foundations are being established.

Specifiers

  • With impairment in reading (encompasses what was formerly called dyslexia): word reading accuracy, reading rate/fluency, reading comprehension
  • With impairment in written expression (encompasses dysgraphia): spelling accuracy, grammar/punctuation, clarity/organization
  • With impairment in mathematics (encompasses dyscalculia): number sense, math facts, calculation, math reasoning

SLD affects approximately 5-15% of school-age children. It requires academic skills substantially below age expectations, confirmed by both clinical assessment and standardized testing. The DSM-5 does not require an IQ-achievement discrepancy, moving away from the discrepancy model toward a response to intervention (RTI) approach.

Intellectual Disability (Intellectual Developmental Disorder)

While specific learning disorders target isolated academic skills despite otherwise typical cognitive development, intellectual disability represents a more global impairment that touches reasoning, problem-solving, and adaptive functioning across nearly every domain of daily life. The distinction matters clinically: a child with dyslexia may struggle to decode words but can solve complex math problems and navigate social situations with ease, whereas a child with intellectual disability faces broader challenges that require support systems extending well beyond academic accommodations.

Intellectual disability involves deficits in intellectual functions (reasoning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, academic learning) and adaptive functioning (conceptual, social, and practical domains) with onset during the developmental period.

Numerous genetic and environmental factors can lead to intellectual disability. Among genetic causes, Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is the most common chromosomal etiology. It results from an error during cell division—most often nondisjunction—that produces an extra copy of chromosome 21. This trisomy occurs in approximately 1 in 700 live births, and the risk increases substantially with advancing maternal age, particularly after age 35. Individuals with Down syndrome typically present with mild to moderate intellectual disability, characteristic facial features (flat nasal bridge, epicanthal folds, protruding tongue), hypotonia, and an increased risk of congenital heart defects, thyroid dysfunction, and early-onset Alzheimer's disease. With appropriate educational supports and early intervention, many individuals achieve meaningful levels of independence and adaptive functioning.

Key Genetic Causes of Intellectual Disability

Down syndrome (Trisomy 21): Most common chromosomal cause; extra chromosome 21 due to nondisjunction; associated with maternal age.
Fragile X syndrome: Most common inherited (single-gene) cause; X-linked mutation in the FMR1 gene; more severe in males.
Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders: Most common preventable environmental cause; results from prenatal alcohol exposure.
Phenylketonuria (PKU): Autosomal recessive metabolic disorder; preventable through early dietary restriction of phenylalanine.

Example

A psychologist evaluating a 4-year-old child with Down syndrome would assess both cognitive abilities and adaptive functioning across conceptual, social, and practical domains. Even if the child's IQ score falls in the moderate range, the severity classification should be guided primarily by the level of adaptive support required. Early intervention services targeting speech-language development, fine motor skills, and social interaction are recommended to optimize functional outcomes.

Clinical Pearl

A major DSM-5 change: severity levels (mild, moderate, severe, profound) are now determined by adaptive functioning rather than IQ score alone. While IQ testing is still used (approximately 2 standard deviations below the mean, or roughly IQ 65-75), the DSM-5 emphasizes that it is the level of adaptive support needed that determines severity. IQ scores are less valid at lower ranges and can be influenced by the Flynn effect and other measurement issues.

Tic Disorders and Tourette's Disorder

The neurodevelopmental conditions we have covered so far — ADHD, ASD, learning disorders, and intellectual disability — primarily affect cognition, attention, or social functioning. Tic disorders, by contrast, announce themselves through the body: sudden, involuntary movements and vocalizations that the child can sometimes suppress briefly but cannot eliminate. A nine-year-old who blinks rapidly, clears his throat dozens of times per hour, and occasionally jerks his shoulder is not being disruptive on purpose; his motor system is generating unwanted output that feels as irresistible as a sneeze.

Tic disorders involve sudden, rapid, recurrent, nonrhythmic motor movements or vocalizations. Tics can be simple (eye blinking, throat clearing) or complex (facial gestures, echolalia, coprolalia).

DSM-5 Tic Disorder Hierarchy

  • Tourette's disorder: Both multiple motor and one or more vocal tics present at some time (not necessarily concurrently). Onset before age 18. Duration more than 1 year. Tics wax and wane.
  • Persistent (chronic) motor or vocal tic disorder: Single or multiple motor OR vocal tics (not both). Onset before 18. Duration more than 1 year.
  • Provisional tic disorder: Single or multiple motor and/or vocal tics. Duration less than 1 year since first onset. Onset before 18.

EPPP Example

Coprolalia (involuntary utterance of obscene words) is often associated with Tourette's in popular culture, but it actually occurs in only about 10-15% of individuals with Tourette's disorder. The most common initial tics are facial (eye blinking, grimacing). Tourette's is highly comorbid with ADHD (approximately 50%) and OCD (approximately 30%). The CSTC circuit implicated in OCD is also involved in tic disorders.

  • ADHD age of onset changed to before age 12 in DSM-5 (from age 7 in DSM-IV)
  • ADHD now uses "presentations" instead of "subtypes"; can be comorbid with ASD
  • ASD consolidated autistic disorder, Asperger's, CDD, and PDD-NOS into one diagnosis
  • ASD uses a dyad (social communication + RRBs) instead of DSM-IV's triad
  • ASD severity: Level 1 (requiring support), Level 2 (substantial support), Level 3 (very substantial support)
  • Former Asperger's diagnosis maps to ASD Level 1 without intellectual/language impairment
  • SLD is a unified diagnosis with specifiers for reading, written expression, and mathematics
  • Intellectual disability severity now based on adaptive functioning, not IQ alone
  • Tourette's requires both motor AND vocal tics for more than 1 year, onset before age 18
  • Coprolalia occurs in only 10-15% of Tourette's cases
