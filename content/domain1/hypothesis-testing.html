Introduction to Inferential Statistics

In the previous chapter, correlation and regression gave us the tools to describe relationships and make predictions — but they did not tell us whether those relationships are real or merely the product of sampling luck. A clinical researcher finds that therapy clients who complete more sessions show greater symptom reduction (r = .34). Is that correlation strong enough to trust, or could it have emerged by chance from a small sample? Answering questions like this requires a fundamentally different toolkit. Inferential statistics allow researchers to draw conclusions about entire populations based on data collected from samples, enabling us to test hypotheses and determine whether observed effects are likely genuine or attributable to chance (Field, 2018).

Understanding what determines the magnitude of the standard error of the mean is essential for grasping how sample-based estimates gain or lose precision. The standard error (SE = σ / √n) is governed by two factors that work in opposing directions:

Factor Change Effect on Standard Error Consequence for Estimation
Population standard deviation (σ) Increases SE increases Less precise estimate of μ; wider confidence intervals
Population standard deviation (σ) Decreases SE decreases More precise estimate of μ; narrower confidence intervals
Sample size (n) Increases SE decreases More precise estimate of μ; greater statistical power
Sample size (n) Decreases SE increases Less precise estimate of μ; reduced statistical power

Researchers can reduce the standard error—and thereby increase the precision and power of their analyses—by collecting larger samples. However, they cannot directly control population variability. In some cases, researchers employ strategies to indirectly reduce variability, such as using more homogeneous samples, standardizing measurement procedures, or employing within-subjects designs that remove individual differences from the error term. Each of these approaches effectively reduces the variability that feeds into the standard error, producing more sensitive statistical tests.

Clinical Note

In clinical research, populations often exhibit high variability in treatment response due to differences in severity, comorbidities, and individual characteristics. This inherent variability inflates the standard error and makes it harder to detect treatment effects. Researchers may address this by using ANCOVA to control for baseline severity or by employing repeated measures designs, both of which reduce error variance and effectively lower the standard error used in hypothesis tests.

The central limit theorem (CLT) is one of the most foundational principles in inferential statistics. It states that the sampling distribution of the mean will approximate a normal (bell-shaped) distribution as sample size grows, regardless of how the original population scores are distributed. Even if the population is heavily skewed, bimodal, or uniform, the distribution formed by repeatedly drawing samples of size n and plotting their means will become increasingly normal as n increases.

This theorem has profound practical consequences. Most parametric inferential tests—including t-tests, ANOVA, and z-tests—rely on the assumption that sampling distributions are normal. The CLT provides the mathematical justification for using these tests even when the underlying population distribution departs from normality, provided the sample size is sufficiently large. A commonly cited guideline is that sample sizes of 30 or more generally produce sampling distributions that are approximately normal, though more extreme population skew may require larger samples.

The CLT also specifies two additional properties of the sampling distribution of means: (1) the mean of the sampling distribution equals the population mean (μ), making the sample mean an unbiased estimator, and (2) the standard deviation of the sampling distribution—the standard error—equals σ / √n, meaning it shrinks as sample size increases.

Central Limit Theorem

As sample size increases, the distribution of sample means approaches a normal curve with a mean equal to the population mean (μ) and a standard deviation equal to σ / √n, irrespective of the shape of the original population distribution.

Example

Imagine a population of reaction times that is strongly positively skewed, with most responses clustered at fast times and a long tail of slow responses. If you draw thousands of random samples of n = 5 and plot the means, the resulting distribution will still show some skew. However, if you repeat this process with samples of n = 50, the distribution of those sample means will closely resemble a symmetric, normal curve—even though no individual sample of reaction times is normally distributed.

The standard error of the mean (SEM) quantifies how much variability we can expect among sample means drawn from the same population. It serves as a measure of the precision of the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean. The SEM is calculated by dividing the population standard deviation (σ) by the square root of the sample size (n): SEM = σ / √n.

This formula reveals two critical relationships. First, as the population standard deviation increases, the standard error grows larger. When individual scores in a population are widely dispersed, sample means drawn from that population will also be more variable, making any single sample mean a less precise estimate. Second, as sample size decreases, the standard error increases. Smaller samples are more susceptible to the influence of extreme scores and provide less stable estimates of the population mean. Conversely, increasing sample size reduces the standard error, which is why larger studies yield more precise estimates.

These relationships have direct implications for hypothesis testing: a larger standard error makes it harder to detect true population differences because test statistics (such as t and z) place the standard error in the denominator. When the SEM is large, the resulting test statistic is smaller, reducing the likelihood of achieving statistical significance. This is one reason why increasing sample size is one of the most effective strategies for boosting statistical power.

Example

Suppose a population has σ = 20. With a sample of n = 25, the SEM = 20 / √25 = 4.0. If the sample size increases to n = 100, the SEM = 20 / √100 = 2.0. Doubling the square root of n cuts the standard error in half, illustrating why researchers seek larger samples for greater precision. Now consider a different population where σ = 40 with n = 100: the SEM = 40 / √100 = 4.0—twice as large as before, demonstrating how greater population variability inflates the standard error.

The foundation of inferential statistics rests on probability theory and sampling distributions. Understanding these concepts is essential for interpreting research findings and making evidence-based clinical decisions.

Key Terminology

Population: The entire group to which researchers wish to generalize findings.

Sample: A subset of the population that is actually measured.

Parameter: A characteristic of a population (e.g., population mean μ).

Statistic: A characteristic of a sample used to estimate parameters (e.g., sample mean M or x̄).

Sampling Distribution: The theoretical distribution of a statistic across all possible samples of a given size.

Hypothesis Testing Framework

Now that we understand how sampling distributions and the standard error provide the bridge from sample data to population-level conclusions, we need a structured decision-making process for putting those tools to work. When a researcher asks, "Does this new therapy reduce anxiety more than a placebo?", she cannot simply eyeball two group means and declare victory. She needs a formal, repeatable framework that controls the risk of error. Hypothesis testing provides exactly that — a systematic procedure for evaluating claims about populations based on sample data, involving stated hypotheses, a pre-selected significance level, a test statistic, and a clear decision rule.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

The null hypothesis (H₀) states that there is no effect, no difference, or no relationship in the population. It represents the status quo or the assumption of "no change." The null hypothesis is what we attempt to reject through statistical testing.

The alternative hypothesis (H₁ or Hₐ) states that there is an effect, difference, or relationship. It represents what the researcher typically hopes to demonstrate. The alternative hypothesis can be directional (one-tailed) or non-directional (two-tailed).

Example: Formulating Hypotheses

A researcher wants to test whether a new therapy reduces depression scores compared to a control condition.

H₀: There is no difference in depression scores between the therapy and control groups (μ₁ = μ₂)

H₁: The therapy group has lower depression scores than the control group (μ₁ < μ₂) — directional/one-tailed

OR

H₁: There is a difference in depression scores between groups (μ₁ ≠ μ₂) — non-directional/two-tailed

Significance Level (Alpha)

The significance level (α) is the probability of rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true. Conventionally, α is set at .05, meaning researchers accept a 5% chance of making a Type I error. More stringent levels (.01, .001) may be used when the consequences of a false positive are serious.

p-Values

The p-value is the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. If p ≤ α, the result is considered statistically significant, and we reject the null hypothesis. If p > α, we fail to reject the null hypothesis.

Important Distinction

The p-value is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is the probability of the data (or more extreme data) given that the null hypothesis is true. This distinction is commonly misunderstood and frequently tested on the EPPP.

Type I and Type II Errors

Having established the logic of hypothesis testing — setting up null and alternative hypotheses, choosing a significance level, and interpreting p-values — we now confront an uncomfortable reality: no matter how carefully we design a study, we can never be entirely certain our conclusion is correct. Every statistical decision carries the risk of error, and understanding these risks is essential for both conducting research and evaluating it critically in clinical practice.

Imagine a psychologist conducting a clinical trial for a new intervention targeting adolescent depression. At the end of the study, the data will lead to one of two decisions: reject the null hypothesis (concluding the treatment works) or fail to reject it (concluding there isn't sufficient evidence that it works). But here's the catch — reality doesn't change just because we made a decision. The treatment either truly works or it doesn't, and our statistical conclusion might not match that truth. This mismatch is where errors enter the picture, and hypothesis testing involves the possibility of two types of errors:

Type I Error (False Positive)

Picture a pharmaceutical company announcing that its new antidepressant outperforms placebo. Clinicians begin prescribing it, patients place hope in it, and insurance companies start covering it — but in reality, the drug is no better than a sugar pill. The researchers made a statistical error: they rejected a null hypothesis that was actually true. A Type I error occurs when we reject the null hypothesis when it is actually true. Often called a "false positive" or "alpha error," this is the mistake of seeing an effect that isn't genuinely there — finding a signal in what is really just noise.

The probability of committing a Type I error is directly tied to the significance level (α) that the researcher selects before conducting the study. If you set α = .05, you are accepting a 5% chance of making a Type I error when the null hypothesis is true. Think of it this way: if you ran 100 studies in a world where the null hypothesis was always true, you would expect about 5 of those studies to produce a statistically significant result purely by chance. This is precisely why the choice of alpha level is not arbitrary — it represents the researcher's tolerance for false alarms. In fields where false positives carry devastating consequences, such as forensic psychology or high-stakes medical trials, researchers sometimes adopt a more stringent alpha of .01 or even .001 to reduce this risk.

Type II Error (False Negative)

Now consider the opposite scenario. A research team develops a genuinely effective cognitive-behavioral intervention for insomnia in older adults. They run a study, but with a small sample of only 15 participants, the results fail to reach statistical significance. The researchers conclude there is insufficient evidence for the treatment's effectiveness, and the intervention is shelved — despite the fact that it truly works. This is the pain of a Type II error: missing something real. A Type II error occurs when we fail to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false. Also known as a "false negative" or "beta error," this is the mistake of overlooking a genuine effect — failing to detect a signal that is truly present in the data.

The probability of a Type II error is denoted by β (beta), and unlike alpha, it is not directly set by the researcher. Instead, beta depends on several factors, including sample size, the magnitude of the true effect, and the alpha level chosen. A study with a small sample size or a very stringent alpha level will have a higher beta — meaning a greater risk of missing real effects. As we will see in the next section on statistical power, power is defined as 1 − β, making the relationship between Type II errors and study design one of the most practically important concepts in research methodology.

An easy mnemonic can help keep these straight: a Type I error is like a false alarm (you cry wolf when there's no wolf), while a Type II error is like a missed detection (the wolf is there, but you fail to notice it). On the EPPP, a common testing strategy is to present a research scenario and ask you to identify which type of error has occurred — so grounding these definitions in concrete examples is essential. Return to the decision matrix above to reinforce the four possible outcomes of any hypothesis test.

Notice the critical trade-off embedded in this framework: if you make it harder to reject the null hypothesis (by lowering alpha), you reduce the risk of a Type I error but simultaneously increase the risk of a Type II error. Conversely, raising alpha makes it easier to detect real effects but also easier to chase false ones. Researchers must navigate this tension thoughtfully, balancing the relative costs of each type of mistake in the context of their specific study.

Clinical Implications

In clinical contexts, the consequences of Type I and Type II errors differ dramatically — and the "right" balance depends on the stakes involved. A Type I error in treatment research means concluding a treatment works when it doesn't, potentially exposing patients to ineffective treatments with real side effects, wasting healthcare resources, and diverting attention from approaches that might genuinely help. A Type II error means missing a truly effective treatment, denying patients beneficial interventions that could alleviate suffering. Consider HIV screening: a false positive (Type I) causes anxiety and follow-up testing, while a false negative (Type II) means a person with HIV goes undiagnosed and untreated. In forensic settings, a Type I error might mean labeling someone as high-risk for violence when they are not, while a Type II error might mean failing to identify genuine danger. Researchers must consider these trade-offs when designing studies, and clinicians must weigh them when applying research findings to practice.

Statistical Power

Type I and Type II errors frame the two ways a hypothesis test can go wrong — but as a researcher designing a study, one of those errors is directly under your control. While alpha sets the ceiling on false positives, you can actively minimize the risk of a Type II error by designing a study with sufficient statistical power: the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis, or equivalently, the probability of detecting a true effect when one exists. Power = 1 - B. By convention, adequate power is typically .80 or higher, meaning an 80% chance of detecting a true effect (Cohen, 1988).

Factors Affecting Power

Four primary levers allow researchers to increase power before data collection even begins. The hub diagram below maps each factor to its mechanism. Of these, sample size is the lever researchers control most directly — which is why power analysis, discussed next, focuses on determining the minimum n needed to detect an expected effect.

Power Analysis

Power analysis is used to determine the sample size needed to achieve adequate power for detecting an effect of a given size. A priori power analysis (conducted before data collection) helps ensure studies are adequately powered. Post hoc power analysis (conducted after finding non-significant results) is generally discouraged because observed power is a direct function of the obtained p-value.

Cohen, J. (1988). Statistical power analysis for the behavioral sciences (2nd ed.). Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. - Cohen's work established the framework for power analysis and effect size interpretation that remains standard in psychological research.

t-Tests

Having just explored statistical power — the probability that a study will detect an effect when one truly exists — we now turn to one of the most commonly used inferential tests in psychological research. Imagine a clinical psychologist who wants to know whether a new cognitive-behavioral intervention reduces depression more effectively than a waitlist control. She has two groups of participants, their BDI-II scores, and a straightforward question: Are these two means different enough that we can rule out chance? The tool she reaches for is almost certainly a t-test, a workhorse of hypothesis testing that compares means when the population standard deviation is unknown — which, in the real world of clinical research, is virtually always the case.

The t-test was originally developed by William Sealy Gosset, who published under the pseudonym "Student" (hence the term "Student's t-distribution"). The fundamental logic is elegant: the test calculates how far apart two means are relative to the variability within the data. A large t-value means the difference between groups is large relative to the noise in the data, making it more likely to reach statistical significance. There are three main types of t-tests, each tailored to a different research design, and knowing which one to select is a critical skill for both practice and the EPPP.

Independent Samples t-Test

The most frequently encountered version is the independent samples t-test, used to compare means from two different groups in a between-subjects design. Think of the clinical psychologist mentioned above: she has one group receiving the CBT intervention and a separate group on the waitlist. The participants in one group have no connection to those in the other — their scores are independent of each other. This is the hallmark of a between-subjects comparison, and it calls for an independent samples t-test. The test essentially asks: "Is the difference between these two group means larger than what we would expect from sampling variability alone?"

Like all parametric tests, the independent samples t-test rests on specific assumptions that must be reasonably met for results to be trustworthy. These assumptions include:

  • Independent observations — each participant's score is unrelated to every other participant's score
  • Normally distributed populations (or large samples, which invoke the Central Limit Theorem)
  • Homogeneity of variance (equal variances in both groups) — when this assumption is violated, a corrected version such as Welch's t-test can be used

Violations of these assumptions can inflate Type I or Type II error rates. For example, if one group has far greater variability in depression scores than the other, the standard independent samples t-test may yield misleading results. This is why many statistical software packages automatically report both the standard t-test and Welch's corrected version, allowing researchers to choose the appropriate output.

Paired Samples t-Test

Now imagine a different design: instead of comparing two separate groups, a researcher measures anxiety in the same 30 participants before and after a relaxation training program. Because each person serves as their own control, the two sets of scores are inherently linked — participant 5's pre-treatment score is naturally paired with participant 5's post-treatment score. This within-subjects design calls for a paired samples t-test, also called the dependent samples t-test or repeated measures t-test. Rather than comparing two independent group means, this test analyzes the difference scores within each participant, asking whether the average change differs significantly from zero.

The paired samples design has a notable statistical advantage: because each participant serves as their own control, individual differences in baseline anxiety, personality, or other extraneous variables are effectively removed from the equation. This typically reduces error variance, increasing statistical power compared to an independent samples design with the same number of participants. This is one reason why within-subjects designs are popular in clinical outcome research — they can detect smaller treatment effects with fewer participants.

One-Sample t-Test

The simplest member of the t-test family addresses an equally important question: does this sample come from a population with a specific known mean? The one-sample t-test compares a single sample mean to a known or hypothesized population value. For example, a school psychologist administers an IQ test to 25 children in a gifted program and wants to know whether their mean IQ score differs significantly from the general population mean of 100. The one-sample t-test computes how far the sample mean deviates from 100, relative to the standard error of the mean, and evaluates whether that deviation is statistically significant.

Another common application arises in clinical assessment: a neuropsychologist might test whether a sample of patients recovering from traumatic brain injury scores significantly below the normative mean on a measure of processing speed. In each case, the logic is the same — one sample, one known comparison value, and a question about whether the observed difference exceeds what chance alone would produce.

Example: Choosing a t-Test

Scenario: A researcher measures anxiety before and after a relaxation intervention in the same 30 participants.

Appropriate test: Paired samples t-test, because the same participants are measured twice (within-subjects design). The pre- and post-scores are dependent — each person's two scores form a natural pair.

Scenario: A researcher compares anxiety scores between 30 participants who received treatment and 30 different participants who received placebo.

Appropriate test: Independent samples t-test, because different participants are in each group (between-subjects design). There is no logical pairing between a specific treatment participant and a specific placebo participant.

Scenario: A researcher recruits 40 first-year graduate students and administers a standardized empathy scale, then tests whether their mean empathy score differs from the published national average for adults.

Appropriate test: One-sample t-test, because there is only one sample being compared to a known population value — no second group is involved.

A final practical note: the t-test is limited to comparisons involving only two means (or one mean versus a known value). When a research question involves three or more groups — say, comparing CBT, medication, and a waitlist control — using multiple t-tests would inflate the Type I error rate (the familywise error problem). This limitation sets the stage for the next topic: the analysis of variance, which elegantly handles comparisons among multiple groups simultaneously.
