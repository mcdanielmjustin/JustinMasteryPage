Schedules of Reinforcement

In the previous chapter, we established the fundamental distinction between reinforcement and punishment — how consequences either strengthen or weaken voluntary behavior. But knowing that reinforcement increases behavior tells only part of the story. A factory worker paid for every widget she assembles behaves very differently from a gambler pulling a slot machine lever, even though both are responding for reinforcement. The critical difference lies in the schedule — the pattern or rule governing when reinforcement is delivered. Skinner and his colleagues discovered that these patterns have profound effects on the rate, consistency, and persistence of responding, including the striking finding that intermittent reinforcement produces behavior far more resistant to extinction than continuous reinforcement — a phenomenon known as the partial reinforcement effect (Ferster & Skinner, 1957).

Continuous vs. Intermittent Reinforcement

Continuous reinforcement (CRF) occurs when every instance of the target behavior is reinforced. This schedule is optimal for initially establishing a new behavior but produces rapid extinction when reinforcement is discontinued.

Intermittent (partial) reinforcement occurs when only some instances of the behavior are reinforced. Behavior maintained on intermittent schedules is more resistant to extinction because the organism has learned that not every response produces reinforcement.

The Four Basic Schedules

Reinforcement schedules are defined by whether the requirement for reinforcement is based on the number of responses (ratio) or the passage of time (interval), and whether that requirement is constant (fixed) or unpredictable (variable). Each combination produces a distinctive pattern of responding with important implications for learning and behavior maintenance.

Fixed Ratio (FR)

Reinforcement is delivered after a fixed number of responses. For example, FR-5 means reinforcement occurs after every 5 responses. FR schedules produce high, steady response rates with a brief pause after reinforcement (post-reinforcement pause). Piecework pay is a real-world example.

Variable Ratio (VR)

Reinforcement is delivered after a variable number of responses, averaging to a specified number. VR schedules produce the highest and most consistent response rates with no post-reinforcement pause. Gambling operates on a VR schedule, which explains its highly addictive nature.

Fixed Interval (FI)

Reinforcement is delivered for the first response after a fixed time period has elapsed. FI schedules produce a characteristic "scalloped" pattern - responding is slow immediately after reinforcement and accelerates as the interval ends. Checking the mailbox for an expected delivery is an example.

Variable Interval (VI)

Reinforcement is delivered for the first response after a variable time period, averaging to a specified duration. VI schedules produce steady, moderate response rates. Checking email or social media operates on a VI schedule.

Two general principles cut across these four schedules: ratio schedules produce higher response rates than interval schedules because reinforcement depends on how fast the organism responds, and variable schedules generate greater resistance to extinction than fixed schedules because the organism can never predict exactly when reinforcement will stop. Shaping and Chaining

Reinforcement schedules explain how existing behaviors are maintained and how resistant they are to extinction — but what about behaviors the organism has never performed before? You cannot reinforce a pigeon for pressing a lever if the pigeon never presses it in the first place. Clinicians face the same challenge when working with a nonverbal child who has never produced a spoken word or a stroke patient who cannot yet lift a spoon. Two powerful procedures — shaping and chaining — solve this problem by building complex new behaviors from simpler components.

Shaping

Shaping, also called the method of successive approximations, is a procedure for teaching new behaviors by reinforcing responses that increasingly resemble the target behavior. The trainer reinforces behaviors that are progressively closer to the desired response while no longer reinforcing earlier approximations (Skinner, 1953).

Fading

Fading is the systematic and gradual removal of prompts — such as visual cues, verbal hints, physical guidance, or gestural signals — that were introduced to help an individual perform a target behavior. The goal of fading is to transfer stimulus control from the artificial prompt to the naturally occurring discriminative stimuli in the environment, so that the learner eventually performs the behavior independently.

Prompts are supplementary antecedent stimuli that increase the likelihood of a correct response during skill acquisition. While prompts are essential for establishing new behaviors, continued reliance on them indicates that the behavior remains under the control of the prompt rather than the natural cue. Fading addresses this by gradually reducing the intensity, frequency, or intrusiveness of the prompt across successive trials. Common fading strategies include reducing the loudness of a verbal cue, decreasing the size of a visual highlight, transitioning from full physical guidance to a light touch, or moving from direct instruction to indirect gestures.

Fading differs from shaping in an important way: shaping involves gradually changing the response requirement, whereas fading involves gradually changing the stimulus conditions (specifically, the prompts). Both procedures use gradual modification, but they target different elements of the behavioral contingency.

Procedure What Changes Gradually Goal
Shaping The response criterion (successive approximations) Establish a new behavior not yet in the repertoire
Fading The antecedent prompts (visual, verbal, physical cues) Transfer stimulus control to natural cues for independent performance

Example

A therapist teaching a child to identify the letter "A" initially points directly at it, says its name aloud, and traces its shape in the air. Over successive trials, the therapist first stops tracing, then reduces the verbal cue to just the initial sound, and finally removes the pointing gesture. Eventually the child identifies "A" without any supplementary assistance — stimulus control has transferred from the prompts to the letter itself.

While shaping involves reinforcing successive approximations of a target behavior, clinicians frequently use prompts—supplementary cues or assistance—to occasion the desired response during the learning process. Prompts can be verbal (instructions or hints), gestural (pointing or modeling), physical (hand-over-hand guidance), or visual (pictures or written cues). Once the learner begins performing the target behavior reliably, these prompts must be systematically removed through a procedure called fading.

Fading

The systematic and gradual removal of prompts so that the target behavior comes under the control of naturally occurring stimuli rather than remaining dependent on artificial assistance. Fading ensures that the learner can perform the behavior independently.

Fading can proceed in several ways. Most-to-least prompting begins with the most intrusive prompt (e.g., full physical guidance) and gradually reduces to less intrusive prompts (partial physical, gestural, then no prompt). Least-to-most prompting starts with minimal assistance and increases only as needed. Clinicians may also fade along stimulus dimensions—for example, gradually reducing the size, color intensity, or position of a visual prompt until it is no longer present.

Example

A speech therapist teaching a child to request "juice" might initially use a full verbal model ("Say juice"), then fade to a partial verbal prompt ("juh..."), then to a gestural prompt (pointing to the juice), and finally to the natural stimulus alone (the presence of juice). If prompts are removed too quickly, the behavior may fail to occur; if they are maintained too long, the learner may become prompt-dependent—unable to perform without the artificial cue.

Shaping is essential when the target behavior does not occur spontaneously in the organism's repertoire. For example, training a pigeon to turn in a complete circle requires first reinforcing any slight head turn, then quarter turns, half turns, and finally complete rotations.

Clinical Example: Shaping Speech in Autism

Applied behavior analysis (ABA) uses shaping to develop speech in nonverbal children with autism. A clinician might first reinforce any vocalization, then vocalizations that resemble consonant sounds, then consonant-vowel combinations, and eventually approximations of words. This systematic approach has been highly effective in early intervention programs (Lovaas, 1987).

Chaining

Chaining is a procedure for teaching complex behaviors that consist of a sequence of simpler responses. Each response in the chain produces a stimulus that serves as both a conditioned reinforcer for the previous response and a discriminative stimulus for the next response.

Types of Chaining

  • Forward chaining: Teaching the first step first, then adding subsequent steps. Used when early steps must be mastered before later steps can be attempted.
  • Backward chaining: Teaching the last step first, then adding preceding steps. Often preferred because the learner always completes the chain and contacts the terminal reinforcer.
  • Total task presentation: Teaching all steps together with prompts. Useful for simpler chains or more capable learners.

Clinical Application: Teaching Daily Living Skills

Chaining procedures are commonly used to teach daily living skills (dressing, toothbrushing, handwashing) to individuals with developmental disabilities. Backward chaining is often preferred for these tasks because completing the final step provides natural reinforcement (e.g., clean teeth, dry hands).

Stimulus Control

Imagine you're driving down a familiar road and you see a green traffic light ahead. Without even thinking about it, you maintain speed and proceed through the intersection. Now imagine the light turns red — you immediately press the brake. Your driving behavior isn't random; it's governed by the signals in front of you. This everyday experience illustrates one of the most powerful principles in operant conditioning: the way environmental cues come to regulate when and where we perform certain behaviors. Having explored how complex behavior sequences are built through shaping and chaining, we now turn to how organisms learn when to perform those behaviors — and when not to.

Stimulus control occurs when a behavior is more likely to occur in the presence of a particular stimulus than in its absence. When you laugh freely at a comedy club but suppress the same jokes during a job interview, your humor is under stimulus control. The controlling stimulus — the one that signals "go ahead, this behavior will pay off here" — is called a discriminative stimulus (SD, pronounced "S-D"), which signals that reinforcement is available for a particular response. Stimulus control is not about reflexive reactions (that's classical conditioning territory); it's about learned sensitivity to context, and it explains an enormous range of human and animal behavior.

Discriminative Stimuli

Think about a child in a classroom who has learned that raising her hand gets the teacher's attention — but only when the teacher is facing the class. When the teacher's back is turned, hand-raising goes unrewarded. Over time, the child raises her hand primarily when the teacher is looking. The teacher facing the class has become a discriminative stimulus (SD) — a stimulus in the presence of which a particular response has been reinforced. Through experience, organisms learn to respond differently in the presence versus absence of the SD. Crucially, the SD does not elicit the response the way a loud noise elicits a startle (as in classical conditioning); rather, it sets the occasion for the response by signaling that reinforcement is available. Behavior analysts often describe the SD as an "occasion setter" — it tells the organism, "Now is a good time to perform this behavior."

But what about the signals that say "don't bother"? An S-delta (SΔ) is a stimulus that signals reinforcement is not available. In our classroom example, the teacher turned away from the class functions as an SΔ for hand-raising. Through discrimination training — repeated experiences in which responding is reinforced in the presence of one stimulus but not another — organisms learn to respond in the presence of the SD and withhold responding in the presence of the SΔ. This discrimination process is fundamental to adaptive behavior. Without it, organisms would waste enormous energy responding in contexts where no reinforcement is forthcoming.

Example: Stimulus Control in Everyday Life

A ringing telephone (SD) signals that answering will be reinforced by conversation. The silent telephone (SΔ) signals that picking up the receiver will not produce reinforcement. Similarly, an "open" sign on a store (SD) signals that entering will be reinforced, while a "closed" sign (SΔ) signals it will not. In clinical practice, a therapist's warm facial expression may serve as an SD for a client to share vulnerable feelings, while a distracted or hurried demeanor may function as an SΔ — a reminder that the therapeutic context profoundly shapes what clients are willing to disclose.

The Three-Term Contingency

If discriminative stimuli set the stage and consequences deliver the verdict, the behavior itself is the performance in between. Behavior analysts capture this entire sequence in what they call the three-term contingency (or ABC model) — a framework so central to applied behavior analysis that it functions as the field's basic unit of analysis:

  • Antecedent (A): The discriminative stimulus that precedes behavior
  • Behavior (B): The operant response
  • Consequence (C): The reinforcement or punishment that follows

Consider a clinical example: A child with autism engages in tantrum behavior (B) primarily when academic demands are presented (A), and the tantrums result in task removal (C — negative reinforcement). By mapping this ABC sequence, a clinician can see that the academic demand is the antecedent triggering the behavior and that escape from the task is maintaining it. This framework is fundamental to functional behavior assessment and the development of behavior intervention plans in clinical and educational settings (Cooper et al., 2020). On the EPPP, understanding how to identify each component of the three-term contingency — and recognizing that modifying any one element can change behavior — is essential for answering questions about behavioral assessment and intervention planning.

As we move forward, keep in mind that stimulus control helps explain not just simple responses to environmental cues but also more complex learning phenomena. Next, we'll explore how organisms learn to respond to aversive stimuli — specifically, how escape and avoidance behaviors develop and are maintained.

Escape and Avoidance Learning

Imagine a client sitting in your office describing her daily routine: she avoids elevators, declines invitations to crowded restaurants, and takes winding detour routes to bypass bridges. She knows, intellectually, that these situations are unlikely to harm her — yet the avoidance persists, month after month, year after year. Why is avoidance behavior so remarkably resistant to change? And why does it often grow worse over time rather than better? The answers lie in two closely related forms of learning — escape and avoidance — both of which are powered by the engine of negative reinforcement. Understanding these processes is essential not only for the EPPP but for grasping the behavioral mechanisms that keep anxiety disorders alive in millions of people.

In the previous section, we saw how stimulus control explains the way environmental cues guide behavior — how discriminative stimuli signal when reinforcement is available. Now we turn to what happens when those environmental cues signal something unpleasant. When organisms encounter aversive stimuli, they don't simply endure them passively. They learn, often with striking speed, either to terminate ongoing pain or to head it off entirely. These two patterns — escape and avoidance — are among the most clinically significant concepts in all of operant conditioning, forming the behavioral backbone of anxiety maintenance models and the rationale for exposure-based treatments.

Escape Learning

Consider a common, everyday experience: you step outside on a bitterly cold morning and the wind hits your face like a wall. You pull your scarf up over your nose and mouth, and the sharp sting subsides. The next cold morning, you reach for the scarf faster. This is the essence of escape learning — an organism learns to perform a behavior that terminates an ongoing aversive stimulus, and the removal of that aversive stimulus serves as negative reinforcement, strengthening the escape behavior for the future. Notice the critical sequence: the aversive event is already happening, and the behavior ends it. Taking aspirin to relieve a pounding headache follows the same logic — the headache is present, you swallow the pill, and when the pain subsides, the pill-taking behavior has been negatively reinforced. A rat in a shuttle box learns to jump to the other side when it feels a shock through the floor grid; the moment it jumps, the shock stops, and the jumping response grows stronger.

In clinical settings, escape learning helps explain many patterns that might otherwise seem puzzling. A person experiencing intense social anxiety at a party excuses herself to the restroom, and the immediate wave of relief she feels negatively reinforces that retreat. A child throws a tantrum when given a difficult worksheet, and if the teacher removes the assignment, the tantrum has been escape-reinforced. Recognizing these escape sequences is a critical clinical skill because they reveal the functional relationship between behavior and its consequences — the very information a clinician needs to design an effective intervention.

Avoidance Learning

Escape learning addresses what happens after the aversive stimulus has arrived. But organisms are remarkably adept at learning to act before the unpleasant event ever occurs. Avoidance learning takes place when an organism learns to perform a behavior that prevents an aversive stimulus from happening in the first place. Here, the response is made before the aversive stimulus is presented, typically triggered by warning signals — the discriminative stimuli we discussed in the previous section. A dog hears a tone that has previously preceded a shock, and it jumps to the safe side of the shuttle box before any shock is delivered. A student who once bombed an exam after skipping study sessions now studies diligently whenever exam week approaches, preventing the aversive outcome of failure.

Avoidance learning is deceptively powerful. On the surface, it looks adaptive — the person or organism never has to experience the aversive event. But this very success creates a hidden trap, one that has enormous clinical significance. Because the aversive stimulus never actually occurs, the organism never gets the chance to learn that the situation might no longer be dangerous. The behavior becomes self-perpetuating, locked in place by the absence of disconfirming evidence. This is the phenomenon that makes avoidance the central villain in anxiety disorders.

Two-Factor Theory of Avoidance

If avoidance behavior is maintained by the non-occurrence of an aversive event, a natural question arises: what exactly is reinforcing the avoidance response? After all, the absence of something is a strange kind of reinforcer. O. Hobart Mowrer proposed an elegant answer in 1947, and his model remains one of the most influential accounts of avoidance learning in both experimental and clinical psychology. Mowrer's two-factor theory explains avoidance learning through a seamless combination of classical and operant conditioning working in tandem:

  1. Factor 1 (Classical conditioning): A neutral stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) for fear through pairing with an aversive unconditioned stimulus
  2. Factor 2 (Operant conditioning): The avoidance response is negatively reinforced by reduction of the conditioned fear

Think of it this way: a child is bitten by a dog in a park. Through classical conditioning (Factor 1), the sight of dogs — previously neutral — now elicits a conditioned fear response. The next time the child sees a dog on the sidewalk, her heart races and her palms sweat. She crosses the street to avoid the dog, and the fear immediately drops. That reduction in fear is the negative reinforcer (Factor 2) that strengthens the crossing-the-street response. Critically, she never gets close enough to a friendly dog to learn that most dogs are harmless. The avoidance response, paradoxically reinforced by the relief it provides, walls off the very experiences that could undo the fear.

This theory explains why avoidance behavior is so resistant to extinction — the organism never remains in the presence of the feared stimulus long enough for extinction to occur. Each successful avoidance resets the cycle: the warning signal triggers fear, the avoidance response reduces fear, and the conditioned association is preserved rather than weakened. It is a self-sealing loop, and breaking it requires deliberate intervention — which is precisely what exposure-based therapies are designed to do.

Clinical Implications: Anxiety Maintenance

Two-factor theory provides a behavioral account of anxiety disorder maintenance. Avoidance prevents exposure to feared situations, thereby preventing extinction of conditioned fear. This explains why exposure therapy, which prevents avoidance and allows extinction to occur, is effective for anxiety disorders. When a therapist guides a client with agoraphobia to remain in a crowded supermarket rather than fleeing, the conditioned fear response gradually diminishes because Factor 2 — the reinforcing relief of escape — is blocked, and the classical conditioning from Factor 1 can finally undergo extinction. The theory also explains why reassurance-seeking and safety behaviors maintain anxiety (Salkovskis, 1991). A client who carries a "just-in-case" benzodiazepine in her purse, even if she never takes it, is engaging in a subtle form of avoidance that prevents full disconfirmation of the feared outcome. Identifying and systematically reducing these safety behaviors is a core component of modern cognitive-behavioral treatment for anxiety disorders.

Clinical Applications

Understanding how escape and avoidance learning maintains anxiety disorders naturally raises a broader question: if operant conditioning principles can explain why people stay stuck, can those same principles help people get unstuck? The answer, emphatically, is yes. From treating autism spectrum disorder to addressing substance dependence, from alleviating depression to managing classroom behavior, operant conditioning is not just a laboratory curiosity — it is the engine driving some of the most effective interventions in clinical psychology today. For the EPPP, you need to know not only the theoretical principles we have covered throughout this chapter, but also how those principles translate into real-world treatments that clinicians use every day.

Operant conditioning principles form the foundation for numerous evidence-based interventions across clinical, educational, and organizational settings. What makes these applications powerful is their precision: each one identifies specific contingencies — the relationships between behaviors and their consequences — and then systematically modifies those contingencies to produce meaningful behavior change. Let's examine the most important of these applications.

Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)

Imagine a four-year-old child with autism spectrum disorder who rarely makes eye contact, does not respond to his name, and engages in repetitive hand-flapping that interferes with learning. A clinician designs a structured intervention: every time the child makes eye contact after his name is called, he receives enthusiastic praise and a brief turn with a favorite toy. Over weeks and months of consistent practice, the child begins making eye contact spontaneously, responding to his name across settings, and engaging in more social interaction. This is Applied Behavior Analysis in action — the systematic application of operant conditioning principles to improve socially significant behaviors. ABA is characterized by its unwavering focus on observable behavior, its reliance on data-based decision making (clinicians graph and analyze behavioral data continuously), and its emphasis on outcomes that genuinely matter in a person's life. Since Ivar Lovaas's pioneering research demonstrated dramatic improvements in children with autism (Lovaas, 1987), ABA has become the primary evidence-based treatment for autism spectrum disorder, supported by decades of research. It is also applied in schools, residential settings, and organizational contexts wherever precise behavior change is the goal.

Behavioral Activation

Consider a college student who, after a painful breakup, stops going to class, withdraws from friends, and spends most of the day in bed. The fewer activities she engages in, the worse she feels — and the worse she feels, the less she does. This downward spiral captures the operant logic behind depression. Behavioral activation for depression is grounded in a deceptively simple but powerful operant insight: depression is conceptualized as a state of low positive reinforcement. Depressed individuals gradually withdraw from pleasurable and meaningful activities, which means they receive less and less positive reinforcement from their environment, which deepens their depressed mood, which drives further withdrawal. Treatment involves systematically increasing engagement in reinforcing activities — scheduling pleasant events, reestablishing social contacts, and resuming goal-directed behaviors — thereby breaking the cycle of withdrawal and reduced reinforcement (Martell et al., 2010). Importantly, behavioral activation does not wait for motivation to return before prescribing action; instead, it leverages the operant principle that behavior change precedes mood change. The student doesn't need to feel like going to class — she goes, contacts the reinforcing environment, and the improved mood follows. Research has shown behavioral activation to be as effective as cognitive therapy and antidepressant medication for moderate to severe depression, making it a critical intervention for EPPP examinees to understand.

Contingency Management

Substance use disorders pose a particular treatment challenge: the reinforcement delivered by drugs is immediate, potent, and physiologically compelling, while the benefits of sobriety are delayed and abstract. How do you compete with a reinforcer that powerful? Contingency management interventions answer this question by providing tangible reinforcers contingent on evidence of desired behavior change — typically verified drug abstinence through urine testing. These interventions essentially introduce a competing source of immediate reinforcement that rewards sobriety. They have demonstrated robust efficacy for substance use disorders, and they are particularly valuable for stimulant use disorders (cocaine, methamphetamine), where effective pharmacological treatments remain limited (Higgins et al., 2004). The logic is pure operant conditioning: if you want to increase a behavior, reinforce it immediately and consistently.

Example: Voucher-Based Contingency Management

In voucher-based contingency management for cocaine dependence, patients receive vouchers exchangeable for retail goods — such as movie tickets, sporting goods, or restaurant gift certificates — contingent on cocaine-negative urine specimens. The schedule is designed to maximize motivation: voucher values typically start low (perhaps $2.50) and increase with each consecutive negative specimen, potentially reaching significant sums over several weeks of sustained abstinence. A reset contingency adds an important element: if a patient provides a positive specimen, the voucher value drops back to its initial low level, creating a powerful incentive to maintain the consecutive streak. This escalating-with-reset design leverages both positive reinforcement (increasing rewards for sustained abstinence) and a form of response cost (loss of accumulated value after a lapse). Meta-analyses demonstrate significant effects on drug abstinence outcomes, making contingency management one of the best-supported behavioral treatments for substance use disorders.

Functional Behavior Assessment

A seven-year-old boy in a second-grade classroom throws his worksheet on the floor whenever independent math work is assigned. His teacher has tried reprimands, time-outs, and loss of recess — none have worked. Why? Because all of these interventions assume the function of the behavior without actually assessing it. If the child throws his worksheet because math problems are aversive and the disruption leads to removal from the task (even temporarily), then the behavior is maintained by negative reinforcement — escape from demands. In that case, a time-out that removes the child from the classroom actually reinforces the very behavior the teacher is trying to eliminate. Functional Behavior Assessment (FBA) is the process designed to prevent exactly this kind of mistake. It involves systematically identifying the environmental variables that maintain problem behavior — determining what triggers the behavior and what consequence keeps it going. By identifying the function of behavior (what reinforces it), clinicians can develop function-based interventions that address the maintaining contingencies rather than simply suppressing behavior through punishment, which often fails or produces unwanted side effects (Hanley et al., 2003). For the worksheet-throwing child, an effective function-based intervention might involve teaching him to request a break appropriately (a replacement behavior that serves the same escape function) while ensuring that throwing worksheets no longer produces escape from the task.

For the EPPP, remember that the same topography (what the behavior looks like) can serve entirely different functions across individuals — two children may both hit their peers, but one does it to gain attention and the other to escape a noisy environment. Effective intervention depends entirely on correctly identifying the function, which is why functional behavior assessment is considered a prerequisite to any well-designed behavior intervention plan.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

Commonly Tested Topics for the EPPP

  • Positive vs. Negative: These terms refer to adding (+) or removing (-) stimuli, NOT to pleasant/unpleasant
  • Reinforcement vs. Punishment: Reinforcement increases behavior; punishment decreases behavior
  • Negative Reinforcement: Commonly confused with punishment - remember it INCREASES behavior through stimulus removal
  • Schedules: VR produces highest rate; variable schedules produce greatest resistance to extinction
  • Partial Reinforcement Effect: Intermittent reinforcement produces greater resistance to extinction than continuous
  • Shaping vs. Chaining: Shaping teaches one behavior through successive approximations; chaining links multiple behaviors
  • Discriminative Stimulus: Signals reinforcement availability; sets occasion for behavior (does not elicit it)
  • Three-Term Contingency: Antecedent → Behavior → Consequence (ABC)
  • Two-Factor Theory: Classical conditioning (fear) + operant conditioning (avoidance) explains anxiety maintenance
  • Escape vs. Avoidance: Escape terminates present aversive; avoidance prevents future aversive
  • Skinner: Operant conditioning, radical behaviorism, schedules of reinforcement
  • Thorndike: Law of Effect, puzzle box research
