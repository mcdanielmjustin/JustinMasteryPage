Introduction to Research Designs

Imagine a clinical psychologist reading about a new therapy for PTSD that claims to reduce symptoms by 60%. Before recommending it to patients, she needs to ask a critical question: How do we know the therapy actually caused that improvement? Perhaps participants would have improved on their own. Perhaps they also started exercising more, or the simple act of receiving attention from a caring professional did the heavy lifting. The answer to her question depends almost entirely on how the study was designed — and that is why understanding research design is one of the most consequential skills a psychologist can develop.

Research design refers to the overall strategy that integrates the different components of a study — its participants, variables, procedures, and analyses — in a coherent and logical manner, ensuring the research question is effectively addressed. Think of it as the architectural blueprint for a study: just as an architect's choices about foundations, load-bearing walls, and materials determine whether a building can withstand a storm, a researcher's design choices determine whether the study can withstand scientific scrutiny. The choice of research design ultimately determines the type of conclusions that can be drawn, particularly regarding whether one variable actually causes a change in another (Shadish et al., 2002). For psychologists who must evaluate the quality of evidence supporting clinical interventions and make evidence-based decisions in practice, fluency in research design is not optional — it is foundational.

To navigate this territory, you will need a working vocabulary of the core concepts that distinguish one design from another. These terms will recur throughout the chapter, so let's establish them clearly before moving forward.

Key Terminology

  • True Experiment: A design with random assignment, manipulation of the independent variable, and control of extraneous variables
  • Quasi-Experiment: A design lacking random assignment but including manipulation of the independent variable
  • Random Assignment: Procedure ensuring each participant has an equal chance of being assigned to any condition
  • Control Group: A comparison condition that does not receive the experimental treatment
  • Between-Subjects Design: Different participants in each condition
  • Within-Subjects Design: Same participants tested in all conditions

Notice how these terms connect to one another in a logical hierarchy. A true experiment uses random assignment to place participants into either a treatment group or a control group, then manipulates an independent variable while holding everything else constant. A quasi-experiment attempts something similar but lacks the critical ingredient of random assignment — perhaps because the researcher is studying pre-existing groups (e.g., comparing students at two different schools) or because ethical constraints prevent random assignment (e.g., you cannot randomly assign people to experience childhood trauma). This single difference — the presence or absence of random assignment — has profound consequences for the conclusions a study can support.

Consider a concrete example. Suppose a researcher wants to test whether a mindfulness app reduces test anxiety in college students. In a between-subjects design, she randomly assigns half the students to use the app for four weeks while the other half uses a relaxation podcast; each student experiences only one condition, and the researcher compares anxiety scores between the two groups. Alternatively, in a within-subjects design, the same students might complete a test after using the app for four weeks and another test after a four-week period of using the podcast, so each person serves as their own comparison. Each approach has distinct strengths and vulnerabilities — within-subjects designs eliminate individual differences as a confound but introduce order effects, while between-subjects designs avoid order effects but require more participants to achieve the same statistical power.

Research designs exist on a continuum of internal validity, with true experimental designs providing the strongest evidence for causal inference and observational designs providing the weakest. This continuum is one of the most important organizing frameworks in this chapter: as you move from true experiments to quasi-experiments to correlational and descriptive designs, your confidence that "X caused Y" progressively diminishes. However, designs with lower internal validity may have compensating advantages in external validity — the degree to which findings generalize to real-world settings — or in practical feasibility (Cook & Campbell, 1979). A tightly controlled laboratory experiment may tell you with confidence that a therapy works under ideal conditions, but a well-designed naturalistic study may reveal how that therapy actually performs in the messy reality of community mental health clinics. The art of research lies in selecting the design that best balances these trade-offs for the question at hand.

True Experimental Designs

Having established the vocabulary and the internal-validity continuum that organizes all research designs, we begin at the gold standard. When a clinical researcher wants to know whether a treatment genuinely causes improvement — not merely correlates with it — the true experiment is the design of choice. Its three hallmark features — manipulation of at least one independent variable, random assignment of participants to conditions, and control over extraneous variables — work together to rule out alternative explanations and support strong causal inferences (Kazdin, 2017).

Pretest-Posttest Control Group Design

The pretest-posttest control group design includes random assignment of participants to treatment and control groups, with measurements taken before and after treatment. This design allows researchers to assess change over time and control for pre-existing differences between groups.

Research Example

A researcher randomly assigns 60 patients with depression to either cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) or a waitlist control. Both groups complete the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) before and after the 12-week treatment period. The researcher can compare change scores between groups to determine if CBT produces significantly greater improvement than the passage of time alone.

Posttest-Only Control Group Design

When pretesting may sensitize participants to the treatment or when random assignment is expected to produce equivalent groups, researchers may use a posttest-only control group design. This design omits the pretest, relying on random assignment to equate groups prior to treatment.

Solomon Four-Group Design

The Solomon four-group design combines elements of both previous designs, using four groups: two receive pretests (one treatment, one control) and two do not (one treatment, one control). This design allows researchers to detect and control for testing effects and sensitization (Solomon, 1949).

Solomon Four-Group Design Structure

  • Group 1: Pretest → Treatment → Posttest
  • Group 2: Pretest → No Treatment → Posttest
  • Group 3: No Pretest → Treatment → Posttest
  • Group 4: No Pretest → No Treatment → Posttest

Factorial Designs

Factorial designs manipulate two or more independent variables simultaneously, allowing researchers to examine both main effects and interaction effects. A 2 × 2 factorial design, for example, has two independent variables, each with two levels, creating four conditions.

Factorial Design Example

A researcher examines the effects of therapy type (CBT vs. psychodynamic) and medication (present vs. absent) on anxiety symptoms using a 2 × 2 factorial design. This allows examination of: (1) the main effect of therapy type, (2) the main effect of medication, and (3) the interaction between therapy and medication (e.g., whether CBT is particularly effective when combined with medication).

When a factorial design yields both statistically significant main effects and a significant interaction effect, the interaction must be interpreted first because it qualifies the meaning of the main effects. A main effect represents the overall influence of one independent variable averaged across levels of the other, but this average can be misleading when the relationship between one factor and the outcome differs depending on the level of the second factor. For instance, in a 2 × 2 design examining therapy type and medication, a main effect might suggest that CBT outperforms psychodynamic therapy overall. However, if a significant interaction reveals that CBT is superior only when combined with medication—and that the two therapies are equally effective without medication—reporting only the main effect would produce an incomplete and potentially erroneous conclusion. In the presence of a significant interaction, researchers should examine simple effects (the effect of one factor at each level of the other) rather than relying on the collapsed main effect averages.

Clinical Note

On the EPPP, if a question describes significant main effects and a significant interaction, the correct interpretive strategy is to focus on the interaction first. The main effects, taken alone, can obscure important conditional relationships between variables and lead to oversimplified or incorrect clinical recommendations.

Quasi-Experimental Designs

True experiments set the standard for causal inference, but the real world of clinical practice and public policy often makes their requirements impossible to meet. A school district wants to evaluate a new anti-bullying program but cannot randomly assign students to different schools. A hospital administrator needs to assess the impact of a staffing change that has already been implemented. In these common situations, researchers turn to quasi-experimental designs, which retain the manipulation of an independent variable but lack the critical ingredient of random assignment (Shadish et al., 2002). While they provide weaker evidence for causation than true experiments, thoughtful quasi-experimental designs can yield valuable findings when the alternatives are no evidence at all.

Nonequivalent Control Group Design

The nonequivalent control group design resembles the pretest-posttest control group design but lacks random assignment. Participants are assigned to conditions based on pre-existing group membership (e.g., students in different classrooms). The pretest helps assess initial group equivalence and can be used as a covariate in analysis.

Clinical Considerations

In clinical settings, quasi-experimental designs are often the most feasible option. For example, comparing outcomes between patients who choose medication versus therapy cannot use random assignment ethically if patients have strong preferences. Careful matching and statistical controls can strengthen causal inferences in such studies.

Interrupted Time-Series Design

The interrupted time-series design involves multiple measurements before and after an intervention, allowing researchers to examine trends over time and detect changes associated with the intervention. This design is particularly useful for evaluating policy changes or community-level interventions (Biglan et al., 2000).

Time-Series Example

A hospital implements a new suicide prevention protocol and tracks monthly suicide attempts for two years before and two years after implementation. By examining the pattern of data points, researchers can determine whether the intervention was associated with a change in level or trend of suicide attempts.

The interrupted time-series design can be understood as a within-subjects approach applied at the group level—essentially a group-level analogue of the single-subject AB design. Just as an AB design establishes a baseline for one individual and then introduces treatment, the time-series design collects repeated measurements from a group (or population) before and after an intervention is introduced. The pre-intervention observations serve as the baseline (A phase), and the post-intervention observations constitute the treatment phase (B phase). The critical advantage over a simple AB design is the multiple data points on each side of the intervention, which allow researchers to model pre-existing trends and distinguish genuine intervention effects from patterns that were already in progress. Despite this improvement, the basic interrupted time-series design shares the AB design's core limitation: without a comparison group, rival explanations such as concurrent historical events remain plausible threats to internal validity.

Regression Discontinuity Design

The regression discontinuity design assigns participants to conditions based on a cutoff score on a pretest measure. Those above the cutoff receive one treatment while those below receive another. The design examines whether there is a discontinuity (jump or drop) in the outcome variable at the cutoff point, which would indicate a treatment effect.

This design has stronger internal validity than other quasi-experimental designs because assignment is based on a known, measured variable (Trochim, 1984).

Single-Subject (Single-Case) Designs

Both true and quasi-experimental designs compare groups of participants — but a behavior analyst working one-on-one with a child exhibiting self-injurious behavior needs to evaluate whether her intervention is working for this specific client, right now. Group averages are beside the point. Single-subject designs, also called single-case experimental designs or N-of-1 designs, address this need by using individual participants as their own controls through repeated measurement of behavior over time and systematic introduction and withdrawal of interventions. They are widely used in applied behavior analysis and clinical psychology (Barlow et al., 2009).

AB Design

The simplest single-subject design is the AB design, consisting of a baseline phase (A) followed by a treatment phase (B). While useful for clinical monitoring, this design provides weak evidence for causation because changes could be due to maturation, history, or other factors rather than the treatment.

ABAB (Withdrawal/Reversal) Design

The ABAB design strengthens causal inference by adding withdrawal and reintroduction phases. The sequence is: baseline (A) → treatment (B) → withdrawal (A) → treatment (B). If behavior improves during treatment phases and deteriorates during withdrawal phases, this provides compelling evidence that the treatment caused the change.

ABAB Design Logic

The withdrawal phase tests whether behavior is maintained by the treatment. If improvements persist after treatment withdrawal, the treatment may have produced lasting change—or other factors may be responsible for the improvement. If behavior returns to baseline levels during withdrawal, this strengthens the inference that the treatment was responsible for initial improvement.

Ethical Considerations

ABAB designs may be inappropriate when withdrawal of an effective treatment poses risks to the client (e.g., self-injurious behavior). In such cases, multiple baseline designs provide an ethical alternative that maintains experimental control without requiring treatment withdrawal.

Multiple Baseline Design

The multiple baseline design introduces treatment at different points in time across multiple baselines. These baselines can be across behaviors (different behaviors in one person), across subjects (same behavior in different people), or across settings (same behavior and person in different environments).

Multiple Baseline Across Behaviors

A therapist targets three problem behaviors in a child with autism: hand-flapping, echolalia, and tantrums. After establishing baselines for all three, she implements a token economy first for hand-flapping. Once reduction is observed, she extends the intervention to echolalia, then to tantrums. If each behavior decreases only when the intervention is applied to it, this demonstrates experimental control without requiring withdrawal.

Changing Criterion Design

The changing criterion design involves systematically changing the criterion for reinforcement over time. Experimental control is demonstrated when behavior changes to match each new criterion. This design is particularly useful for shaping behaviors that should increase or decrease gradually (Hartmann & Hall, 1976).

Alternating Treatments Design

The alternating treatments design (also called multi-element design) rapidly alternates between two or more treatments, with different treatments applied in counterbalanced order. This allows direct comparison of treatment effectiveness within a single subject.

Non-Experimental Designs

So far, every design we have examined involves some form of active manipulation — the researcher deliberately introduces, withdraws, or varies a treatment. But many of the most important questions in psychology cannot be studied through manipulation. You cannot randomly assign people to experience childhood abuse, develop schizophrenia, or grow up in poverty. Non-experimental designs address these questions by examining naturally occurring variation, and while they cannot establish causation, they are invaluable for describing phenomena, identifying relationships, and generating hypotheses that experimental designs can later test.

Correlational Research

Correlational research examines relationships between variables without manipulation. While correlation does not imply causation, sophisticated correlational techniques such as structural equation modeling and path analysis can test theoretical models about causal relationships (Kline, 2015).

Survey Research

Survey research uses questionnaires or interviews to collect data from a sample, typically to describe characteristics of a population or examine relationships between variables. Representative sampling is critical for generalizing survey results to the population of interest.

Cross-Sectional vs. Longitudinal Designs

Cross-sectional designs collect data at a single point in time, comparing different groups (e.g., different age groups) simultaneously. While efficient, they cannot distinguish age effects from cohort effects.

Longitudinal designs follow the same participants over time, allowing researchers to track individual change and development. However, they are expensive, time-consuming, and vulnerable to attrition.

Cohort-Sequential Design

The cohort-sequential design combines cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches by following multiple cohorts over time. This allows researchers to separate age effects, cohort effects, and time-of-measurement effects (Schaie, 1965).

Case-Control Studies

Case-control studies compare individuals with a condition (cases) to similar individuals without the condition (controls), examining differences in past exposures or characteristics. These retrospective designs are efficient for studying rare conditions but are vulnerable to recall bias and cannot establish causation.

Cohort Studies

Cohort studies follow groups with different exposures forward in time to observe differences in outcomes. Prospective cohort studies are stronger than retrospective designs because exposure is measured before outcomes occur, reducing certain biases.

Between-Subjects vs. Within-Subjects Designs

Cutting across the experimental, quasi-experimental, and non-experimental categories is another fundamental design choice: should different people experience each condition, or should the same people experience all conditions? This between-subjects versus within-subjects distinction has far-reaching consequences for statistical power, the types of confounds you face, and the number of participants you need.

Between-Subjects (Independent Groups) Designs

In between-subjects designs, different participants are assigned to each condition. Random assignment creates groups that are expected to be equivalent on all variables except the independent variable. The main advantage is avoiding carryover effects; the main disadvantage is requiring more participants and introducing error variance from individual differences.

Within-Subjects (Repeated Measures) Designs

In within-subjects designs, the same participants experience all conditions. This eliminates individual difference variance and requires fewer participants. However, these designs are vulnerable to order effects, including practice effects, fatigue, and carryover.

Counterbalancing

Counterbalancing addresses order effects by varying the sequence of conditions across participants. In complete counterbalancing, all possible orders are used equally. In Latin square counterbalancing, a subset of orders is used such that each condition appears equally often in each position.

Mixed Designs

Mixed designs (also called split-plot designs) include both between-subjects and within-subjects factors. For example, a study might compare two therapy groups (between-subjects) measured at multiple time points (within-subjects).

Sampling Methods

Having explored the architectural decisions that shape a study — whether participants experience one condition or many, whether factors are crossed or nested — we now turn to a question that precedes all of those design choices: Who ends up in the study in the first place? This is not a trivial detail. Imagine a clinical trial testing a new intervention for social anxiety disorder. The treatment could be genuinely effective, and the design could be flawless, but if the sample consists entirely of college sophomores recruited from an introductory psychology course, clinicians working with middle-aged professionals or older adults in community mental health settings may reasonably wonder whether those findings apply to their clients at all.

The method used to select participants determines the degree to which research findings can be generalized beyond the specific people who were studied. At its core, sampling is about bridging the gap between the population you want to understand and the subset of individuals you can actually observe. Get this bridge right, and your conclusions travel well; get it wrong, and even the most elegant analysis rests on a shaky foundation. Two broad families of sampling strategies exist, and they differ fundamentally in the confidence they afford about representativeness. Probability sampling methods give every member of the population a known, nonzero chance of being selected, which provides a mathematical basis for statistical generalization. In contrast, nonprobability methods sacrifice that guarantee — sometimes deliberately and for good reason — but they carry important limitations that researchers must acknowledge.

Probability vs. Nonprobability Sampling Methods
Probability Sampling
  • Every member has known, nonzero selection chance
  • Supports statistical generalization to population
  • Simple Random — equal chance, unbiased
  • Stratified Random — ensures subgroup representation
  • Cluster — selects groups, practical for dispersed populations
  • Systematic — every kth member from a list
Nonprobability Sampling
  • No guarantee of known selection probability
  • Limits formal statistical generalization
  • Convenience — readily available participants
  • Purposive — handpicked for specific characteristics
  • Snowball — participants recruit from social networks
  • Best for exploratory, qualitative, hidden populations

Types of Probability Sampling

The split comparison above captures the broad distinction; now let's examine each probability method in greater detail. Consider a researcher who wants to study burnout rates among licensed psychologists in the United States. In principle, every licensed psychologist should have a chance of being included — but how that chance is structured varies considerably across the four methods below.

Method Description Advantage
Simple Random Every member has equal chance of selection Unbiased representation
Stratified Random Population divided into strata; random sampling within each Ensures representation of subgroups
Cluster Randomly select clusters (e.g., schools), sample all within Practical for geographically dispersed populations
Systematic Select every kth member from a list Simple to implement

To make these methods concrete, return to the burnout study. With simple random sampling, the researcher would obtain a comprehensive list of all licensed psychologists and use a random-number generator to select participants — like drawing names from an enormous hat. This approach is theoretically ideal because it produces an unbiased sample, but it requires a complete sampling frame, which can be difficult or impossible to obtain for many populations.

With stratified random sampling, the researcher might first divide the population into meaningful subgroups — or strata — such as clinical psychologists, counseling psychologists, school psychologists, and neuropsychologists, and then randomly sample within each stratum. This guarantees that smaller specialty areas are not accidentally underrepresented, which is particularly valuable when subgroup comparisons are planned. Stratified sampling often produces more precise estimates than simple random sampling, especially when the variable of interest (here, burnout) differs across strata.

Cluster sampling takes a different practical approach. Rather than sampling individuals directly, the researcher randomly selects naturally occurring groups — clusters — and then studies everyone (or a random subset) within those clusters. For instance, the researcher might randomly select 30 states, then survey all licensed psychologists within those states. This dramatically reduces logistical costs when the population is geographically dispersed, though it introduces an additional source of sampling error because individuals within a cluster tend to be more similar to each other than to the population at large.

Finally, systematic sampling offers procedural simplicity: the researcher picks a random starting point on a list and then selects every kth name (e.g., every 50th person on a licensure registry). This method works well when the list has no hidden periodic pattern that could bias the sample. For example, if an alphabetical list alternated between two practice settings in a predictable way, systematic sampling could inadvertently over- or underrepresent one setting.

Nonprobability Sampling

In an ideal world, every study would use probability sampling to ensure clean generalization from sample to population. In practice, however, probability sampling is often impractical, prohibitively expensive, or simply unnecessary for the research question at hand. Nonprobability sampling methods do not ensure that every member of the population has a known chance of being selected, which limits formal statistical generalization — but they remain indispensable tools in both clinical research and qualitative inquiry.

The most common form is convenience sampling, in which researchers recruit whoever is readily available — students enrolled in a psychology course, patients presenting at a particular clinic, or visitors to a website. The vast majority of published psychological research relies on convenience samples, which is why replication across diverse samples matters so much. Purposive sampling (sometimes called judgmental sampling) takes a more deliberate approach: the researcher handpicks participants because they possess specific characteristics relevant to the research question. A qualitative study exploring the lived experience of psychologists who transitioned from private practice to telehealth during a pandemic, for example, would specifically seek out individuals with that experience rather than sampling at random. Snowball sampling leverages social networks — initial participants recruit additional participants from among their acquaintances. This method is especially valuable when studying hard-to-reach or hidden populations, such as undocumented immigrants seeking mental health services or individuals in recovery from substance use who are not currently connected to formal treatment programs.

For the EPPP, remember the essential distinction: probability sampling supports statistical generalization to a defined population, while nonprobability sampling limits generalizability but may be the only feasible or appropriate strategy, particularly in exploratory, qualitative, and clinical research contexts. Understanding which method was used — and its implications — is critical to evaluating the external validity of any study you encounter.
