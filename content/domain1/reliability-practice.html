Types of Reliability

The previous chapter established the theoretical foundation of Classical Test Theory — the X = T + E equation, the concept of the reliability coefficient, and the emerging alternative of Item Response Theory. With that framework in place, a practical question arises: how do we actually estimate reliability for a given test? The answer depends on which source of measurement error concerns us most. A personality inventory used to track therapeutic progress needs to demonstrate stability over time. A licensing exam offered in multiple versions needs to show equivalence across forms. A single-administration achievement test needs items that pull together to measure the same construct. Each of these concerns maps onto a different type of reliability, and choosing the appropriate one depends on the nature of the test and how it will be used (AERA et al., 2014).

Test-Retest Reliability

Test-retest reliability (also called temporal stability) assesses the consistency of scores across time. The same test is administered to the same individuals on two occasions, and the correlation between scores indicates stability.

Test-Retest Reliability

  • Method: Administer same test twice; correlate scores
  • Source of error: Time sampling (changes in examinees over time)
  • Considerations: Interval between testings, memory/practice effects, true changes in the construct

Optimal Testing Interval

The appropriate interval depends on the construct being measured:

  • Shorter intervals (days to weeks): Minimize true change but increase practice effects
  • Longer intervals (months): Reduce practice effects but allow more true change
  • Stable traits (intelligence, personality): Longer intervals are appropriate
  • Variable states (mood, anxiety): Very short intervals or alternative methods

Alternate (Parallel) Forms Reliability

Alternate forms reliability assesses consistency across different versions of the same test. Two equivalent forms are administered to the same individuals, and the correlation between forms indicates reliability. This method controls for memory effects and provides evidence of content sampling consistency.

Alternate Forms Example

A publisher develops Form A and Form B of an achievement test, each with different items measuring the same content domain. Students take Form A one week and Form B the next. A correlation of .85 between forms indicates good alternate forms reliability, suggesting the two versions measure the same construct consistently.

For forms to be truly parallel, they must have equal means, equal variances, and equal correlations with other measures. In practice, this is difficult to achieve, so most alternate forms are better described as "equivalent" rather than strictly parallel.

Internal Consistency Reliability

Internal consistency assesses the homogeneity of items within a single test administration. It indicates the extent to which items measure the same construct and is the most commonly reported type of reliability.

Split-Half Reliability

The test is divided into two halves (typically odd vs. even items), and the correlation between halves is calculated. Because each half is only half as long as the full test, the Spearman-Brown formula is used to estimate the reliability of the full-length test.

Spearman-Brown Prophecy Formula

rxx = (2 × rhh) / (1 + rhh)

Where rhh is the correlation between the two halves.

For example, if the correlation between halves is .60, the estimated full-test reliability is:

(2 × .60) / (1 + .60) = 1.20 / 1.60 = .75

Cronbach's Alpha (Coefficient Alpha)

Cronbach's alpha (α) is the most widely used measure of internal consistency. It represents the average of all possible split-half reliabilities and indicates how well items hang together as a set. Alpha is appropriate for items with multiple response options (Cronbach, 1951).

Alpha values are interpreted as follows:

Alpha Value Interpretation
≥ .90 Excellent (individual decision-making)
.80 - .89 Good
.70 - .79 Acceptable (research purposes)
.60 - .69 Questionable
< .60 Poor/Unacceptable

Kuder-Richardson Formulas

The Kuder-Richardson formulas are special cases of Cronbach's alpha for dichotomously scored items (correct/incorrect). KR-20 is used when item difficulties vary, while KR-21 assumes equal item difficulties (Kuder & Richardson, 1937).

Limitations of Internal Consistency

  • May be inappropriately high for speeded tests (items are not independently answered)
  • Does not assess stability over time
  • May be artificially inflated by redundant items
  • Not appropriate for multidimensional tests (should be calculated separately for each subscale)

Inter-Rater (Inter-Scorer) Reliability

Inter-rater reliability assesses the consistency of scores assigned by different raters or scorers. This is essential for tests involving subjective judgment, such as essay tests, behavioral observations, projective tests, and structured interviews.

A significant threat to the accuracy of inter-rater reliability estimates is consensual observer drift (sometimes called observer drift or rater drift). This phenomenon occurs when two or more raters who work closely together gradually develop a shared, idiosyncratic interpretation of the scoring criteria that deviates from the original operational definitions. Over time, the raters' scoring systems converge with each other but diverge from the intended scoring standards. Because the raters are drifting in the same direction together, their agreement remains high—or even increases—producing an artificially inflated inter-rater reliability coefficient that does not reflect true scoring accuracy.

The core problem is that high agreement between raters is mistaken for accurate measurement. Two observers may consistently agree on ratings while both systematically misapplying the scoring rubric. This inflated consistency masks a decline in scoring validity. Consensual drift is most likely when raters are trained together and continue to observe or score together over extended periods without independent calibration checks.

Clinical Note

To guard against consensual observer drift, researchers and clinicians should periodically retrain raters against criterion standards ("gold standard" recordings or master-rated exemplars), rotate rater pairings so that observers do not always work alongside the same partner, and include independent reliability checks with outside raters who have not been exposed to the local scoring culture. These strategies help ensure that inter-rater agreement reflects genuine adherence to scoring criteria rather than shared deviation from them.

Methods of Assessment

  • Percent agreement: Simple but does not account for chance agreement
  • Cohen's kappa (κ): Corrects for chance agreement; used for categorical ratings
  • Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC): Used for continuous ratings; accounts for both agreement and consistency

Cohen's Kappa Interpretation

  • κ < .20: Poor agreement
  • κ = .21 - .40: Fair agreement
  • κ = .41 - .60: Moderate agreement
  • κ = .61 - .80: Substantial agreement
  • κ > .80: Almost perfect agreement

Standard Error of Measurement

Reliability coefficients tell you about the overall consistency of a test across a group of examinees — but a clinician sitting across from a single client needs a different kind of information. If this client scored 112 on an IQ test, how far might that observed score be from her true score? Could her true ability be as low as 105 or as high as 119? The standard error of measurement (SEM) answers this question by quantifying the amount of error expected in an individual's observed score, translating the abstract reliability coefficient into a concrete band of uncertainty around any single score (Harvill, 1991).

SEM Formula

SEM = SD × √(1 - rxx)

Where SD is the standard deviation of test scores and rxx is the reliability coefficient.

SEM Calculation Example

A test has M = 100, SD = 15, and reliability = .91

SEM = 15 × √(1 - .91) = 15 × √.09 = 15 × .30 = 4.5

This means the typical measurement error is about 4.5 points.

Confidence Intervals

The flow diagram above walks through the full sequence from SEM calculation to confidence interval construction. The step labeled "Choose Confidence Level" is where clinical judgment enters: a 68% band (plus or minus 1 SEM) gives a rough sense of likely true-score range, while a 95% band (plus or minus 1.96 SEM) provides the level of certainty typically expected in clinical reporting. Clinicians should report and interpret these intervals rather than treating observed scores as exact values — especially when scores fall near diagnostic cutoffs, where a few points can shift a classification.

Standard Error of the Difference

When comparing two scores (e.g., Verbal IQ vs. Performance IQ, or pretest vs. posttest), the standard error of the difference (SEdiff) is used to determine whether the difference is statistically significant:

SEdiff = √(SEM₁² + SEM₂²)

A difference is typically considered significant if it exceeds 1.96 × SEdiff (for 95% confidence).

Factors Affecting Reliability

Understanding how to compute the SEM and build confidence intervals gives you the tools to interpret individual scores with appropriate caution. But what if you want to improve a test's reliability in the first place — to shrink the SEM and tighten those confidence bands? Several factors systematically influence how reliable a test's scores will be, and knowing them empowers both test developers and clinicians who must evaluate whether a given instrument is adequate for a particular purpose.

Factors That Increase vs. Decrease Reliability
Increases Reliability
  • Longer test (more items)
  • Greater score variability in sample
  • Items near optimal difficulty (p ≈ .50–.625)
  • High item discrimination (D ≥ .40)
  • Homogeneous item content
  • Standardized administration conditions
  • Well-trained raters
  • Constructed-response format (eliminates guessing)
Decreases Reliability
  • Shorter test (fewer items)
  • Range restriction (homogeneous sample)
  • Items too easy or too hard
  • Low or negative item discrimination
  • Multidimensional item mix
  • Variable testing conditions
  • Consensual observer drift in raters
  • True/false format (50% guessing floor)

Test Length

Longer tests are generally more reliable because they sample the content domain more thoroughly. The Spearman-Brown formula can estimate how reliability changes with test length:

rkk = (k × rxx) / [1 + (k - 1) × rxx]

Where k is the factor by which the test length is multiplied.

Spearman-Brown Example

If a 20-item test has reliability of .70, doubling its length (k = 2) would yield estimated reliability of:

(2 × .70) / [1 + (2 - 1) × .70] = 1.40 / 1.70 = .82

Score Variability (Range Restriction)

Reliability coefficients are higher when scores are more variable. Restriction of range—when scores cluster together—attenuates reliability estimates. This is important when estimating reliability in selected samples (e.g., university students, clinical populations).

Item Quality and Homogeneity

Items that are too easy or too difficult contribute little to reliability because they produce little variability. Items with optimal difficulty (around .50 for maximum discrimination) and high item-total correlations enhance reliability.

Item Difficulty Index (p)

The item difficulty index, symbolized as p, represents the proportion of examinees who answer an item correctly. It is calculated simply as the number of correct responses divided by the total number of examinees.

The item difficulty index ranges from 0 to +1.0. A p value of 0 indicates that no examinee answered the item correctly—making it an extremely difficult item. A p value of +1.0 means every examinee responded correctly, indicating a very easy item. Note the counterintuitive labeling: higher p values denote easier items, not harder ones. For maximum discrimination between high- and low-ability examinees, items with difficulty values near .50 are generally optimal because they produce the greatest score variability. However, when correcting for guessing on multiple-choice items, the optimal p value shifts upward: it is calculated as halfway between perfect performance (1.0) and the probability of a correct guess (1/number of options). For a four-option item, this optimal difficulty is (1.0 + .25) / 2 = .625.

p Value Item Difficulty Level
0 – .20 Very difficult
.21 – .40 Difficult
.41 – .60 Moderate
.61 – .80 Easy
.81 – 1.0 Very easy

Example

On a classroom exam taken by 200 students, 40 answer Item 12 correctly. The item difficulty index is p = 40/200 = .20, classifying this as a very difficult item. If 180 students answer Item 3 correctly, its difficulty index is p = 180/200 = .90, making it a very easy item.

When test developers want to adjust overall test difficulty, they manipulate the average item difficulty index (p) of the items selected. Because lower p values correspond to harder items (fewer examinees answering correctly), adding items with an average p value near 0.20 would substantially increase the difficulty of the test. For example, if a statistics instructor wanted to create a more challenging final examination, selecting items that only about 20% of students typically answer correctly would accomplish this goal. Conversely, to make a test easier, one would select items with higher p values (e.g., p near .80), ensuring that most examinees can respond correctly.

Clinical Note

In practice, constructing a test entirely from very difficult items (p ≈ .20) would compress scores at the low end of the distribution, reducing variability and potentially lowering reliability. Test developers must balance desired difficulty against the need for adequate score spread. A mix of difficulty levels—anchored around the target average—typically yields better psychometric properties than uniformly hard or easy items.

Item Discrimination Index (D)

The item discrimination index (D) quantifies how effectively a single item differentiates between examinees who perform well on the overall test and those who perform poorly. It is calculated by comparing the proportion of correct responses in the upper group (typically the top 27% of scorers) to the proportion correct in the lower group (bottom 27%):

D = Pupper − Plower

The discrimination index ranges from −1.00 to +1.00. A value of +1.0 represents perfect positive discrimination: every examinee in the high-scoring group answered correctly while every examinee in the low-scoring group answered incorrectly. Conversely, a value of −1.0 represents perfect negative discrimination, meaning low-scoring examinees outperformed high-scoring examinees on that item—a clear signal that the item is flawed and likely should be revised or eliminated. A D value of 0.00 indicates the item fails to distinguish between groups at all.

D Value Interpretation Action
≥ .40 Excellent discrimination Retain
.30 – .39 Good discrimination Retain; minor revision possible
.20 – .29 Marginal discrimination Review and consider revision
< .20 Poor discrimination Revise or discard
Negative Inverse discrimination (item is dysfunctional) Remove immediately

Example

On a 100-item psychology exam, Item 47 is answered correctly by 90% of the top-scoring group but only 30% of the bottom-scoring group. The discrimination index is D = .90 − .30 = .60, indicating excellent discrimination. Item 52, however, is answered correctly by 25% of the top group and 55% of the bottom group, yielding D = .25 − .55 = −.30. This negative value suggests the item may be misleading knowledgeable examinees—perhaps due to ambiguous wording—and should be removed or rewritten.

The item difficulty index (p) represents the proportion of examinees who answer an item correctly. Values range from 0 (no one correct) to 1.0 (everyone correct), with higher values indicating easier items. For maximum score variability and discriminating power, items at a difficulty of .50 produce the greatest variance. However, the optimal average difficulty for a full test depends on the item format, because guessing inflates the effective floor of the difficulty scale.

Optimal Average Item Difficulty

The recommended average p value is calculated as the midpoint between perfect performance (1.0) and the probability of answering correctly by chance alone:
Optimal p = (1.0 + chance probability) / 2

For a four-option multiple-choice test, the chance level is .25, so the optimal average difficulty is (1.0 + .25) / 2 = .625. For a five-option format, it is (1.0 + .20) / 2 = .60. For a true/false test, where the guessing probability is .50, the optimal average difficulty becomes (1.0 + .50) / 2 = .75. This higher target accounts for the fact that true/false items have a high guessing baseline—setting average difficulty at .50 would mean many examinees could not be distinguished from chance responders. By centering items around .75, the test maximizes the spread of scores among examinees who genuinely differ in knowledge.

Examinee and Administration Factors

  • Test-taking conditions: Standardized administration enhances reliability
  • Examinee characteristics: Fatigue, anxiety, and motivation affect consistency
  • Guessing: Random guessing adds error variance, reducing reliability
  • Scorer training: Well-trained raters produce more consistent scores

The number of response options per item has a direct impact on reliability through its effect on guessing probability. A true/false test typically yields the lowest reliability coefficient among common item formats because examinees have a 50% chance of guessing correctly on each item, introducing substantial error variance. A three-option multiple-choice format reduces guessing probability to approximately 33%, and a four-option format reduces it further to about 25%. Fill-in-the-blank (constructed-response) items essentially eliminate guessing entirely, since examinees must generate rather than recognize the correct answer. Consequently, when other factors such as test length and content coverage are held constant, true/false items produce the most error variance due to guessing and therefore tend to have the weakest reliability, while constructed-response formats tend to produce stronger reliability because correct responses more accurately reflect true knowledge.

Reliability and Validity

Throughout this chapter, we have treated reliability as a goal in its own right — and it is. But reliability also matters because of its intimate relationship with validity. A test can be perfectly consistent and yet measure the wrong thing entirely, but a test that is inconsistent cannot possibly measure anything well. This asymmetry means that reliability sets an upper limit on validity: a test cannot correlate with an external criterion more highly than the square root of its reliability coefficient. This relationship is expressed as:

rxy(max) = √rxx

Reliability-Validity Relationship

If a predictor test has reliability of .64, the maximum possible validity coefficient is:

√.64 = .80

The test cannot correlate higher than .80 with any criterion, even if the test were a perfect measure of the construct.

The correction for attenuation formula estimates what the correlation between two measures would be if both were perfectly reliable:

rxy(corrected) = rxy / √(rxx × ryy)

This formula is useful for theoretical purposes but should be used cautiously in applied contexts.

Closely related to these concepts is the reliability index, which equals the square root of the reliability coefficient (rxx). Formally expressed as √rxx, the reliability index represents the theoretical correlation between observed scores and true scores. For instance, if a test has a reliability coefficient of .81, its reliability index is √.81 = .90, meaning observed scores correlate .90 with the hypothetical true scores. Note that the reliability index will always be equal to or larger than the reliability coefficient (since taking the square root of a value between 0 and 1 produces a larger number). This concept reinforces why reliability constrains validity: because the reliability index represents the maximum possible correlation between observed scores and any perfectly measured variable, it serves as the theoretical ceiling for criterion-related validity coefficients.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Classical Test Theory: X = T + E; reliability is the proportion of true score variance
  • Test-retest reliability assesses stability over time; affected by testing interval and practice effects
  • Alternate forms reliability requires two equivalent versions of the test
  • Cronbach's alpha is the most common internal consistency measure; represents average of all split-half correlations
  • KR-20 and KR-21 are used for dichotomous items (special cases of alpha)
  • Spearman-Brown formula estimates reliability for different test lengths
  • SEM formula: SEM = SD × √(1 - rxx); used to construct confidence intervals
  • Reliability is higher with longer tests and greater score variability
  • Inter-rater reliability is essential for subjectively scored tests; Cohen's kappa corrects for chance
  • Reliability sets the upper limit for validity: rxy(max) = √rxx
  • IRT vs. CTT: CTT is test-level; IRT is item-level. IRT item parameters are sample-independent; CTT item statistics are not
  • IRT models: 1PL/Rasch (difficulty only), 2PL (+ discrimination), 3PL (+ guessing)
  • Item Characteristic Curves: S-shaped curves showing P(correct) as a function of theta (ability)
  • IRT applications: Computerized adaptive testing (CAT), test equating, DIF detection
  • The EPPP uses IRT-based scoring—scores reflect theta estimates, not raw percentage correct
