Overview of Language Development

Language acquisition is one of the most remarkable achievements of early childhood. By age 5, most children have mastered the basic grammar of their native language and possess a vocabulary of several thousand words -- all without formal instruction. Understanding the components of language, the milestones of acquisition, and the major theoretical perspectives is essential for EPPP preparation.

Language development intersects with cognitive development, social development, and clinical assessment. Delays or disorders in language are among the most common reasons for referral in pediatric and educational settings.

Components of Language

As we noted in the preceding section, language development sits at the crossroads of cognitive, social, and clinical domains — and delays in language are among the most common reasons children are referred for evaluation. But before we can track how language unfolds across the lifespan, we need a shared vocabulary for what language actually consists of. Think of it this way: when a three-year-old says "I goed to the store," that single utterance reveals something about her sound system, her grasp of word-formation rules, her sentence structure, her understanding of meaning, and her ability to communicate in a social context. Each of these dimensions represents a distinct component of language, and each follows its own developmental trajectory. Understanding these components is essential not only for the EPPP but for any clinician who needs to pinpoint exactly where a child's language system is breaking down.

Consider a clinical scenario: a school psychologist receives a referral for a six-year-old boy who "talks funny" and "doesn't make sense." Is the problem with the sounds he produces? With how he strings words together? With his vocabulary and understanding of meaning? Or is he struggling with the social rules of conversation — perhaps speaking in a monotone, missing conversational turns, or failing to adjust his language for different listeners? Each possibility points to a different component of language, and each calls for a different assessment and intervention strategy. Let's examine these components one by one.

Key Terminology

Phonology: The sound system of a language. Phonemes are the smallest units of sound that distinguish meaning (e.g., /b/ and /p/ in "bat" vs. "pat"). English has approximately 44 phonemes. A child who says "wabbit" for "rabbit" is demonstrating a phonological process — she perceives and organizes the sounds of her language differently from an adult speaker.

Morphology: The rules governing the formation of words. Morphemes are the smallest units of meaning (e.g., "unhappiness" contains three morphemes: un- + happy + -ness). Free morphemes can stand alone; bound morphemes (prefixes, suffixes) cannot. When a toddler says "goed" instead of "went," she is actually demonstrating knowledge of a morphological rule — she has learned that adding "-ed" signals past tense, and she is overapplying it.

Syntax: The rules governing how words are combined into sentences. Also called grammar. Syntax is what allows us to recognize that "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" have very different meanings despite containing the same words — word order carries meaning in English.

Semantics: The meaning of words and sentences. Includes understanding of vocabulary and how meaning changes in context. A child who learns that "run" can refer to running in a race, a run in her stocking, or running a business is expanding her semantic knowledge well beyond a simple one-word-one-meaning mapping.

Pragmatics: The social rules governing language use in context -- knowing what to say, how to say it, and when to say it. Includes turn-taking, adjusting speech to the listener, and understanding nonliteral language (irony, metaphor). Pragmatic deficits are particularly notable in autism spectrum disorder, where a child may have a large vocabulary and correct grammar but still struggle to maintain a conversation, interpret sarcasm, or recognize when a listener is confused.

A helpful way to remember these five components is to think of them as layers of a communication system, from the most basic building blocks (sounds) up to the most socially complex (pragmatics). Phonology deals with the sounds we produce, morphology with how those sounds are assembled into meaningful units, syntax with how those meaningful units are arranged into sentences, semantics with the meaning those sentences convey, and pragmatics with how all of it functions in real social interaction. On the EPPP, you may be asked to identify which component is impaired in a clinical vignette — so practice mapping specific language behaviors to their corresponding component.

For the EPPP, it is also critical to distinguish between receptive language (comprehension — understanding what others say) and expressive language (production — generating speech). Receptive language consistently develops ahead of expressive language; children understand far more words than they can produce. A twelve-month-old, for example, may comprehend fifty or more words but produce only a handful. This receptive-expressive gap has important clinical implications: a child who cannot understand language directed at her faces a fundamentally different challenge than one who comprehends everything but struggles to formulate responses. Standardized assessments typically measure these two domains separately, and discrepancies between them can help guide diagnosis and intervention planning.

Pragmatic competence extends well beyond basic conversational turn-taking. Individuals with pragmatic language difficulties—sometimes termed social communication deficits—often struggle to interpret nonliteral forms of speech such as humor, sarcasm, irony, and idiomatic expressions. Because sarcasm requires the listener to recognize that the speaker's intended meaning is the opposite of the literal words, it depends heavily on reading contextual cues, vocal prosody, and the speaker's communicative intent. Similarly, understanding jokes often hinges on detecting ambiguity, incongruity, or violations of conversational expectations. Pragmatic language deficits are frequently observed in individuals with autism spectrum disorder, right hemisphere brain damage, traumatic brain injury, and some forms of specific language impairment. These individuals may interpret sarcastic comments at face value, miss the punchline of jokes, or respond inappropriately to teasing—difficulties that can significantly impair social relationships and contribute to peer rejection or isolation.

Language Development Milestones

Before cooing or babbling begins, crying serves as the neonate's primary communicative tool. Research has identified three distinct cry patterns present from birth, each associated with a different internal state:

Neonatal Cry Types

Hunger cry: A rhythmic, repetitive pattern that typically starts softly and gradually intensifies. It follows a predictable cycle of cry–pause–cry.
Anger cry: Similar in rhythm to the hunger cry but distinguished by greater volume and intensity, with more forceful airflow through the vocal cords producing a harsher acoustic quality.
Pain cry: Characterized by a sudden onset — a long, loud initial wail followed by an extended period of breath-holding (apnea) before the next burst. This pattern is notably different from the more gradual buildup of hunger or anger cries.

Experienced caregivers can often differentiate among these cry types, and the ability to do so facilitates responsive caregiving. From a developmental perspective, these early vocalizations represent the infant's first communicative acts — reflexive and undifferentiated compared to later language, yet functional in eliciting caregiver attention and meeting basic needs. By around 3–4 weeks, infants also begin producing a "fake" or fussy cry that appears more volitional, representing an early step toward intentional communication.

Clinical Note

Atypical cry patterns can serve as early diagnostic indicators. An unusually high-pitched, shrill cry (sometimes called a "cat cry" or cri du chat) is associated with chromosomal abnormalities, specifically a deletion on chromosome 5. Abnormal cry acoustics have also been observed in infants with neurological damage, prenatal substance exposure, and other medical conditions, making cry analysis a potentially useful screening tool in neonatal assessment.

As children begin acquiring their first words, they frequently make systematic errors in how broadly or narrowly they apply those words. Two complementary error patterns are particularly noteworthy:

Overextension

Overextension occurs when a child uses a word too broadly, applying it to objects or people beyond its correct referent based on perceived similarities. For instance, a child who has learned the word "dada" may begin using it to refer to all adult men, not just her father. Similarly, a child might call all four-legged animals "doggy" or use "ball" for any round object, including oranges and the moon. Overextensions are based on salient perceptual features such as shape, size, movement, or texture.

Underextension

Underextension is the opposite pattern: the child applies a word too narrowly, restricting it to only a specific exemplar rather than the full category. For example, a child might use "cup" to refer only to her own sippy cup but not to other cups, or "car" only for the family vehicle but not for other automobiles.

Overextensions are far more commonly observed in expressive language (production) than in receptive language (comprehension). A child who calls all men "dada" may still correctly identify her own father when asked "Where's Dada?" This asymmetry suggests that overextensions often reflect a retrieval limitation — the child's productive vocabulary is too small to provide the correct label, so the nearest available word is recruited. Overextensions peak during the one-word (holophrastic) stage and decline sharply as vocabulary expands, particularly during the naming explosion around 18 months. Underextensions tend to appear earlier and resolve more quickly, making them less noticeable to caregivers.

Clinical Example: Overregularization as Evidence for Rule Learning

A 3-year-old says "I goed to the store" and "I saw two mouses." These overregularization errors are clinically significant because they demonstrate that the child is not merely imitating adult speech but is actively extracting and applying grammatical rules. The child previously said "went" correctly (through rote imitation) but now applies the past tense rule ("-ed") systematically. This U-shaped curve (correct, then incorrect, then correct again) provides strong evidence against purely imitation-based theories of language acquisition.

Theoretical Perspectives

Nativist Theory: Chomsky's Language Acquisition Device

Noam Chomsky (1965) argued that humans are biologically equipped with an innate Language Acquisition Device (LAD) -- a neurological mechanism that contains a Universal Grammar (UG), the set of grammatical principles common to all human languages. Key arguments for the nativist position include:

  • Poverty of the stimulus: The language input children receive is insufficient to explain the complexity and creativity of the grammar they acquire. Children produce sentences they have never heard.
  • Universal stages: Children across all cultures and languages follow a similar sequence of language milestones
  • Critical period: There appears to be a biologically determined window for optimal language acquisition
  • Overregularization errors: These demonstrate rule extraction, not imitation
  • Creolization: Children exposed to pidgin languages (simplified contact languages) spontaneously develop full grammatical systems (creoles), suggesting innate grammar-building capacity

Behaviorist Theory: Skinner's Verbal Behavior

B. F. Skinner (1957) proposed in Verbal Behavior that language is acquired through the same learning principles as any other behavior: operant conditioning (reinforcement and shaping) and imitation. Skinner classified verbal behavior into functional categories:

  • Mand: A verbal response controlled by a state of deprivation or aversive stimulation (essentially a request or demand, e.g., "water" when thirsty)
  • Tact: A verbal response controlled by a nonverbal stimulus (essentially labeling, e.g., saying "dog" when seeing a dog)
  • Echoic: Verbal behavior that repeats the verbal behavior of another person (imitation)
  • Intraverbal: Verbal behavior controlled by other verbal behavior (e.g., answering questions, conversation)

Chomsky's (1959) devastating review of Verbal Behavior argued that reinforcement and imitation cannot account for the creativity and rule-governed nature of language. This review is considered one of the pivotal moments in the cognitive revolution.

Interactionist and Social-Pragmatic Approaches

More contemporary theories emphasize the interaction between biological predispositions and social experience:

  • Bruner's Language Acquisition Support System (LASS): Jerome Bruner (1983) proposed that social interaction provides the crucial scaffold for language learning. The LASS complements Chomsky's LAD -- biology provides the capacity, but social interaction activates and shapes it.
  • Child-directed speech (CDS): Also called "motherese" or "parentese" -- the simplified, high-pitched, melodic speech that adults naturally use with infants. CDS features shorter sentences, exaggerated intonation, and repetition, facilitating language learning.
  • Tomasello's usage-based theory: Michael Tomasello (2003) argued that children learn language through general cognitive abilities (pattern recognition, intention-reading) applied to social interaction, rather than through a specialized language module.

Critical and Sensitive Periods for Language

Lenneberg (1967) proposed a critical period hypothesis for language acquisition, suggesting that the ability to acquire a first language natively is constrained to roughly the first 12 years of life, after which language acquisition becomes qualitatively different and more effortful.

Evidence supporting this hypothesis comes from several sources:

  • Cases of extreme isolation: Genie, discovered at age 13 after severe deprivation, acquired some vocabulary but never mastered syntax despite years of intervention (Curtiss, 1977)
  • Deaf children and sign language: Deaf individuals exposed to sign language from birth acquire it natively; those exposed later show decreasing grammatical proficiency as age of first exposure increases (Newport, 1990)
  • Second language acquisition: Immigrants who arrive before puberty typically achieve native-like proficiency; those arriving later rarely do, even after decades of exposure (Johnson & Newport, 1989)

Most contemporary researchers prefer the term sensitive period rather than "critical period," recognizing that language learning remains possible beyond this window but becomes progressively more difficult and less likely to reach native levels.

Clinical Implications

The sensitive period concept has profound implications for clinical practice. Early identification and intervention for hearing impairment, autism spectrum disorder, and specific language impairment are critical because the window for optimal language development is limited. Children with cochlear implants show the best language outcomes when implanted before age 2 (Tomblin et al., 2005). Similarly, early intensive behavioral intervention for children with ASD shows the strongest effects on language outcomes when initiated before age 3.

A common historical misconception held that early exposure to sign language would impede the development of oral communication skills in deaf and hard-of-hearing children. Contemporary research has largely overturned this belief. Studies demonstrate that acquiring sign language during infancy and early childhood actually facilitates spoken language development rather than hindering it. Children who gain a strong linguistic foundation through sign are better equipped to map spoken words onto concepts they already understand, accelerating vocabulary growth and grammatical comprehension once they receive auditory input through hearing aids or cochlear implants. Early sign exposure ensures that critical-period neural mechanisms for language are engaged on schedule, building the cognitive and linguistic scaffolding upon which spoken language can later be layered. This evidence supports a "bilingual-bimodal" approach in which sign and speech are treated as complementary rather than competing modalities, maximizing overall communicative competence during the years when the brain is most receptive to linguistic input.

Bilingualism

Having explored how critical and sensitive periods shape language acquisition — including the finding that early sign language exposure actually supports rather than hinders spoken language development — we arrive at a question with profound clinical and educational implications: What happens when children grow up acquiring two languages? For much of the twentieth century, parents and professionals alike worried that raising a child bilingually would "confuse" the developing brain, slow cognitive development, or leave children without full competence in either language. A pediatrician might have advised immigrant parents to speak only English at home. A school psychologist might have attributed a bilingual child's smaller English vocabulary to a language disorder rather than recognizing it as a normal feature of dual-language development. These concerns, though well-intentioned, reflected deep misunderstandings about how the brain handles multiple linguistic systems — misunderstandings that research has now decisively corrected.

Consider a four-year-old named Sofia, raised in a Spanish-speaking home and now attending an English-language preschool. Her English vocabulary is somewhat smaller than that of her monolingual English-speaking peers, and her teacher wonders whether she should be referred for a speech-language evaluation. At the same time, Sofia's Spanish vocabulary is slightly smaller than that of monolingual Spanish-speaking children her age. On the surface, this might look like a problem — but a closer look at the research reveals something important. When researchers measure Sofia's total conceptual vocabulary across both languages — counting every concept she can label in either Spanish or English — her knowledge is comparable to, and sometimes exceeds, that of monolingual peers (Pearson et al., 1993). The apparent "deficit" in each individual language is simply a reflection of the fact that Sofia's linguistic knowledge is distributed across two systems, not crammed into one. This is a critical distinction for clinicians and school psychologists conducting language assessments, where failing to consider total vocabulary across both languages can lead to misdiagnosis of language impairment.

Research on bilingual development has dispelled many such misconceptions, yielding a set of key findings that are essential for clinical practice and frequently tested on the EPPP:

  • No cognitive disadvantage: Contrary to earlier beliefs, bilingualism does not cause language confusion or cognitive delay. Bilingual children may have slightly smaller vocabularies in each individual language but their total conceptual vocabulary across languages is comparable to monolinguals (Pearson et al., 1993)
  • Cognitive advantages: Bilingual individuals often show enhanced executive function, particularly in tasks requiring inhibitory control and cognitive flexibility (Bialystok, 2001). This "bilingual advantage" may extend into older adulthood as a protective factor against cognitive decline
  • Code-switching: Alternating between languages within a conversation is a sign of linguistic competence, not confusion. It follows systematic grammatical rules
  • Sequential vs. simultaneous bilingualism: Children exposed to two languages from birth (simultaneous) typically achieve native-like proficiency in both; sequential bilinguals (who learn the second language after age 3) may show accent or grammatical differences in the later-acquired language

The cognitive advantages associated with bilingualism deserve special attention because they connect language development to broader neuropsychological functioning. Why would speaking two languages enhance executive function? The answer lies in what a bilingual brain must do every time its owner speaks: both languages are active simultaneously, meaning the brain must constantly monitor which language is appropriate for the current context, inhibit the non-target language, and flexibly switch between systems when the situation calls for it. This ongoing cognitive workout — performed thousands of times a day, year after year — appears to strengthen the same attentional control networks that underlie executive functioning more broadly. Bialystok's (2001) research demonstrated that bilingual children outperform monolinguals on tasks requiring them to ignore misleading information and shift between rule sets, and subsequent studies have suggested these advantages may persist into late adulthood, potentially delaying the onset of dementia symptoms by several years.

Code-switching — the practice of alternating between two languages within a single conversation or even within a single sentence — is another phenomenon that clinicians must understand correctly. When Sofia says to her grandmother, "Mira, abuela, I found a ladybug!" she is not demonstrating confusion or an inability to separate her languages. She is deploying a sophisticated linguistic strategy that follows precise grammatical rules about where switches can and cannot occur. Bilingual speakers code-switch to express nuance, convey cultural identity, fill lexical gaps, or calibrate their speech to a listener who shares their bilingual competence. Recognizing code-switching as a marker of linguistic skill rather than deficit is essential for any clinician working with bilingual populations.

The distinction between simultaneous and sequential bilingualism also carries clinical relevance, particularly when considered alongside the sensitive period research discussed earlier in this chapter. Children exposed to two languages from birth — simultaneous bilinguals — typically develop native-level proficiency in both languages, often achieving developmental milestones on roughly the same timetable as monolinguals, though with some normal variability. Sequential bilinguals, who begin acquiring a second language after approximately age three, may achieve high proficiency but are more likely to retain subtle accent differences or grammatical patterns influenced by their first language. This distinction matters when clinicians evaluate whether a bilingual client's language patterns reflect normal second-language acquisition effects or a genuine language disorder — a determination that requires understanding the client's language history, not just their current performance in one language.

In immigrant families, children and adolescents who achieve proficiency in the host country's language more rapidly than their parents often assume the role of language broker—translating and interpreting during interactions with schools, medical providers, landlords, government agencies, and other institutions. This phenomenon, known as language brokering, is widespread across immigrant communities worldwide and carries a complex mix of developmental consequences.

Research indicates that language brokering can yield several positive outcomes. Children who serve as translators for their families frequently develop advanced bilingual proficiency, heightened metalinguistic awareness, and stronger perspective-taking abilities. Many language brokers report feelings of pride, maturity, and a deepened sense of family responsibility. The experience can also strengthen bicultural identity and foster resilience.

At the same time, language brokering poses notable risks. Children placed in adult communicative roles may experience stress, anxiety, and a sense of burden—particularly when translating emotionally charged or high-stakes content such as medical diagnoses, legal proceedings, or financial difficulties. The reversal of typical parent-child authority dynamics can create role confusion, with children assuming decision-making responsibilities that exceed their developmental readiness. Some studies have found associations between frequent brokering and increased parent-child conflict, parentification, and academic interference when brokering duties compete with schoolwork.

The psychological impact depends on several moderating factors, including the child's age, the frequency and emotional intensity of brokering situations, cultural attitudes toward filial obligation, and whether the child perceives the role as voluntary or imposed. When families and institutions acknowledge the child's contribution while also providing appropriate adult support—such as professional interpreter services—the negative effects can be substantially mitigated.

Example

A 10-year-old girl accompanies her mother to a parent-teacher conference and translates the teacher's concerns about her own academic performance into Spanish. She must simultaneously process the evaluative content about herself, manage her mother's emotional reaction, and accurately convey nuanced educational terminology. This situation illustrates how brokering can create a psychologically complex experience that blends linguistic skill with emotional labor and potential role strain.

Beyond its grammatical sophistication, code-switching serves important sociolinguistic and identity functions. Bilingual and multilingual speakers frequently alternate between languages to signal group membership and establish solidarity with listeners who share their linguistic and cultural background. For example, a Spanish-English bilingual professional might conduct a business meeting entirely in English but seamlessly shift to Spanish when chatting with a coworker from the same heritage community, signaling shared identity and in-group affiliation. Code-switching can also be used to convey emotional nuance, mark quotations, emphasize particular points, or navigate topics that feel more natural in one language than another. Research demonstrates that these alternations are governed by pragmatic competence and social awareness, further underscoring that code-switching reflects advanced communicative skill rather than linguistic deficiency.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Know the five components of language: phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, pragmatics
  • Distinguish phonemes (smallest unit of sound) from morphemes (smallest unit of meaning)
  • Know the major language milestones and their approximate ages: cooing (2 months), babbling (6 months), first words (12 months), telegraphic speech (18-24 months), vocabulary spurt (18 months)
  • Overregularization errors demonstrate rule learning, not imitation -- a key argument against behaviorist theory
  • Chomsky's LAD and Universal Grammar: know these concepts and the poverty of the stimulus argument
  • Skinner's verbal behavior categories: mand, tact, echoic, intraverbal
  • Receptive language develops before expressive language
  • The critical/sensitive period hypothesis: Lenneberg proposed it; Genie and deaf signers provide supporting evidence
  • Bilingualism provides cognitive advantages and does not cause language confusion or delays
  • Child-directed speech (motherese/parentese) facilitates language acquisition
  • Perceptual narrowing: infants lose the ability to discriminate non-native phonemes around 8-10 months
