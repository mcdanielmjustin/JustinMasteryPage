Overview of Temperament and Personality Development

Temperament refers to constitutionally based individual differences in emotional reactivity and self-regulation that appear early in life and show moderate stability across development. Personality development, particularly as conceptualized by Erikson's psychosocial stages and Marcia's identity statuses, describes how the self evolves across the lifespan in response to both biological maturation and social experience.

For the EPPP, candidates must understand the major temperament classification systems (Thomas & Chess, Kagan), how temperament interacts with environment to shape developmental outcomes, and Erikson's eight psychosocial stages in detail. These topics appear frequently and have direct relevance to clinical assessment and intervention.

Thomas and Chess: The New York Longitudinal Study

Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess (1977) conducted the landmark New York Longitudinal Study (NYLS), following 133 children from infancy into adulthood. They identified nine dimensions of temperament and classified children into three broad temperament types, plus a mixed category.

While Thomas and Chess provided the foundational temperament typology, Mary Rothbart developed an influential alternative framework grounded in psychobiological research. Rothbart's model identifies three broad dimensions of temperament rather than discrete types. Surgency/Extraversion encompasses positive anticipation, high activity level, impulsivity, and sensation seeking. Negative Affectivity captures proneness to fear, frustration, sadness, and general distress, along with slower recovery from negative arousal. Effortful Control — often considered the most clinically significant dimension — reflects the capacity to voluntarily regulate attention, inhibit dominant responses, and activate subdominant responses. Effortful control develops substantially during the preschool years and has been linked to better emotion regulation, fewer externalizing behavior problems, higher academic achievement, and greater conscience development. Rothbart's framework is distinguished from earlier models by its emphasis on self-regulatory processes (effortful control) as a core temperamental dimension, not merely a byproduct of maturation. For the EPPP, it is essential to recognize that Rothbart's three dimensions differ from Thomas and Chess's nine-dimension, three-type classification and from Kagan's focus on behavioral inhibition as a single temperamental continuum.

Three Temperament Types

Temperament Classifications

Easy temperament (~40% of sample): Regular biological rhythms, positive approach to new situations, high adaptability, generally positive mood, mild to moderate intensity of reactions. These children are generally a pleasure to parent and adjust readily to new routines.

Difficult temperament (~10% of sample): Irregular biological rhythms, withdrawal from new situations, slow adaptability, predominantly negative mood, intense reactions. These children are at higher risk for behavioral problems, particularly when there is poor goodness of fit with caregivers.

Slow-to-warm-up temperament (~15% of sample): Initially withdrawn and negative in response to new stimuli, but gradually adapt with repeated exposure. Low activity level and mild reactions. May be mislabeled as "shy" but can adjust well given patience and gentle encouragement.

Mixed/Unclassifiable (~35%): Did not fit neatly into any category, showing combinations of traits from multiple types.

Goodness of Fit

Thomas and Chess's most important clinical contribution was the concept of goodness of fit -- the match between a child's temperament and the demands and expectations of the environment (particularly the parents). When there is a good fit, development proceeds smoothly; when there is a poor fit, the child is at increased risk for behavioral and emotional problems.

Clinical Example: Goodness of Fit

A mother describes her 3-year-old as "impossible" -- the child has irregular sleep patterns, intense emotional reactions, and withdraws from new situations. The mother, who values structure and punctuality, feels frustrated and incompetent. Assessment reveals the child has a difficult temperament, and the mother's rigid expectations create a poor goodness of fit. Intervention focuses not on changing the child's temperament but on helping the mother adjust her expectations and parenting strategies: establishing predictable routines (accommodating the child's slower adaptability), preparing the child for transitions, allowing extra time for new situations, and reframing the child's intensity as a sign of passion rather than defiance. This approach exemplifies how temperament-informed parenting can improve outcomes without requiring temperament change.

Kagan's Behavioral Inhibition

Jerome Kagan (1994) conducted extensive longitudinal research on behavioral inhibition -- a temperamental trait characterized by heightened vigilance and fearfulness in response to unfamiliar people, objects, and situations. Approximately 15-20% of infants show consistently high reactivity (vigorous limb movements, crying, arching of back) to novel stimuli at 4 months of age.

Biological Basis

Kagan proposed that behavioral inhibition has a biological basis in a low threshold for arousal in the amygdala and associated limbic circuits. Behaviorally inhibited children show:

  • Higher resting heart rates and less heart rate variability
  • Greater cortisol reactivity to stress
  • More right frontal EEG asymmetry (associated with withdrawal motivation)
  • Larger pupil dilation in response to novel stimuli

Developmental Trajectory

Approximately two-thirds of highly reactive 4-month-olds become behaviorally inhibited toddlers, and roughly one-third to one-half of behaviorally inhibited toddlers go on to develop social anxiety disorder in adolescence (Kagan & Snidman, 2004). However, the trajectory is not deterministic:

  • Sensitive, supportive parenting can reduce the behavioral expression of inhibition
  • Overprotective or anxious parenting can exacerbate it
  • The biological reactivity often persists even when behavioral inhibition decreases, suggesting that inhibited children learn to manage (not eliminate) their reactivity

Clinical Implications

Behavioral inhibition is one of the strongest temperamental predictors of anxiety disorders, particularly social anxiety disorder. Early identification of behaviorally inhibited children allows for preventive intervention. Research supports the use of graduated exposure to novel situations, parent training to avoid accommodation of avoidance, and cognitive-behavioral strategies. Importantly, parents should be counseled that the goal is not to eliminate the child's temperamental sensitivity but to help the child develop coping skills that prevent the temperament from developing into a clinical disorder.

Erikson's Psychosocial Stages

Erik Erikson (1902-1994) proposed eight stages of psychosocial development spanning the entire lifespan. Each stage presents a central psychosocial crisis -- a conflict between two opposing tendencies that must be resolved for healthy development. Successful resolution results in a basic virtue or strength; unsuccessful resolution results in maladaptive tendencies.

Stage Details for EPPP

Trust vs. Mistrust: Resolved through consistent, responsive caregiving. Corresponds closely to Bowlby's attachment theory. When caregivers are unreliable, the infant develops mistrust of the world.

Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt: Toddlers need to explore and assert independence. Overly controlling or punitive parenting leads to shame and self-doubt. Corresponds to Piaget's sensorimotor/early preoperational period and the development of self-will.

Initiative vs. Guilt: Preschoolers initiate activities and assert power through play. If punished for initiative or made to feel that their desires are always wrong, excessive guilt develops.

Industry vs. Inferiority: School-age children develop competence through academic and social achievements. Failure or negative comparison leads to feelings of inferiority.

Identity vs. Role Confusion: The central crisis of adolescence. Successful resolution results in a coherent sense of self; failure leads to identity confusion. This stage has been extensively elaborated by James Marcia.

Generativity vs. Stagnation: Middle-aged adults seek to contribute to the next generation through parenting, mentoring, or creative work. Failure leads to self-absorption and stagnation.

Erikson's fourth stage corresponds developmentally to Freud's latency stage, with both frameworks placing children between approximately six and eleven years of age during this period. In Freud's model, the latency period is characterized by a relative quieting of sexual and aggressive drives, allowing psychic energy to be redirected toward social and intellectual pursuits. Erikson's parallel conceptualization emphasizes the psychosocial dimension of this redirection: children channel their energy into mastering academic skills, developing competencies, and comparing their abilities to those of peers. The convergence of these two frameworks highlights that middle childhood is a critical period for developing a sense of capability—when children feel productive and skilled, they develop industry; when they repeatedly experience failure or unfavorable comparison, they risk internalizing a pervasive sense of inferiority. EPPP questions may test the ability to align Freud's psychosexual stages with Erikson's psychosocial stages at corresponding developmental periods.

Identity Development: Marcia's Identity Statuses

James Marcia (1966) operationalized Erikson's identity concept by proposing four identity statuses based on two dimensions: whether the individual has experienced an identity crisis (a period of active exploration of alternatives) and whether they have made a commitment (a firm decision about identity elements such as occupation, ideology, and relationships).

StatusCrisis (Exploration)CommitmentDescription
Identity AchievementYes (resolved)YesHas explored alternatives and made firm commitments. Associated with the best psychological outcomes: high self-esteem, mature reasoning, and strong relationships.
MoratoriumCurrently in crisisNo (not yet)Actively exploring identity alternatives but has not yet committed. Often characterized by anxiety and questioning, but this is a healthy and necessary process.
ForeclosureNoYesHas made commitments without exploration -- typically adopting parents' or authority figures' values without question. Appears stable but may be rigid and defensive when beliefs are challenged.
Identity DiffusionNoNoNo exploration and no commitment. May appear apathetic or aimless. Associated with the poorest outcomes when it persists. In early adolescence, this is normative; in late adolescence/adulthood, it is concerning.

Clinical Example: Assessing Identity Status

A college counseling center psychologist works with a 20-year-old pre-med student who reports feeling lost and questioning everything. She originally chose medicine because "my parents are both doctors and it's what I've always been expected to do," but she is now intensely interested in art and environmental science. She feels anxious, guilty, and uncertain. This student appears to be transitioning from foreclosure (committed without exploration, based on parental expectations) to moratorium (actively exploring alternatives, experiencing productive anxiety). The psychologist normalizes this process as healthy identity work rather than pathologizing the distress, while helping the student explore her values, interests, and the distinction between her own goals and her parents' expectations.

Clinical Implications

Identity development continues beyond adolescence, and many adults revisit identity questions during major life transitions (career change, divorce, retirement). Marcia's framework is useful in clinical assessment for understanding clients' developmental challenges. Moratorium, while anxious, is a necessary precursor to identity achievement. Foreclosed individuals may present for therapy when life events challenge their unexamined commitments, and identity-diffused adults may present with depression, purposelessness, or personality pathology. Erikson noted that societies need to provide a psychosocial moratorium -- a sanctioned period of exploration (e.g., college years) -- to facilitate healthy identity development.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

Parenting Style and Bullying Dynamics

Research on Baumrind's parenting styles has extended beyond academic and social competence outcomes to examine how different parenting approaches relate to children's involvement in bullying — both as perpetrators and as victims. Two parenting styles in particular have been linked to elevated risk for bullying involvement: authoritarian parenting and permissive parenting. While both styles increase the likelihood of a child being involved in bullying dynamics, they do so in distinct ways that reflect the underlying characteristics of each approach.

Authoritarian parenting — characterized by high demandingness, strict control, harsh discipline, and low warmth — is more strongly associated with children who engage in bullying behavior. Children raised in authoritarian households are exposed to coercive power dynamics and may learn that aggression and dominance are effective means of controlling others. The use of punitive discipline without explanation models the use of power over those who are weaker, and the lack of emotional warmth may impair empathy development.

Permissive parenting — characterized by high warmth but low demandingness, few behavioral expectations, and minimal monitoring — is more strongly associated with children becoming targets of bullying. Children raised with permissive parenting may lack the assertiveness skills, boundary-setting abilities, and conflict resolution strategies needed to resist peer aggression. The absence of structure and limit-setting at home may leave these children ill-equipped to navigate dominance hierarchies or stand up for themselves in peer contexts.

Parenting Style Key Features Bullying Association Proposed Mechanism
Authoritarian High control, harsh discipline, low warmth Stronger link to perpetrating bullying Models coercive power use; impairs empathy; child externalizes aggression with peers
Permissive Low control, few limits, high warmth Stronger link to victimization Fails to build assertiveness and self-advocacy; poor conflict resolution skills; limited monitoring
Authoritative Firm limits with warmth, reasoning, responsiveness Protective against both roles Builds social competence, empathy, assertiveness, and self-regulation

Clinical Note

When assessing children involved in bullying — whether as aggressors, victims, or both (bully-victims) — clinicians should evaluate parenting practices as part of the broader case conceptualization. Interventions that shift parenting toward an authoritative style — combining clear expectations and consistent limits with emotional warmth and open communication — have shown effectiveness in reducing both perpetration and victimization. This finding underscores the goodness-of-fit concept discussed earlier: the child's social outcomes are shaped not by temperament or parenting alone, but by the interaction between the child's characteristics and the caregiving environment.

Self-Recognition and the Emergence of Secondary Emotions

The development of secondary emotions (also called self-conscious emotions) depends on cognitive prerequisites that emerge during toddlerhood. Research by Michael Lewis and colleagues demonstrated that mirror self-recognition — the ability to recognize oneself in a mirror, typically assessed via the "rouge test" — serves as a necessary foundation for the earliest self-conscious emotions to appear. In the rouge test, a spot of rouge or a sticker is surreptitiously placed on the child's face; children who touch the mark on their own face (rather than on the mirror) demonstrate visual self-recognition, which typically emerges between 18 and 24 months of age.

Once children can recognize themselves as distinct entities — establishing a rudimentary sense of "me" — they become capable of experiencing emotions that involve self-evaluation or social comparison. The first secondary emotions to emerge are embarrassment and envy, both of which require an awareness of the self in relation to others. Embarrassment requires recognizing that one is the object of others' attention, while envy requires understanding that another person possesses something the self desires. These early self-conscious emotions appear shortly after mirror self-recognition is achieved.

Later-developing secondary emotions — including pride, shame, and guilt — require not only self-recognition but also an understanding of social standards and rules, which typically emerges closer to age 3. These are sometimes called the "self-conscious evaluative emotions" because they involve measuring one's behavior against internalized expectations.

Emotion Type Examples Cognitive Prerequisite Typical Onset
Primary (basic) emotions Joy, anger, sadness, fear, surprise, disgust None — biologically preprogrammed Birth to ~9 months
Early secondary emotions Embarrassment, envy Mirror self-recognition ~18–24 months
Self-conscious evaluative emotions Pride, shame, guilt Self-recognition + knowledge of standards/rules ~2.5–3 years

Clinical Note

The link between self-recognition and secondary emotions has implications for understanding developmental delays. Children with autism spectrum disorder often show delayed or atypical mirror self-recognition, which may correspond to differences in the timing and expression of self-conscious emotions. Similarly, Erikson's stage of Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt (ages 1–3) maps onto this developmental window — the capacity to experience shame requires the same emerging self-awareness that mirror recognition reflects.

Applying Kohlberg's Moral Development to Exam Scenarios

Kohlberg's theory of moral development, while covered in the Moral Development chapter, frequently intersects with personality and identity content on the EPPP. Kohlberg identified three levels of moral reasoning—preconventional, conventional, and postconventional—each containing two stages. Exam questions often present a character's response to the classic Heinz dilemma (in which a man must decide whether to steal an unaffordable drug to save his dying wife) and require candidates to classify the reasoning into the correct level and stage.

At the conventional level (Stages 3 and 4), moral judgments are guided by the desire to uphold social expectations, maintain relationships, and preserve social order. A Stage 3 response emphasizes being a "good" person, fulfilling role expectations, and gaining approval (e.g., "Heinz should steal the drug because a good husband takes care of his wife" or "He shouldn't steal because people will think he's a criminal"). A Stage 4 response focuses on obeying laws, respecting authority, and maintaining the functioning of society as a whole (e.g., "Stealing is against the law and if everyone broke laws when it suited them, society would fall apart").

Example: Identifying Conventional Reasoning

When asked about the Heinz dilemma, Morgan responds: "Heinz should steal the drug because it's a husband's duty to protect his wife, and anyone would understand why he did it." This response reflects Stage 3 (Good Boy/Good Girl orientation) reasoning within the conventional level, because Morgan's justification centers on interpersonal role expectations and social approval rather than self-interest (preconventional) or abstract ethical principles (postconventional). On the EPPP, look for references to duty, social roles, law and order, or concern about what others think as markers of conventional moral reasoning.

Parenting Styles and Developmental Outcomes

Diana Baumrind's (1966, 1991) research on parenting styles is closely linked to temperament and personality development. Her framework classifies parenting along two primary dimensions—demandingness (the degree to which parents set expectations and enforce rules) and responsiveness (the degree to which parents are warm, supportive, and attuned to children's needs). The intersection of these dimensions produces four parenting styles.

Authoritative Parenting

Authoritative parents are both highly demanding and highly responsive. They genuinely enjoy interacting with their children and maintain warm, engaged relationships. At the same time, they hold high expectations for behavior and achievement, establishing clear rules that are age-appropriate and enforced consistently. When discipline is required, authoritative parents explain the reasoning behind rules, encourage dialogue, and respect the child's perspective while maintaining firm boundaries. This style is associated with the most favorable developmental outcomes across domains, including higher self-esteem, greater social competence, better academic performance, and lower rates of behavioral problems.

Parenting Style Demandingness Responsiveness Key Characteristics
Authoritative High High Warm, firm, uses reasoning; developmentally appropriate rules consistently enforced
Authoritarian High Low Strict, punitive, demands obedience without explanation; "because I said so"
Permissive Low High Warm and accepting but sets few limits; avoids confrontation
Uninvolved (Neglectful) Low Low Disengaged, indifferent; minimal guidance or warmth; worst outcomes

Example

An authoritative parent whose 10-year-old wants to stay up past bedtime on a school night might respond: "I understand you want to finish your show, and I enjoy watching it with you too. But you need sleep for school tomorrow, so bedtime is 9:00. You can record the show and we'll watch the rest together this weekend." This response validates the child's desire, maintains the rule, offers a rationale, and proposes a compromise—all hallmarks of the authoritative approach.

Personality Trait Development Across Adulthood: The Big Five

While temperament describes early-appearing individual differences in reactivity and regulation, adult personality is most commonly assessed using the Big Five (Five-Factor Model) framework, which includes Openness to Experience, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism (often remembered by the acronym OCEAN). A substantial body of longitudinal research has examined how these traits change across the adult lifespan.

Cross-sectional and longitudinal studies consistently demonstrate that agreeableness and conscientiousness are the two Big Five dimensions that show the most robust increases throughout adulthood. Agreeableness—which encompasses warmth, cooperation, trust, and prosocial orientation—tends to rise steadily from young adulthood through older age. Conscientiousness—reflecting self-discipline, organization, dependability, and goal-directed behavior—also increases, particularly during the twenties and thirties as individuals take on greater occupational and familial responsibilities. These patterns have been replicated across cultures and are sometimes described as evidence of personality maturation, reflecting a general developmental trend toward greater social adaptation and self-regulation.

In contrast, neuroticism tends to decline modestly over the adult years, extraversion shows mixed patterns (with social dominance increasing but social vitality sometimes declining), and openness to experience remains relatively stable before showing modest decreases in later life. These normative shifts suggest that personality is not fixed after adolescence but continues to evolve in response to biological aging, social role changes, and accumulated life experience.

Clinical Note

The age-related increases in agreeableness and conscientiousness are relevant to clinical practice because they suggest that certain personality-related difficulties (e.g., impulsivity, interpersonal antagonism) may naturally attenuate with age. This is consistent with research showing that antisocial and borderline personality features tend to diminish across adulthood, a phenomenon partly attributable to these normative trait shifts.

Self-Recognition and the Emerging Sense of Self

The development of self-recognition is a foundational milestone in personality development, marking the emergence of a distinct sense of "me" as separate from others. The classic method for assessing this capacity is the rouge test (also called the mirror self-recognition task), originally developed by Lewis and Brooks-Gunn (1979). In this paradigm, a spot of rouge or a sticker is surreptitiously placed on the child's face (typically the nose or forehead), and the child is then placed in front of a mirror. If the child touches the mark on their own face rather than on the mirror image, this is taken as evidence that they recognize the reflection as themselves.

Most infants begin demonstrating mirror self-recognition by approximately 18 months of age, with the majority succeeding between 18 and 24 months. Before this age, infants typically react to their mirror image as though it were another child — smiling, vocalizing, or reaching toward the reflection. The emergence of self-recognition around 18 months corresponds temporally with several other developmental achievements, including the use of personal pronouns ("me," "mine"), self-conscious emotions such as embarrassment and pride, and early forms of empathy. These convergent developments suggest that a coherent self-concept begins to consolidate during the second year of life.

Clinical Note

Mirror self-recognition is considered a prerequisite for the development of self-conscious emotions (e.g., shame, guilt, embarrassment, pride), which require an awareness of the self as an object of others' evaluation. This has direct relevance to Erikson's second stage (Autonomy vs. Shame/Doubt), which occurs during the same developmental window. Children cannot experience shame without first having a sense of self to evaluate.

Baumrind's Parenting Styles

Diana Baumrind (1971) identified distinct parenting styles based on two core dimensions: demandingness (the degree to which parents set expectations, enforce rules, and monitor behavior) and responsiveness (the degree to which parents are warm, accepting, and attuned to children's emotional needs). The intersection of these dimensions produces four parenting styles, later elaborated by Maccoby and Martin (1983).

Authoritative Parenting

A style characterized by high levels of both demandingness and responsiveness. Authoritative parents establish clear expectations and consistent boundaries while simultaneously providing warmth, open communication, and respect for the child's perspective. They explain the reasoning behind rules, encourage age-appropriate autonomy, and use disciplinary strategies that are firm but not punitive.

Decades of research consistently identify authoritative parenting as producing the most favorable developmental outcomes across childhood and adolescence. Children raised by authoritative parents tend to exhibit higher self-esteem, greater social competence, stronger academic performance, fewer behavioral problems, and better emotional regulation compared to children raised under other styles. These positive outcomes have been replicated across multiple cultures, though the specific expression of authoritative practices may vary culturally.

Parenting Style Demandingness Responsiveness Typical Child Outcomes
Authoritative High High High self-esteem, social competence, academic achievement, self-regulation
Authoritarian High Low Obedience but lower self-esteem, more anxiety, poorer social skills
Permissive Low High Poor self-regulation, impulsivity, difficulty with authority
Uninvolved (Neglectful) Low Low Poorest outcomes overall: low competence, attachment difficulties, behavioral problems

Clinical Note

For EPPP purposes, distinguish carefully between authoritative and authoritarian. Authoritarian parents are high in demandingness but low in warmth — they expect obedience without explanation and rely on power-assertive discipline. Authoritative parents pair equally high expectations with emotional support, open dialogue, and democratic decision-making. The authoritative approach connects conceptually to Thomas and Chess's goodness of fit: these parents are adept at adjusting their demands to match the child's temperament while maintaining appropriate structure.

Carstensen's Socioemotional Selectivity Theory

Laura Carstensen's (1992) socioemotional selectivity theory (SST) offers a motivational account of how social goals and preferences shift across the lifespan. The theory posits that individuals' perception of time — specifically, how much future time they believe remains — fundamentally shapes the goals they prioritize. When time is perceived as expansive (as it typically is in youth), people tend to pursue knowledge-related goals: seeking novel information, meeting new people, and exploring unfamiliar experiences that may yield future benefits. When time is perceived as limited (as it typically is in later life), people shift toward emotionally meaningful goals: deepening existing close relationships, savoring positive experiences, and seeking emotional satisfaction in the present moment.

This shift in goal priority explains a well-documented pattern: older adults tend to have smaller but more emotionally satisfying social networks. Rather than reflecting social loss or cognitive decline, this pruning of peripheral relationships represents an adaptive, deliberate strategy to maximize emotional well-being. Older adults selectively invest in relationships that provide the greatest emotional reward — typically long-standing bonds with family members and close friends — while allowing more casual acquaintanceships to fade.

A key feature of SST is that the mechanism is not age per se but perceived time remaining. Research has demonstrated that younger adults facing terminal illness or other life-threatening circumstances show the same preference for emotionally close social partners as healthy older adults. Conversely, older adults who are told to imagine an extended future show goal preferences more typical of younger individuals.

Example

A 70-year-old retired professor declines invitations to large professional conferences where she might meet new colleagues, preferring instead to spend time with her two closest friends and her grandchildren. According to socioemotional selectivity theory, this reflects a rational motivational shift: with a contracted time horizon, she prioritizes emotionally rich interactions over information-gathering opportunities. Notably, if this same professor were told that a medical breakthrough would add 20 healthy years to her life, SST predicts she would become more interested in novel social opportunities again.

Feature Expansive Time Horizon (Younger Adults) Limited Time Horizon (Older Adults)
Primary goals Knowledge acquisition, future planning Emotional meaning, present satisfaction
Social network Larger, more diverse Smaller, more intimate
Social partner preference Novel, informative contacts Familiar, emotionally close contacts
Emotional experience More mixed emotions Positivity effect; better emotion regulation

Bem's Gender Schema Theory

Sandra Bem (1981) proposed gender schema theory, which integrates principles from both cognitive developmental approaches and social learning perspectives to account for how children acquire gender-typed knowledge and behavior. According to Bem, children develop cognitive frameworks — or schemas — for understanding what it means to be male or female within their culture. These schemas are built through observation, direct instruction, and cultural messaging about gender-appropriate behavior, interests, and attributes.

Once a gender schema is established, children actively use it to organize and interpret incoming information. New experiences are filtered through the lens of gender: children pay more attention to, and better remember, information consistent with their own gender schema while ignoring or distorting schema-inconsistent information. For example, a boy who has developed a schema associating tools with masculinity may recall seeing a man using a hammer but forget or misremember seeing a woman doing the same task.

Bem's theory differs from Kohlberg's cognitive developmental account in a critical way. Kohlberg argued that children must first achieve gender constancy (the understanding that gender is permanent and unchanging) before they begin selectively attending to same-gender models. Bem argued that children begin filtering the world through gender schemas much earlier — as soon as they can categorize themselves as male or female, typically around age 2-3. The theory also differs from pure social learning accounts by emphasizing the child's active cognitive processing rather than passive absorption of reinforced behaviors.

Gender Schema

A cognitive structure that organizes an individual's understanding of gender-related attributes, roles, and behaviors. Gender schemas guide attention, memory, and behavior by providing a framework for interpreting gender-relevant information from the social environment.

Clinical Note

Bem introduced the concept of androgyny, suggesting that individuals who incorporate both masculine and feminine traits into their self-concept — rather than rigidly adhering to one gender schema — tend to show greater psychological flexibility and adaptability. On the EPPP, remember that Bem's theory uniquely bridges the cognitive and social learning traditions: children are not merely shaped by reinforcement (social learning) nor must they wait for cognitive maturity (Kohlberg), but instead actively construct and apply gender schemas from an early age based on culturally available information.
