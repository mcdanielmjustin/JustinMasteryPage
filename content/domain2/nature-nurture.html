Nature vs. Nurture

Genetics, epigenetics, gene-environment interactions
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Overview of the Nature-Nurture Debate

The nature-nurture question is one of the oldest and most fundamental issues in psychology: To what extent are human characteristics determined by genetic inheritance (nature) versus environmental experience (nurture)? Contemporary developmental psychology has moved well beyond this dichotomy, recognizing that virtually all traits arise from complex interactions between genes and environments.

For the EPPP, candidates must understand behavioral genetics methodology, the concept of heritability, epigenetic mechanisms, and the specific types of gene-environment interactions and correlations that shape development.

Some genetic traits follow X-linked inheritance patterns because the relevant gene is located on the X chromosome. Because males carry only one X chromosome (XY), they will manifest any trait coded by an allele on that X — whether the allele is dominant or recessive — since there is no second X chromosome carrying a potentially counteracting allele. This is referred to as hemizygosity. Females, who carry two X chromosomes (XX), require two copies of a recessive X-linked allele (one inherited from each parent) in order to express the associated phenotype. If a female inherits only one copy of the recessive allele, she becomes a carrier — she does not display the trait herself but can transmit the allele to her offspring.

Example

Red-green color blindness is a classic X-linked recessive condition. A male who inherits the recessive allele from his mother will be color blind because his Y chromosome carries no corresponding gene to override it. A female must receive the recessive allele from both her mother and her father to be color blind — a far less probable event. This explains why approximately 8% of males but fewer than 1% of females exhibit red-green color deficiency. Other X-linked conditions relevant to psychology include Fragile X syndrome, which is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability and involves a trinucleotide repeat expansion on the X chromosome.

Understanding X-linked inheritance is clinically relevant because it accounts for the pronounced sex differences in prevalence rates observed for several neurodevelopmental and medical conditions. When constructing family pedigrees or evaluating genetic risk, clinicians should note that affected males typically inherit X-linked recessive conditions through carrier mothers, while affected females are comparatively rare and must have an affected father and a carrier (or affected) mother.

Canalization is a concept introduced by C.H. Waddington (1957) that describes how strongly the genotype constrains phenotypic expression to a narrow range of developmental outcomes, regardless of environmental variation. Highly canalized traits follow a predictable developmental trajectory that is difficult to deflect through environmental influence. For example, basic motor milestones such as sitting, crawling, and walking emerge in a relatively fixed sequence and timeline across vastly different rearing environments — these are strongly canalized. In contrast, traits with low canalization, such as personality characteristics or intellectual achievement, are far more susceptible to environmental modification because the genotype permits a wide range of phenotypic outcomes.

The concept is often visualized using Waddington's epigenetic landscape, in which a ball rolling down a terrain of valleys represents development. Deep, narrow valleys represent highly canalized traits where the ball's path is constrained; shallow, wide valleys represent traits with greater environmental sensitivity. An important corollary is that more extreme environmental forces are required to deflect a highly canalized trait from its typical path, whereas even modest environmental differences can shift the expression of weakly canalized traits.

Example

Babbling in infants is a highly canalized behavior — it appears at roughly the same developmental point even in children who are deaf from birth or raised in linguistically impoverished settings. By contrast, vocabulary size by age three is poorly canalized, varying enormously depending on the quantity and quality of language input a child receives. On the EPPP, understanding canalization helps distinguish traits that are robust against environmental perturbation from those that are highly modifiable.

Genotype, Phenotype, and Their Distinction

Understanding the nature-nurture framework requires grasping the fundamental distinction between genotype and phenotype. The genotype is an individual's complete set of genetic instructions — the DNA sequences inherited from both biological parents. The phenotype, by contrast, is the observable expression of those genetic instructions as they interact with environmental conditions throughout development. A phenotype encompasses physical characteristics (height, eye color), physiological processes, behavioral tendencies, and psychological traits. Crucially, the phenotype is never a direct readout of the genotype alone. Even highly heritable traits are shaped by nutritional status, disease exposure, psychosocial stress, and countless other environmental variables. This means that individuals carrying genes associated with a particular characteristic may not manifest that characteristic if environmental conditions work against its expression — or may express it to a degree that differs from what their genetic endowment alone would predict.

Example

Consider a 22-year-old man whose biological parents are both considerably taller than average. Based on his genotype, one might predict above-average height. However, his actual measured height — his phenotype — turns out to be shorter than average. This discrepancy can be explained by environmental factors that influenced growth, such as chronic malnutrition during childhood, prolonged illness, or exposure to growth-inhibiting substances. His phenotype reflects the combined product of his genetic blueprint and the environmental context in which development occurred, illustrating why genotype alone is an incomplete predictor of any observable trait.

Key Definitions

Genotype: The genetic makeup of an organism; the full set of inherited alleles.
Phenotype: The observable characteristics of an organism resulting from the interaction of its genotype with environmental influences throughout development.

Sensitive and Critical Periods in Development

A central concept at the intersection of nature and nurture is that of developmental timing — the idea that organisms are differentially receptive to environmental input at different points in their maturation. A sensitive period is a developmental window during which particular skills or capacities are acquired with greatest ease. The neural and biological systems underlying these abilities are maximally plastic during this timeframe, making learning especially efficient. However, unlike a critical period, a sensitive period does not represent an absolute deadline. If the relevant environmental input is absent or delayed, acquisition can still occur after the window has closed, though it typically requires greater effort and may not reach the same level of proficiency. Most domains of human development — including language acquisition, social bonding, and sensory-motor coordination — are governed by sensitive rather than strict critical periods. For instance, children exposed to language input during the first several years of life acquire grammar and phonology with remarkable ease, while individuals who encounter their first language much later in life can still learn to communicate but often show persistent deficits in certain grammatical structures. This concept underscores that genetic maturational timetables set the stage for when the organism is most ready to benefit from experience, while environmental availability of appropriate stimulation determines whether that potential is realized.

Sensitive Period vs. Critical Period

A sensitive period is a time of heightened receptivity during which learning occurs most readily, but acquisition remains possible beyond this window at reduced efficiency. A critical period is a more rigid developmental window after which certain capacities cannot be acquired at all if the necessary input was absent. In practice, most human psychological abilities follow a sensitive-period model rather than a strict critical-period model.

Example

Children born with cataracts that are removed early in infancy develop normal vision, while those whose cataracts are not corrected until later childhood show lasting visual deficits despite eventually receiving proper sensory input. This demonstrates a sensitive period for visual cortex development: the neural circuitry is most receptive to patterned visual input during the first months of life, yet some degree of recovery remains possible — distinguishing it from a rigid critical period with no capacity for later change.

Behavioral Genetics

Behavioral genetics is the field that examines the relative contributions of genetic and environmental factors to individual differences in behavior and psychological traits. It relies on several key research designs to disentangle genetic from environmental influences.

Twin Studies

Twin studies compare monozygotic (MZ) twins (who share 100% of their DNA) with dizygotic (DZ) twins (who share approximately 50% of their segregating genes, like any siblings). The logic is straightforward: if MZ twins are more similar on a trait than DZ twins, genetic factors are implicated.

Key Terminology

Concordance rate: The probability that both twins exhibit a given trait when one twin has it. Higher concordance in MZ vs. DZ twins suggests genetic influence.

Equal environments assumption: The critical assumption in twin studies that MZ and DZ twins share environmental influences to the same degree. Violations of this assumption can inflate heritability estimates.

The landmark Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (Bouchard et al., 1990) found that MZ twins raised in different families showed remarkably similar IQ scores (correlation ~.70), personality traits, and even behavioral quirks, providing compelling evidence for strong genetic influences on many psychological characteristics.

Twin research has also yielded striking findings regarding personality. Across multiple studies, monozygotic twins raised in separate households consistently demonstrate greater similarity on personality measures than dizygotic twins raised together in the same family. For example, data from the Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart revealed MZ-apart personality correlations in the range of .45–.50, whereas DZ twins sharing the same home typically correlated around .20–.25 on the same dimensions. This pattern carries a profound implication: genetic similarity matters more for personality resemblance than growing up under the same roof. It also underscores the relatively limited role of shared environment in shaping personality traits, since DZ twins who experience the same household, schools, and neighborhood are still less alike than MZ twins who never lived together. These findings helped establish the now widely accepted estimate that roughly 40–60% of personality variance is attributable to genetic factors, with most of the remaining variance due to nonshared environmental influences rather than the family environment siblings have in common.

Bouchard and McGue (1981) conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis summarizing median IQ correlations across different degrees of genetic and environmental relatedness. Their findings revealed a clear gradient: monozygotic twins reared together showed the highest median correlation (~.86), followed by MZ twins reared apart (~.72), dizygotic twins reared together (~.60), biological siblings raised in the same home (~.47), and biological parent-child pairs living together (~.42). Notably, the lowest correlation among biologically related pairs was found for biological parents and their children living apart (~.22), reflecting the absence of shared environmental influence. In contrast, adoptive parent-child pairs and unrelated siblings raised together showed modest correlations (~.19-.32), which were attributable entirely to shared environment. This pattern — where correlations increase systematically with greater genetic overlap and decrease when shared environment is removed — provides some of the strongest evidence that both heredity and environment contribute to cognitive ability, with genetic factors carrying substantially more weight.

To fully appreciate twin study methodology, it helps to understand the broader framework of genetic relatedness. Full siblings and dizygotic (fraternal) twins are both first-degree relatives who share approximately 50% of their segregating genes. Other first-degree relatives — biological parents and their children — also share roughly 50%. Half-siblings, who have one biological parent in common, share approximately 25% of their genetic material, placing them at the same level of relatedness as aunts/uncles with nieces/nephews or grandparents with grandchildren. These known degrees of genetic overlap allow researchers to generate precise predictions about trait similarity across different family relationships. If a trait is entirely genetic, the correlation between relatives should directly mirror their degree of genetic sharing. Deviations from these expected patterns reveal the contribution of shared and nonshared environmental factors.

Adoption Studies

Adoption studies compare adopted children with both their biological parents (who share genes but not environment) and their adoptive parents (who share environment but not genes). If adopted children more closely resemble their biological parents on a trait, genetic factors are implicated; if they more resemble their adoptive parents, environmental factors are suggested.

The Colorado Adoption Project (Plomin et al., 1997) followed adopted and non-adopted children longitudinally, revealing that the correlation between adopted children and their biological parents on cognitive measures actually increases with age, while correlations with adoptive parents decrease -- a finding consistent with increasing genetic influence over time.

Clinical Example: Adoption Study Interpretation

A researcher examines schizophrenia rates among adults who were adopted at birth. The biological mothers of the adopted-away children had schizophrenia. Results show that these adoptees have elevated schizophrenia rates (~10-15%) compared to adoptees whose biological parents had no psychiatric history (~1-2%), despite being raised by non-schizophrenic adoptive parents. This classic design (Heston, 1966; Kety et al., 1968) demonstrates genetic vulnerability while also highlighting that the majority of at-risk adoptees do not develop the disorder, pointing to the importance of environmental factors.

A particularly informative adoption study by Capron and Duyme (1989) employed a cross-fostering design that allowed simultaneous examination of both genetic and environmental contributions to intelligence. By identifying children born to either high- or low-SES biological parents who were subsequently adopted into either high- or low-SES families, the researchers could independently assess each factor's influence. The results revealed that the socioeconomic status of the adoptive family exerted a significant and consistent effect on children's IQ scores: children placed with high-SES adoptive parents scored approximately 12 points higher than those placed with low-SES adoptive parents, regardless of their biological parents' socioeconomic background. Biological parent SES also contributed to IQ differences, confirming genetic influence. This study is especially valuable because it demonstrates that environmental enrichment can produce meaningful cognitive gains even among children with varying genetic backgrounds—a finding that powerfully illustrates why high heritability does not preclude substantial environmental effects on a trait.

Heritability

Heritability (h-squared) is a statistic that estimates the proportion of variance in a trait within a population that is attributable to genetic differences among individuals. It ranges from 0.0 (no genetic contribution to individual differences) to 1.0 (all variation due to genetic differences).

Critical Misunderstandings About Heritability

Heritability applies to populations, not individuals. A heritability of .50 for intelligence does NOT mean that 50% of an individual's intelligence is genetic.

Heritability does not mean immutability. A highly heritable trait can still be strongly influenced by environmental intervention (e.g., PKU is nearly 100% genetic but intellectual disability is prevented by dietary modification).

Heritability is context-dependent. It changes across populations, ages, and environments. When environments are more uniform, heritability estimates are higher because less environmental variation exists.

Heritability cannot explain group differences. Even if a trait is highly heritable within groups, differences between groups may be entirely environmental in origin (Lewontin, 1970).

Heritability estimates for major psychological traits include approximately .50 for general intelligence (with estimates increasing from childhood to adulthood), .40-.60 for personality traits (Big Five), and .60-.80 for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.

Shared vs. Nonshared Environment

Behavioral genetics partitions environmental influence into two components:

  • Shared environment (c-squared): Environmental factors that make siblings more similar to each other (e.g., family SES, parenting style, neighborhood). Surprisingly, shared environment accounts for relatively little variance in most traits by adulthood.
  • Nonshared environment (e-squared): Environmental factors that make siblings different from each other (e.g., differential parental treatment, unique peer groups, random events, measurement error). This component is typically the largest environmental contributor to individual differences.

Plomin and Daniels (1987) published a highly influential paper asking "Why are children in the same family so different from one another?" Their conclusion -- that nonshared environmental influences dominate over shared ones for most psychological traits -- reshaped how psychologists think about family influence.

Twin studies have provided critical insights into how the relative importance of shared and nonshared environmental factors shifts across the lifespan. For intelligence specifically, shared environmental influence is substantial during childhood but begins to decline markedly during adolescence. By adulthood, shared environment accounts for a negligible proportion of variance in cognitive ability. In contrast, nonshared environmental influence remains relatively consistent throughout the lifespan, maintaining a steady contribution to individual differences in intelligence from childhood through adulthood. This developmental pattern helps explain a seemingly paradoxical finding: as children grow older and gain more autonomy, the family environment they share with siblings becomes less predictive of their cognitive outcomes, while the unique experiences, relationships, and random events specific to each individual continue to differentiate them. This pattern is consistent with the increasing role of active gene-environment correlation (niche-picking) during adolescence and adulthood, as individuals progressively shape their own cognitive environments in ways that align with their genetic predispositions.

Epigenetics

Epigenetics refers to changes in gene expression that do not involve alterations to the DNA sequence itself. These modifications determine whether specific genes are "turned on" or "turned off" and can be influenced by environmental factors including stress, nutrition, toxins, and early caregiving experiences.

Key Terminology

DNA methylation: The addition of methyl groups to DNA, typically silencing gene expression. Increased methylation of a gene promoter region generally reduces that gene's activity.

Histone modification: Chemical changes to the histone proteins around which DNA is wound, affecting how tightly DNA is packaged and thus how accessible genes are for transcription.

Transgenerational epigenetic inheritance: The transmission of epigenetic marks from one generation to the next without changes in DNA sequence.

A groundbreaking study by Meaney and colleagues (Weaver et al., 2004) demonstrated that rat pups who received high levels of maternal licking and grooming showed altered methylation patterns at the glucocorticoid receptor gene, resulting in lower stress reactivity throughout life. This effect was reversible through cross-fostering, demonstrating a direct mechanism by which early caregiving shapes gene expression and stress physiology.

Clinical Implications

Epigenetics provides a biological mechanism for understanding how early adversity (e.g., childhood abuse, neglect) can have lasting effects on stress reactivity, mental health, and even physical health. Studies of human suicide victims with histories of childhood abuse have found increased methylation of the glucocorticoid receptor gene in the hippocampus (McGowan et al., 2009), paralleling the animal findings. This research supports the importance of early intervention and suggests that environmental enrichment may help reverse adverse epigenetic modifications.

One of the most clinically significant examples of environmentally induced epigenetic change involves prenatal alcohol exposure. When a developing fetus is exposed to alcohol, it can produce widespread alterations in DNA methylation patterns and histone modifications across multiple gene systems. These epigenetic disruptions affect genes involved in neural development, growth regulation, and cellular differentiation, contributing to the spectrum of physical, cognitive, and behavioral abnormalities known as fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD). Research has demonstrated that alcohol interferes with normal methylation processes during critical periods of embryonic development, leading to aberrant gene expression patterns that persist long after the prenatal exposure has ended. This epigenetic framework helps explain why FASD produces such variable outcomes—the timing, duration, and quantity of alcohol exposure interact with the fetus's developing epigenome to produce differing constellations of deficits, from the full syndrome of fetal alcohol syndrome (facial dysmorphology, growth restriction, CNS impairment) to subtler neurobehavioral effects.

Gene-Environment Interactions and Correlations

While behavioral genetics focuses on how genes and environments jointly influence development, Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory provides a complementary framework for understanding the multiple layers of environmental context that shape a child's development. Bronfenbrenner proposed that children develop within a nested set of environmental systems, each exerting influence at different levels of proximity to the child.

Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Systems

The microsystem includes settings in which the child directly participates (family, school, peer group). The mesosystem consists of interactions between microsystems (e.g., parent-teacher communication). The exosystem encompasses settings that affect the child indirectly—the child does not participate in these contexts, but events within them influence the child's immediate environment. The macrosystem refers to the broader cultural, economic, and political context. The chronosystem captures changes over time in both the individual and the environment.

The exosystem is particularly relevant to understanding how distal environmental forces shape developmental outcomes through indirect pathways. A parent's workplace is a classic exosystem example: the child never enters this setting, yet job-related stress, work schedules, and income directly affect parenting quality and family functioning. Similarly, the extended family network—grandparents, aunts, uncles—may provide resources or create stressors that filter through the parents to affect the child. Mass media also operates at the exosystem level, shaping societal attitudes, parental beliefs, and community norms that indirectly influence the child's developmental context.

Example

A factory closure in a small town (an exosystem event) leads to parental unemployment, increased family conflict, and reduced access to extracurricular activities for children. The children never set foot in the factory, yet the economic disruption cascades through the family system to affect their emotional well-being and academic performance. This illustrates how exosystem forces interact with genetic vulnerabilities—a child with a genetic predisposition toward anxiety may be especially affected by the family instability triggered by such distal events, exemplifying gene-environment interaction across ecological levels.

Understanding gene-environment transactions also requires attention to the broader ecological context in which development unfolds. Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (2004) provides a framework for conceptualizing multiple layers of environmental influence on the developing child. The theory identifies five nested systems:

The exosystem is particularly relevant to understanding how structural and institutional factors shape developmental outcomes even without the child's direct involvement. Available legal protections, social welfare programs, and community mental health resources all operate at this level. For instance, a parent's access to workplace leave policies or the availability of domestic violence services in a community can profoundly affect the child's home environment and caregiving quality, even though the child never directly contacts these systems.

Clinical Note

When conceptualizing a child's presenting problems, clinicians should assess exosystem-level factors that may be contributing to family stress. A child exhibiting behavioral difficulties may be reacting to a parent's job loss or the family's inability to access adequate social services — circumstances the child does not directly experience but that alter the quality of the caregiving environment. Bronfenbrenner's model reminds practitioners that effective intervention often requires addressing systemic factors beyond the therapy room.

Bronfenbrenner's Bioecological Model

While behavioral genetics focuses on parsing genetic and environmental variance, Urie Bronfenbrenner's bioecological theory provides a complementary framework for understanding how environments themselves are structured. Bronfenbrenner proposed that development occurs within a series of nested environmental systems, each exerting influence on the developing person. The microsystem encompasses the immediate settings in which the child directly participates — family, school, peer group, and neighborhood. The mesosystem refers to the connections and interactions that occur between microsystem elements. For example, the relationship between a child's parents and teachers constitutes a mesosystem link: when parents are actively engaged with the school, the child benefits from the coherence between these two microsystems. Beyond these, the exosystem includes settings that affect the child indirectly (e.g., a parent's workplace policies), the macrosystem encompasses broader cultural values, laws, and economic conditions, and the chronosystem captures the dimension of time, including historical events and life transitions. This model is particularly relevant to gene-environment research because it highlights that "environment" is not a monolithic construct but a multi-layered system of influences. Gene-environment interactions and correlations operate differently across these levels — a child's genetically influenced temperament may shape microsystem dynamics through evocative rGE, while macrosystem factors like poverty or cultural norms moderate how genetic predispositions are expressed.

Example: The Mesosystem in Action

Consider a child with a genetic predisposition toward ADHD symptoms. At the microsystem level, both the home and school are direct contexts of influence. The mesosystem — the quality of communication between parents and teachers — determines whether behavioral strategies are consistent across settings. Strong mesosystem connections (e.g., regular parent-teacher conferences, shared behavioral plans) can buffer the expression of genetic vulnerability, while weak connections may exacerbate difficulties. This illustrates how Bronfenbrenner's framework enriches our understanding of gene-environment dynamics.

Gene-Environment Interaction (GxE)

Gene-environment interaction occurs when the effect of a gene depends on the environment (or vice versa). The same genetic variant may lead to different outcomes depending on environmental conditions.

The classic example is Caspi et al. (2003), who found that individuals with the short allele of the 5-HTTLPR serotonin transporter gene were more likely to develop depression following stressful life events than those with the long allele. The gene alone did not cause depression, nor did stress alone -- it was the interaction that mattered. (Note: This finding has been debated in subsequent replication attempts, highlighting the complexity of GxE research.)

Gene-Environment Correlations (rGE)

Scarr and McCartney (1983) described three types of gene-environment correlation, which explain how genes and environments become linked:

TypeDescriptionExampleAge Trend
Passive rGEParents provide both genes and environment; the child passively receives bothMusically gifted parents pass on musical genes AND provide instruments and lessonsStrongest in early childhood
Evocative rGEThe child's genetically influenced traits elicit particular responses from the environmentA temperamentally easy baby elicits more positive parenting than a difficult babyStable across development
Active rGEThe child actively selects and creates environments compatible with genetic predispositions (also called "niche-picking")An intellectually curious child seeks out libraries, stimulating peers, and academic challengesIncreases with age as autonomy grows

Clinical Example: Diathesis-Stress Model

The diathesis-stress model is a specific form of gene-environment interaction in which a genetic vulnerability (diathesis) is expressed as a disorder only when triggered by environmental stress. For example, an individual may carry genetic risk for anxiety disorders but only develop symptoms following significant life stressors. The more recently proposed differential susceptibility hypothesis (Belsky & Pluess, 2009) extends this concept, suggesting that the same genetic variants that confer vulnerability to adversity also confer enhanced sensitivity to positive environments -- making some individuals "orchids" (highly sensitive to environment, for better or worse) and others "dandelions" (resilient regardless of environment).

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Heritability is a population statistic, not an individual measure -- it cannot explain group differences and does not imply immutability
  • Know the three types of gene-environment correlation (passive, evocative, active) and their developmental trajectories (passive decreases, active increases with age)
  • Distinguish between gene-environment interaction (GxE) and gene-environment correlation (rGE)
  • Understand the logic of twin studies (MZ vs. DZ comparisons) and adoption studies (biological vs. adoptive parent comparisons)
  • Know that nonshared environment is typically more important than shared environment for most psychological traits (Plomin & Daniels, 1987)
  • Understand the diathesis-stress model and the differential susceptibility hypothesis as models of gene-environment interaction
  • Be familiar with epigenetics as a mechanism by which environment alters gene expression without changing DNA sequence
  • Heritability of IQ increases with age (from ~.40 in childhood to ~.60-.80 in adulthood) -- a counterintuitive finding
  • The equal environments assumption is a critical methodological issue in twin studies
