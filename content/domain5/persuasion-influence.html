Introduction: Social Influence

A therapist carefully explains to her client why exposure therapy is the gold-standard treatment for specific phobia. The client nods along, seems persuaded — and then cancels the next three appointments. A public health campaign floods television with graphic images of lung disease, yet smoking rates barely budge. Meanwhile, a friend casually mentions she started meditating, and within a week, you have downloaded an app. Why do some attempts to change minds succeed brilliantly while others fail despite seemingly perfect logic? Social influence — the myriad ways in which people change each other's attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors — lies at the heart of social psychology and provides the answers. This chapter covers the major mechanisms of persuasion, from the dual-process pathways of the Elaboration Likelihood Model to Cialdini's six principles of influence. These are among the most heavily tested topics on the EPPP.

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)

Imagine you're watching two people decide whether to support a new public health policy. One person carefully reads the research studies cited in the proposal, weighs the statistical evidence, and considers counterarguments before forming an opinion. The other glances at the proposal, notices it's endorsed by a physician she admires, and decides she's in favor. Both individuals end up supporting the same policy — but through profoundly different psychological pathways. Understanding these two pathways is essential not only for the EPPP but for any clinician who wants to understand how attitudes form, shift, and endure over time.

Building on the social influence foundations introduced earlier, we turn now to one of the most influential frameworks in persuasion research: the Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), developed by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo. The ELM answers a deceptively simple question — when do people think carefully about persuasive messages, and what happens when they don't? The answer has sweeping implications, from psychotherapy (how do we help clients genuinely internalize new beliefs?) to public health campaigns (how do we craft messages that actually stick?).

The model's central insight is that persuasion is not a single process. Instead, the likelihood that a person will elaborate — that is, engage in effortful cognitive processing of a message's arguments — determines which of two distinct routes to attitude change will dominate. When elaboration is high, the central route prevails: people scrutinize argument quality, evaluate evidence, and generate their own counterarguments or supportive thoughts. When elaboration is low — because the person lacks the motivation, the ability, or both — the peripheral route takes over, and people rely instead on mental shortcuts and surface-level cues to decide what they believe.

Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986)

The ELM proposes two distinct routes to persuasion:
Central route: The audience carefully evaluates the quality and logic of the arguments. Requires high motivation and ability to process the message. Produces durable, resistant attitude change.
Peripheral route: The audience relies on superficial cues (source attractiveness, number of arguments, emotional appeals) rather than argument quality. Used when motivation or ability to process is low. Produces temporary, less resistant attitude change.

What determines whether a person takes the central or peripheral route? Two key factors: motivation and ability. Motivation is high when the topic is personally relevant — a patient recently diagnosed with diabetes, for example, is far more motivated to carefully process information about treatment options than someone with no personal connection to the disease. Ability depends on factors like cognitive capacity, background knowledge, and the absence of distractions. A brilliant argument presented to an exhausted, multitasking audience may fail to engage central processing simply because the listeners cannot concentrate. Both motivation and ability must be present for the central route to dominate; if either is missing, the peripheral route fills the gap.

Clinically, this distinction matters enormously. Consider a therapist using psychoeducation to help a client with panic disorder understand the cognitive model of anxiety. If the client is highly motivated (terrified by the attacks, eager for relief) and able to process the information (not currently in acute distress, possessing adequate cognitive functioning), central-route processing can produce deep, lasting attitude change — the kind that supports long-term behavioral shifts. But if the same psychoeducation is delivered when the client is overwhelmed or distracted, only peripheral cues may register: the therapist's warmth, the reassuring office environment, the confident tone of voice. The resulting attitude change — "I guess I feel a little better" — is real but fragile, unlikely to survive the next wave of panic.

Notice the pattern in the table above: the central route wins on every dimension that matters for lasting change — durability, resistance to counter-persuasion, and strength of behavioral impact. This is why effective persuasion campaigns (and effective psychotherapy) generally aim to engage central processing whenever possible. However, the peripheral route is not without value. In situations where people simply cannot or will not engage deeply — think of a brief public service announcement seen during a commercial break — peripheral cues like an expert spokesperson or an emotionally compelling image may be the only available lever for influence.

One subtle but important point for the EPPP: the two routes are not an either/or switch but rather endpoints on a continuum. Most real-world persuasion involves some mix of both processes. A person might be moderately motivated and attend partially to argument quality while also being influenced by the speaker's credentials. The model's power lies in predicting which route will dominate under specific conditions — and therefore what type of attitude change to expect.

Example

A consumer deeply researches car safety ratings, fuel efficiency data, and engineering reviews before purchasing (central route). Another consumer buys the same car because a celebrity endorses it in a commercial (peripheral route). The first consumer's attitude toward the car will be more persistent and predictive of future behavior.

As we move to Hovland's Communication Model in the next section, keep the ELM framework in mind. Hovland's model identifies the key components of any persuasive communication — source, message, channel, and audience — while the ELM explains how those components exert their effects depending on the depth of processing involved. Together, the two models offer a comprehensive toolkit for understanding when and why persuasion succeeds or fails.

Petty, R. E., & Cacioppo, J. T. (1986). The elaboration likelihood model of persuasion. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 19, 123–205.

Hovland's Communication Model

We've just explored how the Elaboration Likelihood Model distinguishes between two routes to persuasion — central and peripheral — based on the audience's motivation and ability to process a message. But before Petty and Cacioppo ever proposed their dual-route framework, a foundational question had already been under rigorous investigation: What makes any persuasive message work in the first place? What are the basic ingredients of successful persuasion? The answer emerged from one of the most ambitious research programs in the history of social psychology.

During the 1950s, Carl Hovland and his colleagues at the Yale Communication Research Program set out to systematically dismantle the persuasion process into its component parts. Their approach was deceptively simple but profoundly influential: they asked, "Who says what to whom, and with what effect?" That single question gave rise to a framework organized around four key variables — the source of the message, the message itself, the audience receiving it, and the channel through which it travels. Think of it like a recipe: change any one ingredient, and the persuasive outcome changes too. A compelling argument delivered by an untrustworthy spokesperson falls flat. A brilliant message aimed at the wrong audience bounces off. Hovland's model gave researchers — and, eventually, clinicians, advertisers, and public health professionals — a structured way to think about why some persuasive attempts succeed brilliantly while others fail miserably.

Source Variables

Imagine you're sitting in a waiting room and overhear two people offering advice about managing chronic pain. One is an orthopedic surgeon who has treated thousands of patients; the other is a stranger who "read something online." Even if they deliver the exact same recommendation, you'd likely be far more persuaded by the surgeon. That intuition captures the essence of source variables — the characteristics of the person delivering the message that determine whether it lands or bounces off. Hovland's team identified several source characteristics that powerfully shape persuasion, with credibility and attractiveness standing out as the most influential.

The credibility of the communicator interacts with how discrepant a message is from the audience's existing attitude, and this interaction reveals one of the most nuanced findings in persuasion research. When a highly credible source delivers a message, greater discrepancy between the message and the audience's current position produces greater attitude change in a roughly linear fashion — the further the message is from the audience's view, the more they shift. However, when the source is low in credibility, the relationship is curvilinear: moderate discrepancy produces the most change, while highly discrepant messages are rejected as implausible or biased. This occurs because audiences grant credible communicators a wider "latitude of acceptance" — they are willing to consider positions far from their own when the source is perceived as expert and trustworthy. A low-credibility source, by contrast, triggers skepticism when advocating an extreme position, leading to a boomerang effect or outright dismissal. This distinction is crucial for anyone designing interventions: the more extreme the change you're asking for, the more important it becomes that the messenger is seen as credible.

Example

A renowned cardiologist (high credibility) recommending a dramatic dietary overhaul is likely to produce substantial attitude change — the more sweeping the recommendation, the greater the shift. A health blogger with no medical credentials (low credibility) making the same extreme recommendation would likely be dismissed, whereas a moderate suggestion from that blogger might still be somewhat persuasive.

Beyond credibility, Hovland's team identified additional source characteristics that consistently influence persuasion outcomes. These factors don't operate in isolation — they interact with the type of message being delivered and the processing route the audience uses:

  • Credibility: Expert, trustworthy sources are more persuasive
  • Attractiveness: Physically attractive and likable sources are more persuasive (especially via the peripheral route)
  • Sleeper effect: Over time, the persuasive impact of a low-credibility source may increase as the message is dissociated from its source

The sleeper effect is particularly counterintuitive and worth pausing over. Normally, we expect persuasion to decay over time — a message is most powerful immediately after delivery, then fades. But with low-credibility sources, the opposite can happen. Initially, the audience dismisses the message because of who delivered it. But as weeks pass, something curious occurs: the content of the message lingers in memory while the association with the discredited source fades. The result is a delayed increase in persuasion — the "sleeper" waking up after an incubation period. This has real implications for clinicians and educators: a client who dismisses your recommendation today because of perceived role differences may quietly come around to that same idea weeks later, having forgotten the source but retained the substance.

Message Variables

If source variables answer the question "Who is most persuasive?", message variables tackle the equally critical question: "How should the argument be structured?" Hovland's team discovered that the way a message is framed, sequenced, and emotionally charged dramatically affects its persuasive power — sometimes in ways that seem paradoxical. Consider three of the most important message variables:

  • Fear appeals: Moderate fear is most effective; too little does not motivate, too much causes defensive avoidance. Most effective when paired with clear, actionable recommendations
  • One-sided vs. two-sided arguments: Two-sided messages (acknowledging opposing views) are more effective with educated or initially skeptical audiences
  • Primacy vs. recency: When two opposing messages are presented back-to-back, the first message has more impact (primacy). When there is a delay between messages, the second has more impact (recency)

A comprehensive meta-analysis by Tannenbaum and colleagues provided further clarity on when fear-based messages succeed or fail. The analysis demonstrated that fear arousal is maximally effective when the message not only highlights threatening consequences but also clearly specifies the behavioral steps the audience can take to avert those consequences. In other words, simply frightening people is insufficient and may even backfire through defensive avoidance; what makes fear persuasive is coupling the threat with a concrete, achievable action plan. Picture a public service announcement showing graphic images of lung cancer without ever mentioning smoking cessation hotlines, nicotine patches, or support groups — that's a recipe for denial, not change. This finding aligns with and extends the broader principle that moderate fear paired with high efficacy information produces the greatest attitude and behavior change.

Clinical Note

In health psychology interventions, clinicians designing fear-based campaigns (e.g., smoking cessation, safe sex promotion) should ensure that every threatening message is accompanied by specific, actionable guidance. Telling a patient "smoking will kill you" without providing a quit plan, referral to a cessation program, or coping strategies for cravings is unlikely to produce lasting change and may instead trigger denial or avoidance.

The conditions governing primacy and recency effects depend critically on the timing of message presentation and attitude measurement. This is one of those findings that seems complex at first but becomes intuitive once you picture a concrete scenario. Specifically, the primacy effect — where the first message exerts greater persuasive influence — is strongest when opposing arguments are presented in immediate succession with no delay between them, but a significant time interval separates the second message from the point at which attitudes are assessed. Under these conditions, the first message establishes an interpretive framework that colors how the second message is received, and the delay before measurement allows the second message to decay more from memory. Think of a courtroom: if the prosecution and defense deliver closing arguments back-to-back and the jury deliberates days later, the prosecution's argument (heard first) may dominate because it set the interpretive frame. Conversely, recency effects emerge when there is a temporal gap between the two messages but attitudes are measured immediately after the second presentation, giving the more recent argument the advantage of being fresh in memory — like hearing a compelling rebuttal right before you must cast your vote.

Audience Variables

Even the most credible source delivering a perfectly crafted message can fail if the audience isn't primed to receive it. Hovland's model recognized that characteristics of the receiver — their personality, cognitive style, developmental stage, and self-concept — powerfully moderate persuasion outcomes. Who is most and least susceptible to persuasive appeals, and why? The answers are more nuanced than you might expect.

Individual difference variables such as self-esteem and intelligence also predict how susceptible a person is to persuasive appeals. Research indicates that individuals with moderate self-esteem are most easily persuaded — a finding that initially surprises many students but makes elegant sense upon closer examination. Those with high self-esteem possess the confidence to resist external pressure; they essentially think, "I've already figured this out, and I trust my own judgment." Those with very low self-esteem, on the other hand, may be too withdrawn, inattentive, or preoccupied with self-doubt to adequately receive and process persuasive messages — they're so consumed by their own anxiety that the message never fully registers. Moderate self-esteem strikes a balance: these individuals attend to the message but lack the assertive resistance of high self-esteem individuals. They're listening and they're open. Regarding cognitive ability, people with lower intelligence tend to be more susceptible to persuasion because they are less skilled at generating counterarguments and critically analyzing message content. Higher intelligence facilitates scrutiny of weak arguments and detection of logical flaws, providing a natural defense against persuasion attempts. For the EPPP, remember the key takeaway: self-esteem shows a curvilinear relationship with persuasion (moderate = most susceptible), while intelligence shows a more linear one (lower = more susceptible).

Research on age and persuasion susceptibility reveals a curvilinear pattern across the lifespan that mirrors the self-esteem finding in its inverted-U logic. Young adults and older adults tend to be more susceptible to persuasive appeals, while middle-aged adults show the greatest resistance to attitude change. Several factors account for this pattern. Middle-aged individuals typically possess more crystallized attitudes formed through decades of experience, stronger commitments to existing beliefs, and greater cognitive resources for counterarguing — they've "been around the block" enough to know what they think and why. Young adults, still forming many of their core attitudes, are more open to influence — consider how readily a college student's political views may shift after a single compelling lecture, compared to a fifty-year-old whose positions have been reinforced over decades. Older adults may show increased susceptibility due to declining cognitive resources that support critical evaluation of persuasive messages, particularly the executive functions needed to detect manipulation and generate counterarguments. This finding has practical implications for public health campaigns, advertising, and political messaging, where different age cohorts may require different persuasive strategies.

Clinical Note

When designing psychoeducational interventions, clinicians should recognize that younger and older clients may be more receptive to direct persuasive approaches, whereas middle-aged clients may benefit from strategies that engage their existing knowledge and allow them to arrive at conclusions through their own reasoning — aligning with central route processing.

Two additional audience-level variables round out the picture and connect nicely back to the ELM framework discussed earlier. These variables help explain not just whether someone will be persuaded, but which type of persuasive strategy will be most effective for them:

  • Need for cognition: People high in need for cognition are more persuaded by strong arguments (central route)
  • Self-monitoring: High self-monitors are more responsive to image-based appeals; low self-monitors to quality-based appeals

The self-monitoring distinction is particularly useful in clinical contexts. A high self-monitor — someone keenly attuned to how others perceive them — might be persuaded to adopt healthier behaviors by framing those behaviors as socially desirable ("People will notice how much more energetic you seem"). A low self-monitor, by contrast, cares more about intrinsic quality and consistency with their values, responding better to substance-based arguments ("This approach is backed by the strongest evidence"). Knowing which type of client you're working with helps you tailor your persuasive approach for maximum effectiveness.

Sleeper Effect

A delayed increase in the persuasive impact of a message from a non-credible source. Initially, the message is discounted because of its source — the audience thinks, "Consider the source." Over time, however, the message content is remembered but the source is forgotten, allowing the message to exert delayed influence. This effect explains why propaganda and misinformation can become more persuasive over time rather than less, making it a concept with significant real-world implications for media literacy and clinical psychoeducation.

Cialdini's Six Principles of Influence

Robert Cialdini (1984) identified six principles that underlie most influence and compliance strategies. These are essential EPPP material:

  1. Reciprocity: People feel obligated to return favors. "I did something for you, now you owe me."
  2. Commitment and Consistency: Once people commit to a position, they feel pressure to behave consistently with it. This underlies the foot-in-the-door technique.
  3. Social Proof: People look to others to determine correct behavior, especially in ambiguous situations. "Everyone else is doing it."
  4. Authority: People tend to obey legitimate authority figures. This relates directly to Milgram's research.
  5. Liking: We are more easily persuaded by people we like. Factors: physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, familiarity, association.
  6. Scarcity: People value things more when they are rare or dwindling. "Limited time offer" and "Only 3 left!" exploit this principle.

Cialdini, R. B. (2001). Influence: Science and practice (4th ed.). Allyn & Bacon.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • ELM (Petty & Cacioppo): Central route = deep processing, durable change; peripheral route = superficial cues, temporary change. Both motivation and ability must be high for central processing.
  • Hovland's model: "Who says what to whom." Know source variables (credibility, attractiveness), message variables (fear appeals, one-sided vs. two-sided), and audience variables (need for cognition, self-monitoring).
  • Sleeper effect: Low-credibility source gains persuasive impact over time as the message-source link decays.
  • Fear appeals: Most effective at moderate levels when paired with clear, actionable recommendations. High fear without efficacy information triggers defensive avoidance.
  • Self-esteem and persuasion: Curvilinear relationship — moderate self-esteem = most susceptible. High self-esteem resists; very low self-esteem fails to attend.
  • Age and persuasion: Young and older adults more susceptible; middle-aged adults most resistant due to crystallized attitudes and stronger counterarguing skills.
  • Cialdini's six principles: Reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Be able to match compliance techniques to principles.
  • Attitude inoculation (McGuire): Exposing people to weakened counterarguments + refutations builds resistance to future persuasion. Refutational defense is more effective than supportive defense.
