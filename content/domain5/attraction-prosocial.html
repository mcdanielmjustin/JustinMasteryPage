Interpersonal Attraction

On March 13, 1964, a young woman named Kitty Genovese was attacked outside her apartment building in Queens, New York. According to initial reports, 38 neighbors heard her screams but none called the police — a claim that, while later shown to be exaggerated, launched an entire research program into why people fail to help others in distress. That same decade, psychologists were also asking the mirror-image question: Why do we connect with some people in the first place? What invisible forces pull us toward certain individuals and not others? The answers to both questions — why we attract and why we help (or fail to) — form the core of this chapter.

Determinants of Attraction

  • Proximity (propinquity): Physical closeness is one of the strongest predictors of attraction. We tend to like people we encounter frequently. The mere exposure effect (Zajonc) contributes: repeated contact increases familiarity and liking.
  • Similarity: We are attracted to people who share our attitudes, values, interests, and backgrounds. The matching hypothesis states that people tend to form relationships with others of similar physical attractiveness.
  • Reciprocity: We like people who like us. Learning that someone is attracted to us increases our attraction to them.
  • Physical attractiveness: Attractive individuals are rated more positively on many dimensions (the halo effect). There is some cross-cultural consistency in what is considered attractive (e.g., facial symmetry, youthfulness cues).
Matching Hypothesis

People tend to form long-term partnerships with others who are similar in physical attractiveness. While people may initially prefer the most attractive partner, they ultimately choose partners whose attractiveness level matches their own, due to the balance of fear of rejection and desire for the best available partner.

Misattribution of Arousal (Dutton & Aron, 1974)

In the famous "bridge study," male participants who crossed a fear-arousing suspension bridge were more likely to call an attractive female experimenter afterward than those who crossed a low, stable bridge. Physiological arousal from fear was misattributed to attraction, demonstrating the two-factor theory of emotion (Schachter & Singer): we experience arousal and then label it based on context.

Dutton, D. G., & Aron, A. P. (1974). Some evidence for heightened sexual attraction under conditions of high anxiety. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 30(4), 510–517.

Theories of Love

Understanding what draws people together is only the first step. The deeper question — one that preoccupies poets, philosophers, and couples therapists alike — is what transforms initial attraction into lasting love, and why love takes such different forms across relationships and lifetimes. Two frameworks dominate the EPPP's coverage of this topic: Sternberg's triangular theory and the attachment-style model.

Sternberg's Triangular Theory of Love (1986)

Robert Sternberg proposed that love is best understood as a triangle whose three vertices represent distinct psychological components. The presence or absence of each component defines qualitatively different experiences of love — seven types in all, plus the absence of all three (non-love). Critically, Sternberg viewed these components as dynamic: a relationship that begins as infatuation (passion only) may evolve into romantic love (passion plus intimacy) and eventually into consummate love (all three) — or may stall, regress, or transform in other ways.

Sternberg's Three Components

Intimacy: Feelings of closeness, connectedness, and bondedness — the warm, sharing dimension of love that develops through self-disclosure and mutual understanding.
Passion: Physical attraction, sexual desire, and arousal — the "heat" of love, driven by motivational and physiological forces.
Commitment: The decision to love someone and maintain that love over time — the cognitive, deliberate dimension that sustains a relationship through periods when intimacy wanes or passion cools.

Evolutionary Approaches to Attraction

Evolutionary psychology offers a complementary lens on attraction, grounded in the logic of natural selection. Parental investment theory (Trivers, 1972) proposes that the sex making the greater investment in offspring (typically females in mammals, due to pregnancy, lactation, and childcare) will be more selective in choosing mates, while the sex with lower investment (typically males) will compete more intensely for access to mates. This framework predicts that women, on average, will prioritize cues to resource acquisition and commitment (status, ambition, reliability), while men will prioritize cues to fertility and reproductive value (youth, physical attractiveness).

David Buss's (1989) cross-cultural survey of over 10,000 individuals across 37 cultures found broad support for these predictions, though the magnitude of sex differences varied by culture and was far smaller than popular accounts suggest. Critically, both sexes rated kindness and intelligence as the most desirable traits — evolutionary predictions about sex differences operate on top of a large shared foundation of mate preferences. For the EPPP, know that evolutionary approaches complement but do not replace social-psychological explanations of attraction.

Adult Attachment Styles

Building on Bowlby and Ainsworth's attachment theory, Hazan and Shaver (1987) proposed that adult romantic relationships follow attachment patterns similar to infant-caregiver bonds. Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) expanded this into a four-category model:

Clinical Application

Attachment style significantly impacts the therapeutic relationship. Preoccupied clients may become overly dependent on the therapist, while dismissing clients may devalue the relationship. Fearful-avoidant clients may alternate between seeking and rejecting help. Understanding attachment styles helps therapists anticipate transference patterns and tailor interventions accordingly.

Prosocial Behavior & Altruism

Having explored why people are drawn to one another, we now turn to one of the most fundamental questions in social psychology: Why do people help? The answer is less straightforward than it might seem, because the motivations underlying prosocial behavior range from genuine selfless concern to calculated self-interest — and the situational factors that determine whether help is given can override even the strongest compassionate impulses.

Prosocial behavior encompasses any action intended to benefit another person. Altruism is prosocial behavior motivated purely by concern for the other, with no expectation of personal reward. Whether true altruism actually exists — or whether all helping ultimately serves the helper's interests — has been one of social psychology's most enduring debates.

The Empathy-Altruism Hypothesis vs. Negative-State Relief

Empathy-Altruism Hypothesis (Batson, 1991)

When we feel empathic concern for a person in need, we are motivated to help for purely altruistic reasons — to reduce their suffering, not our own discomfort. If we feel personal distress instead of empathy, we help only if it is the easiest way to reduce our own negative feelings (egoistic motivation). The critical distinction is between other-focused empathy ("I feel for you") and self-focused distress ("Your suffering makes me uncomfortable").

Batson's rival, the negative-state relief model (Cialdini et al., 1987), offers an egoistic counter-explanation. It proposes that witnessing someone's suffering puts us in a negative mood, and we help primarily to improve our own emotional state — helping is essentially a mood-management strategy. The key prediction that distinguishes the two models: if an alternative mood boost is available (e.g., receiving unexpected good news), the negative-state relief model predicts helping will decrease (because the mood has already been repaired), while the empathy-altruism hypothesis predicts that people experiencing genuine empathic concern will still help, because their motivation is directed at the other person's welfare, not their own mood.

Example: Testing the Two Models

In one of Batson's experiments, participants who felt empathy for a woman in need helped her even when they could easily leave the situation (which would have ended their own distress). Participants who felt personal distress, however, chose to leave rather than help when escape was easy. This pattern — empathic participants help regardless of escape ease, while distressed participants help only when they cannot escape — supports the empathy-altruism hypothesis and is a frequently tested EPPP distinction.

The Bystander Effect

Bystander Effect (Darley & Latané, 1968)

The bystander effect refers to the robust finding that the probability of any single individual helping decreases as the number of bystanders increases. Darley and Latané developed this research program in direct response to the Kitty Genovese case and identified two key mechanisms:
Diffusion of responsibility: Each individual feels less personally responsible when others are present. The thought "Someone else will help" distributes the burden across all witnesses.
Pluralistic ignorance: Each bystander looks to others for cues about whether the situation is truly an emergency; when everyone appears calm (because everyone is looking at everyone else), each concludes the situation is not serious.

Latané & Darley's Five Steps to Helping

  1. Notice the event (distraction can prevent this)
  2. Interpret as emergency (pluralistic ignorance can block this)
  3. Assume personal responsibility (diffusion of responsibility can block this)
  4. Know how to help (lack of competence can block this)
  5. Decide to help (audience inhibition and cost-benefit analysis can block this)
Example

A person collapses in a crowded subway station. Each bystander sees others not reacting and concludes the situation is not serious (pluralistic ignorance). Each assumes someone else will call 911 (diffusion of responsibility). The same person collapsing in front of a single bystander is much more likely to receive immediate help.

Clinical Application: The Bystander Effect in Institutional Settings

The bystander effect operates in clinical institutions, not just on streets. When multiple staff members witness a patient in distress, diffusion of responsibility can delay intervention — each assumes another colleague will respond. Hospitals and crisis centers now use explicit "designated responder" protocols to counteract this effect, assigning specific individuals to act rather than relying on collective willingness. For therapists, understanding the bystander effect also helps in working with clients who feel guilt about failing to intervene during a witnessed assault or accident: the failure to help reflects a powerful situational force, not a character defect.

Social Exchange & Equity Theory

Social exchange theory views relationships as economic transactions: people seek to maximize rewards and minimize costs. Equity theory adds that satisfaction requires perceived fairness — each partner's ratio of inputs to outcomes should be roughly equal. Both over-benefiting and under-benefiting cause distress.

Clinical Application

Couples therapy often involves restoring equity and reciprocity. Behavioral couples therapy uses behavioral exchange techniques where partners agree to increase rewarding behaviors. Understanding social exchange frameworks helps clinicians identify reward-cost imbalances driving relational distress.

Batson, C. D. (1991). The altruism question: Toward a social-psychological answer. Erlbaum.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Proximity is the strongest predictor of initial attraction; similarity sustains relationships over time.
  • The matching hypothesis: people pair with partners of similar attractiveness.
  • Misattribution of arousal (Dutton & Aron): physiological arousal from any source can be misinterpreted as attraction.
  • Sternberg's triangular theory: Know the three components and the seven types of love, especially consummate love (all three).
  • Know the four adult attachment styles and their corresponding self/other models.
  • Empathy-altruism hypothesis (Batson): Empathic concern leads to true altruism; personal distress leads to egoistic helping.
  • Bystander effect: More bystanders = less helping. Know diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance.
  • Latané & Darley's five steps to helping — each step represents a potential barrier.
  • Social exchange theory: Relationships as reward/cost equations. Equity theory: Satisfaction requires perceived fairness.
