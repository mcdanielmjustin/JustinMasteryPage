How We're Moved — Persuasion, Conformity & Resistance

ELM, Cialdini's principles, Asch, Milgram, compliance techniques
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Introduction: Social Influence

Social influence — the myriad ways in which people change each other's attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors — lies at the heart of social psychology. This chapter covers three major categories: persuasion (deliberate attitude change), conformity (adjusting to group norms), and obedience (complying with authority). These are among the most heavily tested topics on the EPPP.

The Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM)

Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986)

The ELM proposes two distinct routes to persuasion:
Central route: The audience carefully evaluates the quality and logic of the arguments. Requires high motivation and ability to process the message. Produces durable, resistant attitude change.
Peripheral route: The audience relies on superficial cues (source attractiveness, number of arguments, emotional appeals) rather than argument quality. Used when motivation or ability to process is low. Produces temporary, less resistant attitude change.

Processing depthDeep, systematicShallow, heuristic

Example

A consumer deeply researches car safety ratings, fuel efficiency data, and engineering reviews before purchasing (central route). Another consumer buys the same car because a celebrity endorses it in a commercial (peripheral route). The first consumer's attitude toward the car will be more persistent and predictive of future behavior.

Petty, R. E., & Cacioppo, J. T. (1986). The elaboration likelihood model of persuasion. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 19, 123–205.

Hovland's Communication Model

Carl Hovland and the Yale Communication Research Program (1950s) identified four key variables that affect persuasion effectiveness:

Source Variables

The credibility of the communicator interacts with how discrepant a message is from the audience's existing attitude. When a highly credible source delivers a message, greater discrepancy between the message and the audience's current position produces greater attitude change in a roughly linear fashion — the further the message is from the audience's view, the more they shift. However, when the source is low in credibility, the relationship is curvilinear: moderate discrepancy produces the most change, while highly discrepant messages are rejected as implausible or biased. This occurs because audiences grant credible communicators a wider "latitude of acceptance" — they are willing to consider positions far from their own when the source is perceived as expert and trustworthy. A low-credibility source, by contrast, triggers skepticism when advocating an extreme position, leading to a boomerang effect or outright dismissal.

Example

A renowned cardiologist (high credibility) recommending a dramatic dietary overhaul is likely to produce substantial attitude change — the more sweeping the recommendation, the greater the shift. A health blogger with no medical credentials (low credibility) making the same extreme recommendation would likely be dismissed, whereas a moderate suggestion from that blogger might still be somewhat persuasive.

  • Credibility: Expert, trustworthy sources are more persuasive
  • Attractiveness: Physically attractive and likable sources are more persuasive (especially via the peripheral route)
  • Sleeper effect: Over time, the persuasive impact of a low-credibility source may increase as the message is dissociated from its source

Message Variables

  • Fear appeals: Moderate fear is most effective; too little does not motivate, too much causes defensive avoidance. Most effective when paired with clear, actionable recommendations
  • One-sided vs. two-sided arguments: Two-sided messages (acknowledging opposing views) are more effective with educated or initially skeptical audiences
  • Primacy vs. recency: When two opposing messages are presented back-to-back, the first message has more impact (primacy). When there is a delay between messages, the second has more impact (recency)

A comprehensive meta-analysis by Tannenbaum and colleagues provided further clarity on when fear-based messages succeed or fail. The analysis demonstrated that fear arousal is maximally effective when the message not only highlights threatening consequences but also clearly specifies the behavioral steps the audience can take to avert those consequences. In other words, simply frightening people is insufficient and may even backfire through defensive avoidance; what makes fear persuasive is coupling the threat with a concrete, achievable action plan. This finding aligns with and extends the broader principle that moderate fear paired with high efficacy information produces the greatest attitude and behavior change.

Clinical Note

In health psychology interventions, clinicians designing fear-based campaigns (e.g., smoking cessation, safe sex promotion) should ensure that every threatening message is accompanied by specific, actionable guidance. Telling a patient "smoking will kill you" without providing a quit plan, referral to a cessation program, or coping strategies for cravings is unlikely to produce lasting change and may instead trigger denial or avoidance.

The conditions governing primacy and recency effects depend critically on the timing of message presentation and attitude measurement. Specifically, the primacy effect — where the first message exerts greater persuasive influence — is strongest when opposing arguments are presented in immediate succession with no delay between them, but a significant time interval separates the second message from the point at which attitudes are assessed. Under these conditions, the first message establishes an interpretive framework that colors how the second message is received, and the delay before measurement allows the second message to decay more from memory. Conversely, recency effects emerge when there is a temporal gap between the two messages but attitudes are measured immediately after the second presentation, giving the more recent argument the advantage of being fresh in memory.

Audience Variables

Individual difference variables such as self-esteem and intelligence also predict how susceptible a person is to persuasive appeals. Research indicates that individuals with moderate self-esteem are most easily persuaded. Those with high self-esteem possess the confidence to resist external pressure, while those with very low self-esteem may be too withdrawn, inattentive, or preoccupied with self-doubt to adequately receive and process persuasive messages. Moderate self-esteem strikes a balance: these individuals attend to the message but lack the assertive resistance of high self-esteem individuals. Regarding cognitive ability, people with lower intelligence tend to be more susceptible to persuasion because they are less skilled at generating counterarguments and critically analyzing message content. Higher intelligence facilitates scrutiny of weak arguments and detection of logical flaws, providing a natural defense against persuasion attempts.

Research on age and persuasion susceptibility reveals a curvilinear pattern across the lifespan. Young adults and older adults tend to be more susceptible to persuasive appeals, while middle-aged adults show the greatest resistance to attitude change. Several factors account for this pattern. Middle-aged individuals typically possess more crystallized attitudes formed through decades of experience, stronger commitments to existing beliefs, and greater cognitive resources for counterarguing. Young adults, still forming many of their core attitudes, are more open to influence, while older adults may show increased susceptibility due to declining cognitive resources that support critical evaluation of persuasive messages. This finding has practical implications for public health campaigns, advertising, and political messaging, where different age cohorts may require different persuasive strategies.

Clinical Note

When designing psychoeducational interventions, clinicians should recognize that younger and older clients may be more receptive to direct persuasive approaches, whereas middle-aged clients may benefit from strategies that engage their existing knowledge and allow them to arrive at conclusions through their own reasoning — aligning with central route processing.

  • Need for cognition: People high in need for cognition are more persuaded by strong arguments (central route)
  • Self-monitoring: High self-monitors are more responsive to image-based appeals; low self-monitors to quality-based appeals
Sleeper Effect

A delayed increase in the persuasive impact of a message from a non-credible source. Initially, the message is discounted because of its source. Over time, the message content is remembered but the source is forgotten, allowing the message to exert delayed influence.

Cialdini's Six Principles of Influence

Robert Cialdini (1984) identified six principles that underlie most influence and compliance strategies. These are essential EPPP material:

  1. Reciprocity: People feel obligated to return favors. "I did something for you, now you owe me."
  2. Commitment and Consistency: Once people commit to a position, they feel pressure to behave consistently with it. This underlies the foot-in-the-door technique.
  3. Social Proof: People look to others to determine correct behavior, especially in ambiguous situations. "Everyone else is doing it."
  4. Authority: People tend to obey legitimate authority figures. This relates directly to Milgram's research.
  5. Liking: We are more easily persuaded by people we like. Factors: physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, familiarity, association.
  6. Scarcity: People value things more when they are rare or dwindling. "Limited time offer" and "Only 3 left!" exploit this principle.

Cialdini, R. B. (2001). Influence: Science and practice (4th ed.). Allyn & Bacon.

Conformity

Asch's Line Studies (1951)

Solomon Asch demonstrated that people will conform to a clearly incorrect majority. Participants judged which comparison line matched a standard line — an objectively easy task. When confederates unanimously gave the wrong answer, approximately 75% of participants conformed at least once, and the overall conformity rate across trials was about 37%.

Two Types of Social Influence

Normative influence: Conforming to be liked and accepted by the group (going along to get along). Driven by the desire for social approval. Produces public compliance without private acceptance.
Informational influence: Conforming because we believe the group has better information than we do. Driven by the desire to be correct. Produces private acceptance (true attitude change).

Key findings from Asch's research on factors that affect conformity:

  • Conformity increases with group size — but only up to about 3–4 members; beyond that, additional members add little
  • Conformity drops sharply when there is even one dissenter (ally), even if the dissenter gives a different wrong answer
  • Conformity increases when the task is more difficult or ambiguous
  • Conformity increases when responses are public (vs. private)
  • Conformity varies by culture — higher in collectivist cultures

Obedience: Milgram's Studies (1963)

Milgram's Obedience Paradigm

Stanley Milgram studied obedience to authority by instructing participants ("teachers") to administer increasingly severe electric shocks to a confederate ("learner") for wrong answers. In the baseline condition, 65% of participants administered the maximum 450-volt shock, despite hearing screams and pleas to stop. The shocks were not real, but participants believed they were.

Factors That Reduced Obedience in Milgram's Variations

Obedience decreased when: the learner was in the same room (proximity), another "teacher" refused to continue (social support for defiance), the experimenter gave orders by telephone (reduced authority presence), or the study was conducted in a less prestigious setting. Obedience increased when the participant only had an indirect role (e.g., reading the word pairs while another person delivered the shock).

Clinical & Ethical Note

Milgram's study is a landmark in both social psychology and research ethics. Participants experienced significant distress, which contributed to the development of stronger ethical guidelines, including the requirement for informed consent and debriefing. The APA's current Ethics Code (Standard 8.07) requires that deception be justified and that participants be debriefed as early as feasible.

Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 67(4), 371–378.

Reactance Theory

A central contribution of McGuire's inoculation theory is its demonstration that a refutational defense is the most useful strategy for building resistance to persuasive attempts. In this approach, individuals are first presented with counterarguments that challenge their existing attitude and then given clear refutations that dismantle those challenges. This process serves two functions: it signals that one's beliefs may come under attack (creating a motivational readiness to defend) and it equips the person with practiced counterarguing responses. Compared to approaches that simply pile on additional supportive evidence for a held belief, the refutational method produces substantially greater resistance because it directly engages with the form of threat the person will later face. The practical implication is that the best way to protect attitudes is not to shield people from opposing viewpoints but to expose them to weakened versions of those viewpoints in a controlled manner.

Clinical Note

Inoculation principles have been applied in psychoeducation, relapse prevention, and health promotion. For instance, a therapist working with a client in recovery might role-play scenarios in which the client encounters social pressure to use substances, presenting mild challenges and helping the client rehearse effective refusal responses. This "cognitive rehearsal" functions as an inoculation—building the client's resistance to real-world persuasive pressure before it occurs.

Attitude Inoculation (McGuire, 1964)

Drawing an analogy to biological immunization, William McGuire proposed that people can be made resistant to persuasion by pre-exposing them to weakened forms of counterarguments along with refutations of those arguments. Just as a vaccine introduces a mild version of a pathogen to build immune defenses, attitude inoculation exposes individuals to a diluted attack on their existing beliefs and then provides them with the tools to counter it. This two-step process—threat plus refutation—prepares the person to defend against stronger persuasive attempts encountered later.

McGuire's research focused primarily on widely accepted beliefs (called cultural truisms) that people hold strongly but have rarely been called upon to defend—beliefs such as "it is good to brush your teeth after every meal." Because these attitudes have never been challenged, individuals lack practice in generating counterarguments, making them surprisingly vulnerable to persuasive attacks. The inoculation procedure remedies this by (1) alerting the person that their belief may be challenged, creating a motivational "threat," and (2) providing exposure to mild counterarguments together with their refutations, giving the person both the motivation and the cognitive ammunition to resist future persuasion.

Research has consistently shown that this refutational defense is more effective at building resistance than a supportive defense, which simply provides additional reasons to maintain the original attitude without addressing potential counterarguments. The refutational approach works because it activates deeper processing and rehearsal of defensive responses, whereas merely adding supportive bolstering leaves people unprepared for novel attacks on their position.

Example

A public health campaign aims to prevent adolescents from taking up smoking. Rather than only presenting reasons not to smoke (supportive defense), the inoculation approach first exposes teens to the kinds of weak pro-smoking arguments they might hear from peers—"smoking makes you look cool" or "everyone experiments"—and then walks them through why these arguments are flawed. When the teens later encounter real peer pressure, they have already practiced rejecting similar appeals, making them significantly more resistant to influence.

Psychological Reactance (Brehm, 1966)

When people perceive that their freedom to choose is being threatened or eliminated, they experience reactance — a motivational state directed toward restoring the threatened freedom. This often results in doing the opposite of what is demanded. The "forbidden fruit" effect and the "Romeo and Juliet effect" (parental opposition intensifying romantic attraction) are examples of reactance.

Clinical Application

Reactance is clinically relevant in working with resistant clients. Paradoxical interventions (prescribing the symptom) and motivational interviewing (rolling with resistance) are both strategies that leverage or reduce reactance. When a therapist says "You probably aren't ready to change yet," the client's reactance may motivate them to prove the therapist wrong.

Minority Influence

Serge Moscovici demonstrated that consistent minorities can influence the majority, particularly through informational influence. A minority position that is maintained consistently over time leads the majority to re-examine the issue more deeply (central processing), which can produce genuine private attitude change — even when the majority does not publicly agree.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • ELM: Central route = deep processing, durable change; Peripheral route = superficial cues, temporary change. Know what determines which route is used (motivation and ability).
  • Sleeper effect: Low-credibility source gains persuasive power over time as source is forgotten.
  • Cialdini's 6 principles: Reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Be able to match techniques to principles.
  • Asch conformity: ~75% conformed at least once; one dissenter dramatically reduces conformity.
  • Normative vs. informational influence: Normative = acceptance by group; informational = accuracy.
  • Milgram obedience: 65% obeyed to maximum shock. Know factors that increase and decrease obedience.
  • Compliance techniques: Know foot-in-the-door (small then large), door-in-the-face (large then small), and lowball.
  • Reactance theory: Threatened freedom motivates opposing behavior. Relevant to paradoxical interventions in therapy.
  • Minority influence: Consistency is the key to a minority changing the majority's views.
