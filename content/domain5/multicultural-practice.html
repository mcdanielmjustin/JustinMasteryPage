Sue's Multicultural Competencies

Imagine a well-meaning psychologist who genuinely wants to provide effective therapy to clients from all backgrounds. She has examined her own biases through personal therapy, she has read extensively about the cultural histories of her diverse client population, and yet — when she sits down in session — she finds herself defaulting to the same CBT worksheets and Socratic questioning techniques she uses with everyone, regardless of cultural context. Something is missing. But what, exactly? And how would we even organize the different facets of cultural competence so that gaps like this become visible? These are the questions that Derald Wing Sue and his colleagues set out to answer in what became one of the most influential frameworks in the history of multicultural psychology.

In the early 1990s, the field of counseling psychology was grappling with a growing recognition that traditional therapeutic approaches — largely developed by and for White, Western, middle-class populations — were not serving an increasingly diverse client base. Clinicians needed more than good intentions; they needed a systematic way to think about what multicultural competence actually looked like in practice. Sue, Arredondo, and McDavis (1992) responded with a framework that was elegantly simple yet remarkably comprehensive: a model organized around three core domains, each applied across three dimensions, creating a 3 × 3 matrix of nine competency areas that has since shaped training programs, ethical guidelines, and licensing exams alike.

Three Domains of Multicultural Competence (Sue et al., 1992)

1. Awareness: Understanding of one's own cultural values, biases, and assumptions, and how these may affect the therapeutic relationship. This domain asks clinicians to turn the lens inward — to recognize that they are not culturally neutral beings but products of particular cultural contexts that shape how they perceive clients, formulate diagnoses, and conceptualize "health."
2. Knowledge: Understanding of the worldviews, cultural practices, and historical experiences of culturally different clients. This goes beyond surface-level familiarity with cultural customs; it includes understanding systemic oppression, immigration experiences, acculturation stress, and the ways that social identity shapes a client's relationship to help-seeking itself.
3. Skills: The ability to develop and implement culturally appropriate intervention strategies and techniques. Skills represent the "doing" component — translating awareness and knowledge into concrete clinical actions such as adapting assessment tools, modifying therapeutic techniques, or advocating for clients within systems that may be structurally biased against them.

What makes Sue's model especially powerful — and especially testable on the EPPP — is the way these three domains interact with three cross-cutting dimensions: (a) attitudes and beliefs, (b) knowledge, and (c) skills, creating a 3 × 3 matrix of nine competency areas. For example, within the Awareness domain, a clinician must examine her attitudes and beliefs about her own cultural heritage, acquire knowledge about how her background influences her clinical work, and develop skills for ongoing self-monitoring. The matrix structure ensures that competence is not treated as a single trait you either have or lack — it is multidimensional, and a clinician can be strong in some cells while needing growth in others.

Think of it this way: awareness without knowledge is navel-gazing; knowledge without skills is academic exercise; and skills without awareness risk becoming a culturally tone-deaf application of techniques that may do more harm than good. The framework insists that all three domains develop in concert. A therapist who has deep awareness of her own privilege and extensive knowledge of intergenerational trauma in Indigenous communities but who cannot translate that understanding into a culturally grounded treatment plan remains, by this model's standards, incomplete in her competence.

Clinical Application

A psychologist who is aware of their own cultural biases (awareness) and understands the historical trauma experienced by Native American communities (knowledge) but lacks skills in culturally adapted interventions is not yet fully competent. All three domains must be developed. Consider a concrete example: a clinician working with a Lakota client experiencing grief after a family loss might recognize that her own Euro-American assumptions about "healthy" grief timelines could be limiting (awareness), understand that communal mourning rituals and spiritual practices hold deep significance in Lakota culture (knowledge), and then actively collaborate with the client and community elders to integrate traditional healing practices alongside evidence-based approaches (skills). Only when all three domains are engaged does the clinician meet the standard Sue and colleagues envisioned. The EPPP tests this framework frequently — expect questions that present a clinician demonstrating strength in one or two domains and ask you to identify the missing competency area.

As we turn next to the APA Multicultural Guidelines, you will see how Sue's foundational competency framework was taken up and expanded by the American Psychological Association into a broader set of aspirational principles — moving from individual clinician competence to institutional and systemic considerations. Sue's 3 × 3 matrix provided the scaffolding upon which much of that later work was built.

Sue, D. W., Arredondo, P., & McDavis, R. J. (1992). Multicultural counseling competencies and standards: A call to the profession. Journal of Counseling & Development, 70(4), 477–486.

APA Multicultural Guidelines

Having explored Sue's foundational multicultural competencies — the tripartite framework of awareness, knowledge, and skills — we now turn to how the profession's largest governing body translated those aspirations into institutional guidance. If Sue and colleagues issued a "call to the profession," the American Psychological Association answered with a set of formal guidelines designed to embed cultural responsiveness into every corner of psychological work, not just clinical practice but also research, education, training, and organizational policy.

Consider a psychologist who has spent years mastering evidence-based treatments for depression. She knows cognitive-behavioral protocols inside and out, can recite the diagnostic criteria effortlessly, and has strong therapeutic outcomes with many clients. Yet when she begins working with a first-generation Hmong American family, she discovers that the family's understanding of emotional suffering is organized around spiritual and somatic frameworks that do not map neatly onto Western diagnostic categories. Her clinical tools are not wrong — they are simply incomplete without a cultural lens. This is precisely the gap the APA's guidelines were designed to address: the recognition that no psychological act — whether assessment, therapy, research design, or policy recommendation — occurs outside of culture.

The APA has published Multicultural Guidelines that emphasize a deceptively simple but transformative premise: all psychology is embedded in culture. Culture is not a special-topics add-on or a sidebar reserved for "diverse populations." It is the water in which every psychological process swims. Accordingly, the guidelines call on psychologists to weave cultural consideration into all aspects of practice, education, and research. Rather than offering rigid rules, the guidelines articulate aspirational principles — benchmarks that orient the profession toward deeper cultural humility and competence over time. Key principles include:

  • Psychologists are encouraged to recognize that they themselves are cultural beings with attitudes and beliefs that can influence their practice
  • Psychologists aspire to recognize the importance of multicultural sensitivity, responsiveness, knowledge, and understanding about diverse individuals and communities
  • Psychologists are encouraged to apply culturally appropriate skills in clinical and other applied psychological practices
  • Psychologists strive to use organizational change processes to support culturally informed policy development and practices

Notice the deliberate progression embedded in these principles. The first principle mirrors the "awareness" dimension from Sue's model — before you can work effectively across cultures, you must understand your own cultural positioning, biases, and assumptions. A White, middle-class psychologist who has never interrogated how her cultural background shapes her definitions of "healthy family functioning" or "appropriate emotional expression" may inadvertently pathologize culturally normative behavior. Self-awareness is the necessary starting point because, without it, cultural knowledge and skills rest on an unstable foundation.

The second principle broadens the lens outward: once you know yourself as a cultural being, you must actively build knowledge about the communities you serve. This is not about memorizing cultural "facts" — for example, that Latino families tend to be collectivistic — but about developing genuine responsiveness to the lived realities, strengths, histories of oppression, and within-group diversity that characterize any cultural community. A clinician working with Native American clients, for instance, would need to appreciate not just broad cultural values but also the specific tribal context, the intergenerational impact of forced assimilation policies, and the tremendous diversity that exists among the over 570 federally recognized tribes.

The third principle addresses the application of culturally appropriate skills — the "how" of culturally responsive practice. In a clinical setting, this might mean adapting assessment tools for linguistic equivalence, incorporating traditional healing practices alongside empirically supported treatments, or simply knowing when to ask about cultural explanations of distress rather than assuming a biomedical framework. For a school psychologist, it might mean recognizing that a bilingual child's code-switching is a cognitive strength, not a sign of language disorder.

The fourth principle is especially noteworthy because it extends beyond the individual practitioner to the systems and organizations in which psychologists operate. Even the most culturally competent clinician will struggle to provide equitable care within an organization whose intake forms offer only "Male" and "Female," whose waiting room magazines feature only White families, or whose leadership has never examined disparities in service delivery. This principle calls psychologists to be agents of institutional change — advocating for policies, hiring practices, and organizational cultures that reflect the diversity of the populations served.

For the EPPP, remember that the APA Multicultural Guidelines differ from Sue's competencies in scope and institutional weight: they represent the profession's formal aspirational standards, apply broadly across all domains of psychological work (not only counseling), and explicitly frame culture as foundational to — rather than separate from — all psychological endeavors. Together with Sue's competency framework, they form the conceptual backbone for culturally responsive practice that the DSM-5's Cultural Formulation tools, discussed next, translate into structured clinical application.

DSM-5 Cultural Formulation

Having explored the APA Multicultural Guidelines and their call to embed cultural awareness into every aspect of psychological practice, a natural next question arises: How does a clinician actually do this in the therapy room — especially during that critical first encounter with a new client? Imagine sitting across from a middle-aged woman who has recently immigrated from Cambodia. She describes persistent headaches, dizziness, and a feeling of pressure in her chest. She does not use the word "depression." She does not mention sadness. If you rely solely on standard diagnostic interviewing, you might pursue a medical referral and miss the psychological suffering altogether. The developers of the DSM-5 recognized this gap and created a structured tool specifically designed to bridge the divide between a clinician's diagnostic framework and the client's own cultural understanding of what is wrong.

Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI)

The DSM-5 includes a Cultural Formulation Interview — a structured set of 16 questions designed to help clinicians understand the impact of culture on a client's presentation. Rather than imposing Western diagnostic categories from the outset, the CFI invites clients to describe their experience in their own words and within their own cultural framework. It covers four domains: (1) cultural definition of the problem — how the client understands and names what is happening; (2) cultural perceptions of cause, context, and support — what the client believes is causing their distress and who in their life provides help; (3) cultural factors affecting self-coping and past help-seeking — what the client has already tried, including traditional or indigenous healing practices; and (4) cultural factors affecting current help-seeking — what barriers or expectations shape the client's engagement with the clinician right now.

What makes the CFI so valuable is that it shifts the power dynamic. Instead of the clinician serving as the sole expert who categorizes symptoms into a diagnostic box, the CFI positions the client as the expert on their own experience. The clinician becomes a curious, respectful learner — and the information that emerges often transforms the entire case conceptualization. Questions are open-ended and nonjudgmental: "What do you think is causing your problem?" "Are there any kinds of support that make your problem better, such as support from family, friends, or others?" These questions communicate genuine interest and can dramatically strengthen the therapeutic alliance, particularly with clients who may be wary of Western mental health systems.

Example

A Cambodian refugee presents with somatic complaints (headaches, dizziness) rather than reporting depressed mood. Using the CFI, the clinician learns that the client conceptualizes their suffering as "thinking too much" (a culturally recognized idiom of distress in Cambodian culture) and that they have consulted a traditional healer before seeking Western treatment. This information fundamentally shapes the treatment plan. Rather than dismissing the client's somatic focus or insisting on a mood-disorder framework, the clinician can integrate the client's explanatory model, potentially collaborating with the traditional healer and framing interventions in language that resonates — for instance, describing cognitive-behavioral strategies as ways to "quiet the thinking" rather than as treatments for "depression."

Beyond the CFI itself, the DSM-5 took another important step toward cultural competence by including a Glossary of Cultural Concepts of Distress. This glossary describes culturally specific syndromes — patterns of suffering that are widely recognized within particular cultural groups but may be unfamiliar to clinicians trained exclusively in Western diagnostic traditions. For example, ataque de nervios, common in Latin American communities, involves intense emotional episodes with features such as uncontrollable crying, trembling, and a sense of losing control — sometimes triggered by a stressful family event. Susto, also found in Latin American cultures, refers to a condition attributed to a frightening event that causes the soul to leave the body, resulting in unhappiness, sickness, and difficulty functioning. Taijin kyofusho, recognized in Japanese culture, involves an intense fear that one's body or bodily functions are offensive to others — a presentation that overlaps with but is distinct from social anxiety disorder. And khyol cap, described in Cambodian communities, involves a fear of "wind attacks" in which khyol (wind) rises in the body and causes dizziness, blurred vision, and other distressing symptoms. Understanding these cultural concepts of distress allows the clinician to recognize that what might initially appear puzzling or atypical through a Western lens may in fact represent a well-understood pattern of suffering within the client's cultural world.

For the EPPP, remember that the Cultural Formulation Interview consists of 16 questions organized into four domains, and that it was introduced in the DSM-5 as a tool for systematic cultural assessment. Also be prepared to recognize specific cultural concepts of distress from the glossary and to understand that these syndromes are not exotic curiosities — they are clinically meaningful presentations that require culturally informed assessment and treatment. This structured approach to culture in diagnosis reflects the broader principle from the APA Multicultural Guidelines: that culture is not an afterthought in clinical work but a central feature of every client's experience.

Racial & Ethnic Identity Models

Cross's Nigrescence Model (Black Racial Identity)

Cross's Model (1971, revised 1991)

1. Pre-encounter: Internalization of dominant-culture values; low salience of race; may hold anti-Black attitudes.
2. Encounter: A significant experience (often involving racism) shatters the pre-encounter worldview and sparks exploration of racial identity.
3. Immersion-Emersion: Intense immersion in Black culture; may involve anti-White sentiments (immersion) followed by a more nuanced, less reactive stance (emersion).
4. Internalization: Secure, confident Black identity integrated with openness to other groups; activism may or may not be present.
5. Internalization-Commitment: Translation of internalized identity into sustained action toward social justice.

Helms' White Racial Identity Model

Helms' Model

Phase I — Abandonment of Racism:
Contact: Obliviousness to racial issues; colorblind stance.
Disintegration: Growing awareness of racial inequality creates moral dilemma and anxiety.
Reintegration: Resolution of anxiety through idealization of Whiteness and intolerance toward minorities.

Phase II — Defining a Non-Racist White Identity:
Pseudo-independence: Intellectual acknowledgment of White privilege; may still hold paternalistic attitudes.
Immersion-Emersion: Active exploration of what it means to be White; seeking accurate information about racism.
Autonomy: Internalized non-racist White identity; appreciation of cultural diversity; active commitment to anti-racism.

Cass's Sexual Orientation Identity Development Model

Vivienne Cass (1979) proposed one of the first and most influential models of sexual orientation identity development. The model describes six stages through which individuals progressively integrate their sexual orientation into their overall sense of identity:

Cass's Six Stages (1979)

1. Identity Confusion: The individual first recognizes that their thoughts, feelings, or behaviors could be labeled as homosexual. This creates confusion and a sense of inner turmoil ("Could this apply to me?").
2. Identity Comparison: The individual tentatively accepts the possibility of a non-heterosexual identity and begins to explore what this means. Feelings of alienation from heterosexual peers may emerge as the individual recognizes differences ("I may be different from others").
3. Identity Tolerance: The individual acknowledges that they are likely gay or lesbian and begins seeking out the LGBTQ+ community for social and emotional support. The identity is tolerated rather than fully embraced ("I probably am gay").
4. Identity Acceptance: The individual accepts (rather than merely tolerates) their sexual orientation and develops increasing contact with the LGBTQ+ community. Selective disclosure to trusted others begins.
5. Identity Pride: The individual immerses in LGBTQ+ culture and may develop an "us vs. them" stance, with strong pride in the in-group and devaluation of heterosexual norms. Activism and confrontation of homophobia may be prominent.
6. Identity Synthesis: Sexual orientation becomes integrated into the individual's overall identity rather than being the primary defining characteristic. The rigid us/them dichotomy softens, and the individual views their sexual orientation as one important but not all-encompassing aspect of who they are.

Clinical Application

Not all individuals progress through every stage, and some may remain in earlier stages due to environmental constraints, cultural factors, or personal choice. The model is not rigidly linear -- individuals may revisit stages across the lifespan, particularly in response to new social contexts or life transitions. Clinicians should assess where a client falls in this process to inform culturally responsive treatment, recognizing that different stages carry different therapeutic needs (e.g., identity confusion may require exploration and normalization, while identity pride may call for support of activism alongside exploration of relational flexibility).

Cass, V. C. (1979). Homosexual identity formation: A theoretical model. Journal of Homosexuality, 4(3), 219–235.

Cross, W. E., Jr. (1991). Shades of Black: Diversity in African-American identity. Temple University Press.

The ADDRESSING Framework

Having explored how individuals develop and navigate their racial and ethnic identities, we now face a critical clinical question: what happens when race and ethnicity are only part of the story? Consider a 72-year-old Latina woman who presents with depression following a stroke that left her with limited mobility. She is a devout Catholic, grew up in rural Mexico, and lives on a fixed income. If her therapist focuses only on her ethnic identity, they may miss the ways that aging, acquired disability, socioeconomic stress, religious coping, immigration history, and gender all converge to shape her experience of depression — and her expectations for treatment. Effective multicultural practice demands a wider lens, one that systematically accounts for the many dimensions of identity that influence how a person moves through the world.

This is precisely why Pamela Hays developed a practical mnemonic tool that encourages clinicians to think beyond any single identity category. Rather than relying on intuition or defaulting to the most visible aspect of a client's background, the ADDRESSING Framework provides a structured checklist of ten cultural factors that clinicians should consider during assessment, case conceptualization, and treatment planning. The acronym itself serves as a cognitive prompt — each letter pointing to a dimension of identity that may carry privilege, marginalization, or both, depending on the client's social context.

ADDRESSING Framework (Hays, 2001)

Pamela Hays developed the ADDRESSING framework to help clinicians systematically consider the multiple cultural influences that shape each client's identity. The acronym maps onto ten dimensions, each of which may significantly affect a client's worldview, access to resources, experiences of discrimination, and therapeutic needs:
A — Age and generational influences
D — Developmental or other Disability
D — Disability acquired later in life
R — Religion and spiritual orientation
E — Ethnic and racial identity
S — Socioeconomic status
S — Sexual orientation
I — Indigenous heritage
N — National origin
G — Gender

Notice how the framework captures dimensions that might otherwise be invisible in a standard intake. A clinician who asks about ethnicity may never think to ask how a client's generational cohort shapes their attitudes toward mental health. A therapist working with a young man presenting with anxiety might overlook the role of his Indigenous heritage or the impact of immigrating as a child if the framework's prompts aren't used. The power of the ADDRESSING model lies in its comprehensiveness: it ensures that no single identity dimension dominates the clinical picture while others go unexplored.

Each letter in the acronym also invites the clinician to reflect on their own positionality. For example, a clinician who has never experienced disability may hold unconscious assumptions about what life with a disability entails. A therapist from a high socioeconomic background may underestimate how profoundly financial insecurity shapes a client's capacity to follow through on treatment recommendations. By walking through each dimension for both the client and themselves, clinicians can identify potential blind spots, power differentials, and areas where cultural humility is especially needed.

From a practical standpoint, the ADDRESSING framework is most useful when applied early — during intake, treatment planning, and supervision discussions — rather than invoked only after a cultural misunderstanding has already occurred. Think of it as a preventive tool: just as a medical checklist reduces the likelihood of surgical errors, the ADDRESSING framework reduces the likelihood of cultural oversights that can erode the therapeutic alliance, lead to misdiagnosis, or cause premature termination.

Clinical Application

The ADDRESSING framework reminds clinicians to go beyond race and ethnicity and consider the full range of cultural identities that shape a client's experience. Each dimension represents both potential areas of privilege and potential areas of marginalization. For instance, a middle-aged gay man with no disabilities may hold privilege along some dimensions (age, ability status) while experiencing marginalization along others (sexual orientation, and potentially religion if he was raised in a faith tradition that condemns homosexuality). Using this framework systematically during intake and case conceptualization reduces the risk of overlooking important cultural factors. It also encourages clinicians to consider intersectionality — the way multiple identity dimensions interact to produce unique experiences of advantage or disadvantage that cannot be understood by examining any single dimension in isolation.

As we turn next to barriers to treatment and health disparities, keep the ADDRESSING framework in mind. Many of the systemic obstacles that prevent individuals from accessing or benefiting from mental health services map directly onto the dimensions Hays identified — age, disability, socioeconomic status, national origin, and other factors that determine who gets help, who doesn't, and why.

Barriers to Treatment & Health Disparities

Consider a Spanish-speaking mother experiencing severe postpartum depression. She knows something is wrong — the persistent sadness, the inability to bond with her newborn, the intrusive thoughts that terrify her. She musters the courage to call her insurance company's behavioral health line, only to learn that no Spanish-speaking therapists are available within a 50-mile radius. She's offered an interpreter, but the idea of disclosing her darkest thoughts through a stranger feels unbearable. She hangs up and doesn't call back. Six months later, she arrives at the emergency room in crisis. This scenario — repeated in countless variations across communities every day — illustrates why understanding barriers to treatment is not an academic exercise but a matter of clinical urgency and, often, life or death.

As we discussed in the preceding section, the ADDRESSING framework maps the many dimensions of identity that shape a person's experience. Those same dimensions — age, disability status, socioeconomic class, national origin, and others — also predict who encounters obstacles when seeking mental health care. The gap between needing help and receiving effective help is not random; it follows predictable fault lines of race, ethnicity, language, income, and historical experience. Racial and ethnic minorities, in particular, face a constellation of barriers that compound one another, creating disparities that persist even when services technically exist. Understanding these barriers is essential for any clinician who wants to do more than treat the patients who happen to make it through the door.

The major barriers to accessing and benefiting from mental health services can be organized into several overlapping categories, each of which deserves careful attention:

  • Language barriers: Lack of linguistically matched providers; loss of nuance through interpreters
  • Cultural stigma: Many cultures view mental illness as shameful or as a sign of personal weakness
  • Historical mistrust: Legacies of unethical treatment (e.g., Tuskegee syphilis study, forced sterilization) create distrust of healthcare systems
  • Socioeconomic barriers: Lack of insurance, transportation, childcare; limited access to providers in underserved areas
  • Clinician bias: Implicit biases can affect diagnosis, treatment recommendations, and the therapeutic relationship

Notice how these barriers interact and amplify one another. A low-income immigrant client may face language barriers, cultural stigma, historical mistrust of institutions, and lack of insurance simultaneously. The compounding effect is not merely additive — each barrier makes the others harder to overcome. A client who distrusts healthcare systems is less likely to persist through a frustrating search for a bilingual provider. A client already struggling to afford transportation is more likely to discontinue treatment after a culturally insensitive encounter. This is why addressing disparities requires systemic thinking, not just individual goodwill.

Language barriers deserve special emphasis because they affect every stage of clinical contact. Even when interpreters are available, critical therapeutic nuances — the precise emotional coloring of a word, the cultural connotations of an idiom of distress, the implicit meaning carried by a pause or a euphemism — can be lost in translation. Research suggests that clients communicating through interpreters disclose less, feel less understood, and are more likely to terminate treatment prematurely. The therapeutic relationship, which depends so heavily on felt attunement, is fundamentally altered when a third party mediates every exchange.

Historical mistrust, too, warrants more than a passing mention. The Tuskegee syphilis study — in which Black men were deliberately denied treatment for syphilis for decades so researchers could observe the disease's progression — is often cited as a singular atrocity. But it was not singular. Forced sterilization programs targeted Native American, Latina, and Black women well into the 1970s. Psychiatric institutions were used to confine and control people of color for behaviors that challenged racial hierarchies. These are not distant historical footnotes; they are living memories in many communities, passed down through families and reinforced by ongoing experiences of discrimination within healthcare settings. When a client from one of these communities hesitates to trust a clinician, that hesitation is not pathological — it is rational and historically informed.

Example: Diagnostic Disparities

Research consistently shows that African Americans are more likely to be diagnosed with schizophrenia and less likely to be diagnosed with mood disorders compared to White Americans with identical symptoms. This diagnostic disparity likely reflects clinician bias, cultural differences in symptom expression, and the impact of stereotype-driven clinical judgment. In one landmark study, clinicians were given identical clinical vignettes in which only the patient's race was varied; African American patients were significantly more likely to receive a schizophrenia diagnosis. Such findings underscore that clinician bias is not hypothetical — it is measurable, consequential, and directly harmful to the people we are ethically obligated to serve.

Culturally Adapted Interventions

If the barriers outlined above describe the problem, culturally adapted interventions represent one important part of the solution. A common misconception among clinicians-in-training is that cultural adaptation means abandoning evidence-based practice — that you must choose between scientific rigor and cultural responsiveness. In reality, the research tells a different story. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that culturally adapted treatments produce better outcomes for ethnic minority clients than their unadapted counterparts, and these adaptations need not compromise the core mechanisms of change that make the treatments effective in the first place.

Evidence-based treatments can be culturally adapted without sacrificing their empirical foundation. Adaptations may include: incorporating cultural values and metaphors that resonate with the client's worldview, modifying the language and delivery format to be linguistically accessible, involving family members in treatment (consistent with collectivist values that prioritize family harmony and interdependence), directly addressing acculturative stress as a treatment target, and integrating traditional healing practices alongside Western approaches. For example, a cognitive-behavioral therapy protocol for depression might be adapted for Latino clients by incorporating the concept of familismo — the deep value placed on family loyalty and closeness — into behavioral activation goals, framing increased social engagement not as an individual self-care strategy but as a way of fulfilling family obligations and strengthening family bonds.

The key principle is that effective adaptation preserves the active ingredients of a treatment while modifying its surface structure — the examples used, the language spoken, the metaphors invoked, the people included in the room — to fit the client's cultural context. This requires clinicians to possess genuine cultural knowledge, not just good intentions. It also requires humility: the willingness to ask clients what feels relevant and meaningful to them, rather than assuming that a textbook description of their culture applies to their individual experience.

Clinical Application

Microaggressions in therapy — such as a clinician assuming a client's problems stem from their culture, expressing surprise at a client's articulateness, or denying the relevance of race ("I don't see color") — can severely damage the therapeutic alliance. These moments may seem small to the clinician but land with cumulative weight for the client, who has likely experienced hundreds of similar slights across a lifetime. Clinicians must engage in ongoing self-reflection, seek consultation, and be willing to acknowledge and repair ruptures caused by cultural insensitivity. A rupture that is named, explored, and genuinely repaired can actually strengthen the alliance — but only if the clinician resists the impulse to become defensive and instead approaches the moment with curiosity and accountability.

Social Justice in Psychology

The multicultural movement in psychology has increasingly emphasized that competence requires not just individual-level intervention but also advocacy and social justice work. Psychologists are called upon to address systemic barriers, advocate for equitable policies, and challenge institutional structures that perpetuate disparities.

The APA Multicultural Guidelines explicitly state that psychologists should "strive to use organizational change processes to support culturally informed policy development and practices." This represents a shift from viewing cultural competence as solely an individual clinical skill to recognizing it as a professional and ethical obligation with systemic implications.
