Where We Break — Prejudice, Stereotypes & Discrimination

Fundamental Content
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

The Anatomy of Prejudice

Consider a hiring manager reviewing a stack of résumés. She notices a candidate with a traditionally African American name, and a thought flickers through her mind: "People from that background probably didn't attend top schools." That's a generalized belief — a cognitive shortcut applied to an entire group. She feels a twinge of discomfort she can't quite name — a vague unease about the candidate that has nothing to do with qualifications. That's an emotional reaction directed at someone because of group membership. Then she moves that résumé to the bottom of the pile without reading further. That's a behavior — an action taken against someone based solely on the group they belong to. These three moments — the thought, the feeling, and the action — represent three psychologically distinct processes that together form the architecture of prejudice. Understanding how they differ, how they interact, and how one can exist without the others is essential for both clinical practice and the EPPP.

The Three Components

Stereotypes (cognitive): Generalized beliefs about the characteristics of a group and its members. Stereotypes function as mental schemas — they organize information quickly but often inaccurately, assigning traits to individuals based on group identity rather than personal knowledge.
Prejudice (affective): A negative (or positive) emotional attitude toward a group and its members. Prejudice is the feeling dimension — the warmth, hostility, contempt, or anxiety one experiences in response to someone's perceived group membership.
Discrimination (behavioral): Unjustified negative behavior directed toward members of a group based on their group membership. Discrimination is prejudice in action — it translates internal attitudes into observable conduct that creates real-world harm.

These three components map neatly onto the classic tripartite model of attitudes — cognition, affect, and behavior — which makes them easier to remember and clinically useful to assess. A stereotype is what you think, prejudice is what you feel, and discrimination is what you do. This distinction matters because interventions targeting one component may not automatically change the others. A diversity training program might successfully challenge stereotypical beliefs (cognition) while leaving deeply rooted emotional reactions (affect) untouched, or a person may suppress discriminatory behavior in public settings while privately harboring intense prejudice.

A person can hold stereotypes without being prejudiced and can be prejudiced without discriminating. However, all three frequently co-occur and reinforce one another. For instance, a clinician might be aware of the stereotype that older adults are cognitively rigid (cognitive component), yet feel no animosity toward elderly clients and treat them with full clinical rigor. Conversely, someone may genuinely dislike a particular ethnic group (affective prejudice) but never act on that feeling because of social norms, laws, or personal values that suppress discriminatory behavior. Research consistently shows, however, that when stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination align — when thought, feeling, and action point in the same direction — the effects on targets are most damaging, contributing to health disparities, educational inequities, and psychological distress.

As we turn next to theories of prejudice, we'll explore why these patterns develop in the first place — what drives human minds toward stereotyping, what social conditions intensify prejudice, and why discrimination persists even among people who consciously endorse egalitarian values. The anatomy described here gives us the "what"; the theories ahead will give us the "why."

Theories of Prejudice

Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979)

Social Identity Theory

Henri Tajfel proposed that people derive part of their self-concept from the groups to which they belong (in-groups). To maintain positive self-esteem, people are motivated to view their in-groups favorably, leading to in-group favoritism and out-group derogation. Social categorization alone is sufficient to trigger bias.

Minimal Group Paradigm

Tajfel demonstrated that even when people are assigned to groups based on trivial criteria (e.g., preference for Klee vs. Kandinsky paintings), they show in-group bias — allocating more resources to in-group members. Mere categorization, not conflict, is sufficient for bias.

Realistic Conflict Theory

Realistic Conflict Theory & Robbers Cave (Sherif, 1961)

Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment demonstrated that intergroup hostility arises from competition over limited resources. Two groups of boys ("Eagles" and "Rattlers") developed intense animosity when placed in competition. Hostility was reduced only when the groups had to cooperate on superordinate goals — tasks neither group could accomplish alone.

Example: Superordinate Goals

Simply bringing the groups together for pleasant activities (movies, meals) did not reduce hostility. Only when they had to work together — fixing the camp water supply, pulling a stalled truck — did prejudice decrease. This finding has been applied in schools, workplaces, and international diplomacy.

Allport's Contact Hypothesis

Gordon Allport (1954) proposed that intergroup contact can reduce prejudice only under specific conditions:

  • Equal status: Groups must interact as equals
  • Common goals: Groups must work toward shared objectives
  • Intergroup cooperation: Achievement of goals must require cooperation
  • Support of authorities: Contact must be sanctioned by institutional authority

Allport, G. W. (1954). The nature of prejudice. Addison-Wesley.

Modern Forms of Prejudice

Aversive Racism

Aversive racism (Gaertner & Dovidio) describes people who sincerely endorse egalitarian values but harbor unconscious negative feelings toward out-groups. Bias emerges in ambiguous situations where discrimination can be rationalized on non-racial grounds.

Modern (Symbolic) Racism

Modern racism expresses prejudice indirectly through opposition to policies perceived as benefiting minority groups while denying that discrimination still exists.

Microaggressions

Microaggressions (Sue et al., 2007)

Brief, commonplace exchanges that communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative slights toward marginalized groups. Three types: microassaults (deliberate), microinsults (unintentionally demeaning), and microinvalidations (dismissing experiential reality). Examples: "Where are you really from?" or "You're so articulate" (expressed with surprise to a person of color).

Clinical Relevance

Cumulative exposure to microaggressions is associated with depression, anxiety, decreased self-esteem, and racial battle fatigue. Clinicians must recognize microaggressions both as experiences reported by clients and as behaviors they themselves might inadvertently commit in the therapeutic relationship.

Stereotype Threat

Having explored the modern and often subtle faces of prejudice — from aversive racism to the cumulative toll of microaggressions — we now turn to one of the most consequential discoveries in social psychology: the way that simply knowing a negative stereotype exists can undermine the very performance it claims to predict. Imagine a brilliant graduate student sitting down to take the GRE. She has excelled in every course, earned glowing recommendations, and prepared meticulously. But as she opens the quantitative section, a thought flickers through her mind: People say women aren't as good at math. She doesn't believe it — she has a lifetime of evidence to the contrary — yet the thought lingers, quietly siphoning away the cognitive resources she needs. Her working memory, now partly occupied by managing this anxiety, has less bandwidth for the complex problems in front of her. Her score drops — not because she lacks ability, but because she is carrying a psychological burden that her male peers do not share in that moment.

This phenomenon, first documented in a landmark study, revealed something both surprising and deeply important: you do not have to internalize a stereotype for it to hurt you. The mere awareness that others might view you through the lens of a negative group stereotype is enough to trigger a cascade of anxiety, self-monitoring, and cognitive interference that undermines performance. The effect is not about belief — it is about situational pressure.

Stereotype Threat (Steele & Aronson, 1995)

Stereotype threat is the apprehension experienced when a person is at risk of confirming a negative stereotype about their group. This anxiety consumes working memory and impairs performance. The person does not need to believe the stereotype — mere awareness that others might apply it is sufficient. Critically, stereotype threat is situational rather than dispositional — it arises from cues in the environment that make a stereotype salient, meaning that altering those cues can eliminate the effect entirely.

Classic Findings

When Black students were told a test measured intellectual ability, they performed worse than White students. When the same test was described as non-diagnostic, the gap disappeared. Similarly, women performed worse on math tests when gender stereotypes were salient, and White men performed worse on athletic tasks when racial athletic stereotypes were activated. These findings underscore a crucial point: stereotype threat is not limited to any one group. Any individual can experience it when a relevant negative stereotype is made salient — the mechanism is universal even though the stereotypes that trigger it are socially specific.

Understanding that stereotype threat is situational means it can be countered. Research has identified several factors that reduce its impact: reframing the task as non-diagnostic of ability removes the evaluative pressure that activates the threat; emphasizing a growth mindset — the idea that intelligence and skill are malleable rather than fixed — helps individuals interpret difficulty as a normal part of learning rather than as confirmation of a stereotype; providing in-group role models who have succeeded in the domain signals that the stereotype does not define one's potential; and self-affirmation exercises, in which individuals reflect on their core values and strengths before a high-stakes task, bolster a sense of self-integrity that buffers against threat. Each of these interventions works by disrupting the link between the testing situation and the negative stereotype.

Clinical Application

Stereotype threat is directly relevant to psychological testing. When administering cognitive or achievement tests to members of stereotyped groups, clinicians should create non-threatening testing environments and frame tests carefully to obtain more valid results. For example, describing an IQ test as a "problem-solving exercise" rather than a "measure of intellectual capacity" can meaningfully change outcomes. Practitioners should also consider rapport-building, the demographic match between examiner and examinee, and the physical testing environment as factors that may inadvertently activate or mitigate stereotype threat. Failure to account for these dynamics risks interpreting situationally depressed scores as reflections of true ability — a consequential error that can affect diagnoses, placements, and life trajectories.

Stereotype threat bridges the topics we have been exploring — prejudice, discrimination, and their modern expressions — with the question we turn to next: what can be done to reduce prejudice and its effects? If something as subtle as the framing of a test can activate a stereotype and suppress performance, then intentional changes in how we structure environments, communicate expectations, and design interventions can begin to dismantle these effects. The research on stereotype threat reminds us that prejudice does not only operate through overt hostility or even implicit bias — it can be carried in the very air of a testing room, a classroom, or a clinic, affecting outcomes in ways that neither the evaluator nor the evaluated may fully recognize.

Steele, C. M., & Aronson, J. (1995). Stereotype threat and the intellectual test performance of African Americans. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 69(5), 797–811.

Reducing Prejudice

The Jigsaw Classroom

Developed by Elliot Aronson, the jigsaw classroom gives each student a unique piece of information necessary for the group to complete a task. This creates interdependence, equal status, and shared goals — Allport's conditions for prejudice reduction.

Common In-Group Identity Model

Gaertner and Dovidio proposed that prejudice can be reduced by encouraging members of different groups to recategorize themselves as members of a single, inclusive group — transforming "us vs. them" into "we."

Example

A company reorganizes competitive departments into cross-functional teams. Employees who identified as "Marketing" or "Engineering" begin identifying as "Team Alpha," reducing interdepartmental bias and increasing cooperation.

Aggression

Having explored how prejudice and discrimination can be reduced through contact, cooperation, and recategorization, we now turn to a darker but equally essential topic in social psychology: aggression itself. Consider the difference between a toddler who accidentally knocks over a peer while running and a child who deliberately shoves a classmate off the swings. Both cause harm, but only one is acting aggressively. Or think about a clinical scenario: a therapist working with a couple discovers that one partner's "venting" by punching pillows during arguments actually escalates rather than defuses their anger. Understanding aggression — what drives it, how it's learned, and what amplifies it — is critical not only for the EPPP but for virtually every domain of clinical practice, from anger management to forensic assessment to understanding intimate partner violence.

Aggression Defined

Aggression is behavior intended to harm another person who is motivated to avoid that harm. The key element is intent — accidental harm is not aggression, and the target must be unwilling. Aggression can be physical, verbal, or relational. A surgeon who causes pain during a necessary procedure is not behaving aggressively, because the intent is to heal and the patient consents. But a child who spreads malicious rumors to destroy a peer's friendships is engaging in relational aggression — no physical contact needed.

Types of Aggression

Not all aggression looks or feels the same. Imagine two very different scenes: in the first, a father slams his fist on the table after his teenager hurls an insult at him — his face is red, his heart is pounding, and he is acting out of pure rage. In the second, a chess player in a corporate setting deliberately undermines a colleague's presentation to secure a promotion. Both are aggressive, but the underlying motivations are fundamentally different. Researchers distinguish two fundamental types of aggression:

  • Hostile (affective/reactive) aggression: Driven by anger and the desire to harm the target. The harm itself is the goal. It is impulsive, emotional, and typically a response to perceived provocation or frustration. Think of road rage — a driver who screams obscenities and swerves toward another car after being cut off is not trying to achieve any strategic objective. The aggression is the point.
  • Instrumental (proactive) aggression: Aggression used as a means to an end — the goal is not to harm per se, but to obtain something (resources, power, status). It is calculated and goal-directed rather than emotional. A schoolyard bully who takes a smaller child's lunch money isn't necessarily angry — the aggression is a tool for getting what they want.
Example

A person who punches someone in a rage after being insulted is displaying hostile aggression. A hired enforcer who threatens a shop owner to collect a debt is displaying instrumental aggression — the harm serves a strategic purpose. On the EPPP, when a question describes aggression that is cold, calculated, and aimed at achieving a specific goal, think instrumental. When the description emphasizes anger, provocation, and impulsivity, think hostile.

Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis

Why do people become aggressive in the first place? One of the earliest and most influential theories proposed a deceptively simple answer: frustration. Imagine you're running late for an important job interview and your car won't start. You try the ignition again and again. Your jaw tightens, your hands grip the steering wheel harder, and you find yourself wanting to slam the dashboard. This everyday experience — the urge to lash out when a goal is blocked — is precisely what the frustration-aggression hypothesis sought to explain.

Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939)

The original hypothesis proposed that frustration always leads to some form of aggression, and aggression is always the result of frustration. Frustration was defined as the blocking of a goal-directed behavior. This bold, sweeping claim generated enormous research attention — and significant revision.

This strong formulation was later revised by Leonard Berkowitz, who argued that frustration produces a readiness for aggression (negative affect and arousal), but whether aggression actually occurs depends on the presence of aggressive cues in the environment. Frustration creates the emotional groundwork; situational cues trigger the aggressive response. In Berkowitz's updated model, frustration is neither necessary nor sufficient for aggression — it's one of many sources of negative affect that can prime aggressive responding. A frustrated employee, for instance, might suppress their anger in a calm office but explode if they see a provocative email or overhear an argument nearby. The cues matter as much as the emotion.

Dollard, J., Doob, L. W., Miller, N. E., Mowrer, O. H., & Sears, R. R. (1939). Frustration and aggression. Yale University Press.

Social Learning Theory of Aggression (Bandura)

The frustration-aggression hypothesis emphasizes internal states — blocked goals and negative emotions — as the root of aggression. But Albert Bandura's groundbreaking work revealed something more unsettling: aggression doesn't have to come from within. It can be learned simply by watching someone else do it. This insight, demonstrated through one of psychology's most famous experiments, fundamentally changed how we understand the development of aggressive behavior — and has profound implications for everything from parenting practices to media policy.

Bandura's Social Learning Theory & the Bobo Doll Experiment

Albert Bandura demonstrated that aggression can be learned through observational learning (modeling). In his famous Bobo doll experiments (1961, 1963), children who observed an adult model behaving aggressively toward an inflatable Bobo doll were significantly more aggressive when later placed in a room with the doll, compared to children who observed a non-aggressive model or no model. Critically, children imitated specific novel aggressive acts they had observed (e.g., hitting the doll with a mallet, sitting on it, punching its nose) — behaviors they would not have produced spontaneously, proving that the aggression was acquired through observation rather than mere disinhibition.

Key principles from Bandura's social learning theory of aggression carry significant weight on the EPPP and in clinical practice:

  • Observational learning: Aggression is acquired by watching others behave aggressively — children do not need to be directly reinforced to learn aggressive behavior
  • Vicarious reinforcement: When observers see a model rewarded for aggression, they are more likely to imitate it; when the model is punished, imitation decreases (though the behavior is still learned)
  • Modeling: The model's status, similarity to the observer, and the perceived consequences of aggression all influence whether the observed behavior is performed
  • Distinction between acquisition and performance: People may learn aggressive behaviors through observation but only perform them when conditions are favorable

That last point is clinically crucial and frequently tested. In Bandura's studies, children who saw the aggressive model punished were less likely to spontaneously imitate the aggression — but when offered incentives to demonstrate what they had learned, they could reproduce the aggressive acts just as well as children who saw the model rewarded. The behavior had been acquired; it simply wasn't being performed. This distinction explains why a child exposed to domestic violence may appear calm and well-adjusted in certain settings but display aggression in others when the conditions change.

Bandura, A., Ross, D., & Ross, S. A. (1961). Transmission of aggression through imitation of aggressive models. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 63, 575–582.

Excitation Transfer Theory

Have you ever noticed that arguments seem to escalate more quickly when you're already keyed up — after a stressful commute, an intense workout, or even a scary movie? Dolf Zillmann's excitation transfer theory explains why: your body doesn't always know where its arousal is coming from, and leftover physiological activation from one event can supercharge your emotional response to the next one.

Excitation Transfer Theory (Zillmann)

Dolf Zillmann proposed that residual physiological arousal from one situation can transfer to a subsequent situation and intensify an emotional response, including aggression. If a person is still physiologically aroused from exercise, an exciting movie, or a prior confrontation, that residual arousal can be misattributed to a new provocation, making an aggressive response more intense than it otherwise would be. The person genuinely believes their rage is entirely caused by the current provocation, unaware that their body was already primed by something else entirely.

Example

A person finishes an intense workout and is still physiologically aroused (elevated heart rate, adrenaline). Shortly after, another driver honks at them. The residual arousal from the workout transfers to the new situation, amplifying their anger response beyond what the provocation alone would produce. Clinically, this principle helps explain why couples who engage in high-conflict discussions immediately after returning from stressful workdays may escalate more rapidly than they would otherwise — the day's accumulated arousal pours into the disagreement.

The Weapons Effect

Berkowitz's revision of the frustration-aggression hypothesis emphasized the role of environmental cues in triggering aggression. Perhaps his most striking demonstration of this principle involved an everyday object that many people encounter without a second thought: a gun sitting on a table. The mere sight of it, Berkowitz argued, could pull aggression out of people — even when they weren't especially frustrated.

Weapons Effect (Berkowitz & LePage, 1967)

The mere presence of weapons in the environment increases aggressive behavior. In the original study, participants who had been angered delivered more electric shocks when a gun was visible in the room than when a neutral object (such as a badminton racquet) was present. Berkowitz summarized this as: "The finger pulls the trigger, but the trigger may also be pulling the finger." Weapons serve as aggressive cues that prime aggressive cognitions and behaviors. This finding has been replicated across multiple cultures and methodologies, underscoring that the physical environment shapes aggressive behavior in ways people rarely recognize.

The Catharsis Hypothesis

Many people — and some well-meaning therapists — believe that the best way to deal with anger is to "get it out of your system." Punch a pillow. Scream into a void. Watch an action movie. This intuition feels right, which is precisely what makes the research findings so important for clinical practice. The catharsis hypothesis suggests that expressing aggression — or engaging in aggressive activities like watching violent media, punching a pillow, or venting anger — reduces the urge to aggress by releasing pent-up aggressive energy. This idea has roots in Freudian hydraulic models of emotion, which conceptualized aggression as a pressurized force that builds up and must be released.

Research Verdict: Largely Debunked

Extensive research has shown that catharsis does not reduce aggression and often increases it. Venting anger, hitting objects, or engaging in aggressive fantasies tends to maintain or escalate arousal and aggressive feelings rather than dissipate them. Bushman (2002) found that participants who hit a punching bag while thinking about someone who angered them were more aggressive afterward, not less. This is an important counterintuitive finding for the EPPP. In clinical practice, this means that interventions encouraging clients to "vent" their anger through aggressive activities — hitting pillows, screaming, or aggressive role-play — may paradoxically make anger problems worse. Evidence-based anger management focuses instead on cognitive reappraisal, relaxation techniques, and de-escalation strategies.

Biological Factors in Aggression

While social learning, frustration, and environmental cues all shape aggressive behavior from the outside in, biology shapes it from the inside out. Aggression is not purely a psychological phenomenon — it is rooted in neurochemistry, hormones, and brain structures. Understanding these biological substrates is essential for the EPPP and for clinical work with clients whose aggression may have neurological or endocrine contributions. Multiple biological systems contribute to aggressive behavior:

  • Testosterone: Higher levels are correlated with increased aggression, dominance-seeking, and status competition, though the relationship is bidirectional (winning also raises testosterone). This means testosterone is both a cause and consequence of dominance behavior, making simple causal claims misleading.
  • Serotonin: Low serotonin levels are associated with impulsive aggression (hostile/reactive type). The serotonin deficiency hypothesis suggests that low serotonergic activity reduces inhibition of aggressive impulses. This is why SSRIs are sometimes used in the management of impulsive aggression — by boosting serotonin availability, they may restore the brain's braking system on aggressive impulses.
  • Amygdala: Central to threat detection and emotional arousal; stimulation of the amygdala can trigger aggressive responses. Lesions may reduce aggressive behavior. The amygdala essentially serves as the brain's alarm system, rapidly evaluating whether a stimulus is threatening and mobilizing a defensive or offensive response.
  • Prefrontal cortex: Responsible for impulse control and behavioral regulation. Damage or reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex (particularly the orbitofrontal region) is associated with disinhibited aggression. Think of the prefrontal cortex as the brain's executive who can override the amygdala's alarm — when this executive is offline (due to injury, intoxication, or developmental immaturity), aggressive impulses go unchecked.

For exam purposes, remember the key pairing: testosterone up and serotonin down is the hormonal/neurochemical profile most strongly associated with aggression. And at the neural level, aggression often reflects an imbalance between an overactive amygdala (too much threat reactivity) and an underactive prefrontal cortex (too little inhibition).

Cultural Factors in Aggression

Aggression is not just a product of individual biology and psychology — it is profoundly shaped by the cultural norms and values people absorb from their communities. What counts as a justified reason to fight, whether insults demand a physical response, and how aggressively people react to perceived disrespect all vary dramatically across cultures and even across regions within the same country.

Culture of Honor (Nisbett & Cohen, 1996)

Research by Nisbett and Cohen documented a culture of honor in the U.S. South, where insults — particularly those threatening a man's reputation — provoke stronger physiological and behavioral aggressive responses than in Northern U.S. samples. Southern participants who were insulted showed greater increases in cortisol and testosterone and were more likely to respond aggressively. This cultural pattern is linked historically to herding economies where protecting one's reputation and property was essential for survival. In herding cultures, a reputation for toughness deterred theft — allowing one's honor to be challenged without response could invite exploitation.

Media violence effects: Meta-analyses consistently show that exposure to media violence is associated with increased aggressive cognitions, affect, and behavior, and with decreased empathy and prosocial behavior. Effects are present in both short-term (priming) and long-term (desensitization, script development) contexts, though the magnitude of the effect is debated. The short-term pathway works through priming — violent images activate aggressive thoughts and feelings, much like the weapons effect. The long-term pathway operates through desensitization (repeated exposure dulls emotional reactivity to violence) and the development of aggressive cognitive scripts (mental templates for how to respond to provocation). For the EPPP, know that the relationship between media violence and aggression is real but modest in size, and that it operates through multiple mechanisms at different timescales.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Know the cognitive-affective-behavioral triad: stereotypes, prejudice, discrimination.
  • Social identity theory (Tajfel): In-group favoritism from need for positive self-esteem. Minimal group paradigm shows mere categorization suffices for bias.
  • Realistic conflict theory (Sherif): Competition generates hostility; superordinate goals reduce it.
  • Allport's contact hypothesis: Equal status, common goals, cooperation, institutional support.
  • Stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson): Awareness of stereotypes impairs performance via working memory drain. Relevant to testing validity.
  • Aversive racism: Unconscious bias in egalitarian people; emerges in ambiguous situations.
  • Microaggressions: Three types (microassaults, microinsults, microinvalidations) with cumulative psychological impact.
  • Jigsaw classroom (Aronson): Cooperative learning that creates interdependence.
  • Common In-Group Identity Model: Recategorization into shared group reduces bias.
  • Hostile vs. instrumental aggression: Hostile is emotional/reactive (harm is the goal); instrumental is calculated/proactive (harm is a means).
  • Frustration-aggression hypothesis (Dollard et al.): Frustration leads to aggression; Berkowitz's revision adds that frustration creates readiness but aggressive cues trigger the behavior.
  • Bandura's Bobo doll experiment: Aggression is learned through observation and modeling. Children imitated specific novel aggressive acts. Vicarious reinforcement modulates performance, not acquisition.
  • Excitation transfer (Zillmann): Residual arousal from one source intensifies aggression in a new situation.
  • Weapons effect (Berkowitz & LePage): Presence of weapons increases aggressive behavior — weapons serve as aggressive cues.
  • Catharsis hypothesis is debunked: Venting anger increases rather than decreases aggression.
  • Biological factors: Low serotonin = impulsive aggression; high testosterone = dominance; amygdala = threat response; prefrontal cortex = inhibition.
  • Culture of honor (Nisbett & Cohen): Insults provoke stronger aggressive responses in honor cultures.
