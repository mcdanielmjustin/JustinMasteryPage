Racial & Ethnic Identity Models

Chapter 10 introduced Phinney's universal model of ethnic identity development. Here we examine two group-specific models -- Cross's Nigrescence model for Black racial identity and Helms's model for White racial identity -- along with Cass's model for sexual orientation identity. Each traces a developmental arc from unawareness through exploration to integration, but the catalysts and challenges differ markedly depending on which group the individual belongs to. Together, these models equip clinicians to assess identity development in a culturally specific way.

Cross's Nigrescence Model (Black Racial Identity)

Cross's Model (1971, revised 1991)

1. Pre-encounter: Internalization of dominant-culture values; low salience of race; may hold anti-Black attitudes.
2. Encounter: A significant experience (often involving racism) shatters the pre-encounter worldview and sparks exploration of racial identity.
3. Immersion-Emersion: Intense immersion in Black culture; may involve anti-White sentiments (immersion) followed by a more nuanced, less reactive stance (emersion).
4. Internalization: Secure, confident Black identity integrated with openness to other groups; activism may or may not be present.
5. Internalization-Commitment: Translation of internalized identity into sustained action toward social justice.

Helms' White Racial Identity Model

Helms' Model

Phase I — Abandonment of Racism:
Contact: Obliviousness to racial issues; colorblind stance.
Disintegration: Growing awareness of racial inequality creates moral dilemma and anxiety.
Reintegration: Resolution of anxiety through idealization of Whiteness and intolerance toward minorities.

Phase II — Defining a Non-Racist White Identity:
Pseudo-independence: Intellectual acknowledgment of White privilege; may still hold paternalistic attitudes.
Immersion-Emersion: Active exploration of what it means to be White; seeking accurate information about racism.
Autonomy: Internalized non-racist White identity; appreciation of cultural diversity; active commitment to anti-racism.

Cass's Sexual Orientation Identity Development Model

Vivienne Cass (1979) proposed one of the first and most influential models of sexual orientation identity development. The model describes six stages through which individuals progressively integrate their sexual orientation into their overall sense of identity:

Cass's Six Stages (1979)

1. Identity Confusion: The individual first recognizes that their thoughts, feelings, or behaviors could be labeled as homosexual. This creates confusion and a sense of inner turmoil ("Could this apply to me?").
2. Identity Comparison: The individual tentatively accepts the possibility of a non-heterosexual identity and begins to explore what this means. Feelings of alienation from heterosexual peers may emerge as the individual recognizes differences ("I may be different from others").
3. Identity Tolerance: The individual acknowledges that they are likely gay or lesbian and begins seeking out the LGBTQ+ community for social and emotional support. The identity is tolerated rather than fully embraced ("I probably am gay").
4. Identity Acceptance: The individual accepts (rather than merely tolerates) their sexual orientation and develops increasing contact with the LGBTQ+ community. Selective disclosure to trusted others begins.
5. Identity Pride: The individual immerses in LGBTQ+ culture and may develop an "us vs. them" stance, with strong pride in the in-group and devaluation of heterosexual norms. Activism and confrontation of homophobia may be prominent.
6. Identity Synthesis: Sexual orientation becomes integrated into the individual's overall identity rather than being the primary defining characteristic. The rigid us/them dichotomy softens, and the individual views their sexual orientation as one important but not all-encompassing aspect of who they are.

Clinical Application

Not all individuals progress through every stage, and some may remain in earlier stages due to environmental constraints, cultural factors, or personal choice. The model is not rigidly linear -- individuals may revisit stages across the lifespan, particularly in response to new social contexts or life transitions. Clinicians should assess where a client falls in this process to inform culturally responsive treatment, recognizing that different stages carry different therapeutic needs (e.g., identity confusion may require exploration and normalization, while identity pride may call for support of activism alongside exploration of relational flexibility).

The ADDRESSING Framework

Cross, Helms, and Cass each focus on a single dimension of identity -- race or sexual orientation. But real clients arrive carrying multiple, overlapping identities simultaneously. Consider a 72-year-old Latina woman who presents with depression following a stroke that left her with limited mobility. She is a devout Catholic, grew up in rural Mexico, and lives on a fixed income. If her therapist focuses only on her ethnic identity, they may miss the ways that aging, acquired disability, socioeconomic stress, religious coping, immigration history, and gender all converge to shape her experience of depression — and her expectations for treatment. Effective multicultural practice demands a wider lens, one that systematically accounts for the many dimensions of identity that influence how a person moves through the world.

This is precisely why Pamela Hays developed a practical mnemonic tool that encourages clinicians to think beyond any single identity category. Rather than relying on intuition or defaulting to the most visible aspect of a client's background, the ADDRESSING Framework provides a structured checklist of ten cultural factors that clinicians should consider during assessment, case conceptualization, and treatment planning. The acronym itself serves as a cognitive prompt — each letter pointing to a dimension of identity that may carry privilege, marginalization, or both, depending on the client's social context.

ADDRESSING Framework (Hays, 2001)

Pamela Hays developed the ADDRESSING framework to help clinicians systematically consider the multiple cultural influences that shape each client's identity. The acronym maps onto ten dimensions, each of which may significantly affect a client's worldview, access to resources, experiences of discrimination, and therapeutic needs:
A — Age and generational influences
D — Developmental or other Disability
D — Disability acquired later in life
R — Religion and spiritual orientation
E — Ethnic and racial identity
S — Socioeconomic status
S — Sexual orientation
I — Indigenous heritage
N — National origin
G — Gender

Notice how the framework captures dimensions that might otherwise be invisible in a standard intake. A clinician who asks about ethnicity may never think to ask how a client's generational cohort shapes their attitudes toward mental health. A therapist working with a young man presenting with anxiety might overlook the role of his Indigenous heritage or the impact of immigrating as a child if the framework's prompts aren't used. The power of the ADDRESSING model lies in its comprehensiveness: it ensures that no single identity dimension dominates the clinical picture while others go unexplored.

Each letter in the acronym also invites the clinician to reflect on their own positionality. For example, a clinician who has never experienced disability may hold unconscious assumptions about what life with a disability entails. A therapist from a high socioeconomic background may underestimate how profoundly financial insecurity shapes a client's capacity to follow through on treatment recommendations. By walking through each dimension for both the client and themselves, clinicians can identify potential blind spots, power differentials, and areas where cultural humility is especially needed.

From a practical standpoint, the ADDRESSING framework is most useful when applied early — during intake, treatment planning, and supervision discussions — rather than invoked only after a cultural misunderstanding has already occurred. Think of it as a preventive tool: just as a medical checklist reduces the likelihood of surgical errors, the ADDRESSING framework reduces the likelihood of cultural oversights that can erode the therapeutic alliance, lead to misdiagnosis, or cause premature termination.

Clinical Application

The ADDRESSING framework reminds clinicians to go beyond race and ethnicity and consider the full range of cultural identities that shape a client's experience. Each dimension represents both potential areas of privilege and potential areas of marginalization. For instance, a middle-aged gay man with no disabilities may hold privilege along some dimensions (age, ability status) while experiencing marginalization along others (sexual orientation, and potentially religion if he was raised in a faith tradition that condemns homosexuality). Using this framework systematically during intake and case conceptualization reduces the risk of overlooking important cultural factors. It also encourages clinicians to consider intersectionality — the way multiple identity dimensions interact to produce unique experiences of advantage or disadvantage that cannot be understood by examining any single dimension in isolation.

As we turn next to barriers to treatment and health disparities, keep the ADDRESSING framework in mind. Many of the systemic obstacles that prevent individuals from accessing or benefiting from mental health services map directly onto the dimensions Hays identified — age, disability, socioeconomic status, national origin, and other factors that determine who gets help, who doesn't, and why.

Barriers to Treatment & Health Disparities

Consider a Spanish-speaking mother experiencing severe postpartum depression. She knows something is wrong — the persistent sadness, the inability to bond with her newborn, the intrusive thoughts that terrify her. She musters the courage to call her insurance company's behavioral health line, only to learn that no Spanish-speaking therapists are available within a 50-mile radius. She's offered an interpreter, but the idea of disclosing her darkest thoughts through a stranger feels unbearable. She hangs up and doesn't call back. Six months later, she arrives at the emergency room in crisis. This scenario — repeated in countless variations across communities every day — illustrates why understanding barriers to treatment is not an academic exercise but a matter of clinical urgency and, often, life or death.

As we discussed in the preceding section, the ADDRESSING framework maps the many dimensions of identity that shape a person's experience. Those same dimensions — age, disability status, socioeconomic class, national origin, and others — also predict who encounters obstacles when seeking mental health care. The gap between needing help and receiving effective help is not random; it follows predictable fault lines of race, ethnicity, language, income, and historical experience. Racial and ethnic minorities, in particular, face a constellation of barriers that compound one another, creating disparities that persist even when services technically exist. Understanding these barriers is essential for any clinician who wants to do more than treat the patients who happen to make it through the door.

The major barriers to accessing and benefiting from mental health services can be organized into several overlapping categories, each of which deserves careful attention:

  • Language barriers: Lack of linguistically matched providers; loss of nuance through interpreters
  • Cultural stigma: Many cultures view mental illness as shameful or as a sign of personal weakness
  • Historical mistrust: Legacies of unethical treatment (e.g., Tuskegee syphilis study, forced sterilization) create distrust of healthcare systems
  • Socioeconomic barriers: Lack of insurance, transportation, childcare; limited access to providers in underserved areas
  • Clinician bias: Implicit biases can affect diagnosis, treatment recommendations, and the therapeutic relationship

Notice how these barriers interact and amplify one another. A low-income immigrant client may face language barriers, cultural stigma, historical mistrust of institutions, and lack of insurance simultaneously. The compounding effect is not merely additive — each barrier makes the others harder to overcome. A client who distrusts healthcare systems is less likely to persist through a frustrating search for a bilingual provider. A client already struggling to afford transportation is more likely to discontinue treatment after a culturally insensitive encounter. This is why addressing disparities requires systemic thinking, not just individual goodwill.

Language barriers deserve special emphasis because they affect every stage of clinical contact. Even when interpreters are available, critical therapeutic nuances — the precise emotional coloring of a word, the cultural connotations of an idiom of distress, the implicit meaning carried by a pause or a euphemism — can be lost in translation. Research suggests that clients communicating through interpreters disclose less, feel less understood, and are more likely to terminate treatment prematurely. The therapeutic relationship, which depends so heavily on felt attunement, is fundamentally altered when a third party mediates every exchange.

Historical mistrust, too, warrants more than a passing mention. The Tuskegee syphilis study — in which Black men were deliberately denied treatment for syphilis for decades so researchers could observe the disease's progression — is often cited as a singular atrocity. But it was not singular. Forced sterilization programs targeted Native American, Latina, and Black women well into the 1970s. Psychiatric institutions were used to confine and control people of color for behaviors that challenged racial hierarchies. These are not distant historical footnotes; they are living memories in many communities, passed down through families and reinforced by ongoing experiences of discrimination within healthcare settings. When a client from one of these communities hesitates to trust a clinician, that hesitation is not pathological — it is rational and historically informed.

Example: Diagnostic Disparities

Research consistently shows that African Americans are more likely to be diagnosed with schizophrenia and less likely to be diagnosed with mood disorders compared to White Americans with identical symptoms. This diagnostic disparity likely reflects clinician bias, cultural differences in symptom expression, and the impact of stereotype-driven clinical judgment. In one landmark study, clinicians were given identical clinical vignettes in which only the patient's race was varied; African American patients were significantly more likely to receive a schizophrenia diagnosis. Such findings underscore that clinician bias is not hypothetical — it is measurable, consequential, and directly harmful to the people we are ethically obligated to serve.

Culturally Adapted Interventions

If the barriers outlined above describe the problem, culturally adapted interventions represent one important part of the solution. A common misconception among clinicians-in-training is that cultural adaptation means abandoning evidence-based practice — that you must choose between scientific rigor and cultural responsiveness. In reality, the research tells a different story. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate that culturally adapted treatments produce better outcomes for ethnic minority clients than their unadapted counterparts, and these adaptations need not compromise the core mechanisms of change that make the treatments effective in the first place.

Evidence-based treatments can be culturally adapted without sacrificing their empirical foundation. Adaptations may include: incorporating cultural values and metaphors that resonate with the client's worldview, modifying the language and delivery format to be linguistically accessible, involving family members in treatment (consistent with collectivist values that prioritize family harmony and interdependence), directly addressing acculturative stress as a treatment target, and integrating traditional healing practices alongside Western approaches. For example, a cognitive-behavioral therapy protocol for depression might be adapted for Latino clients by incorporating the concept of familismo — the deep value placed on family loyalty and closeness — into behavioral activation goals, framing increased social engagement not as an individual self-care strategy but as a way of fulfilling family obligations and strengthening family bonds.

The key principle is that effective adaptation preserves the active ingredients of a treatment while modifying its surface structure — the examples used, the language spoken, the metaphors invoked, the people included in the room — to fit the client's cultural context. This requires clinicians to possess genuine cultural knowledge, not just good intentions. It also requires humility: the willingness to ask clients what feels relevant and meaningful to them, rather than assuming that a textbook description of their culture applies to their individual experience.

Clinical Application

Microaggressions in therapy — such as a clinician assuming a client's problems stem from their culture, expressing surprise at a client's articulateness, or denying the relevance of race ("I don't see color") — can severely damage the therapeutic alliance. These moments may seem small to the clinician but land with cumulative weight for the client, who has likely experienced hundreds of similar slights across a lifetime. Clinicians must engage in ongoing self-reflection, seek consultation, and be willing to acknowledge and repair ruptures caused by cultural insensitivity. A rupture that is named, explored, and genuinely repaired can actually strengthen the alliance — but only if the clinician resists the impulse to become defensive and instead approaches the moment with curiosity and accountability.

Social Justice in Psychology

The barriers and disparities documented above make clear that individual clinical skill, however refined, is insufficient to close the gaps in mental health care. Even the most culturally competent clinician working in a system riddled with structural inequities will see only incremental improvement for her individual clients while the broader patterns persist. This realization has driven a fundamental shift in how the profession conceptualizes its responsibilities. The multicultural movement in psychology has increasingly emphasized that competence requires not just individual-level intervention but also advocacy and social justice work. Psychologists are called upon to address systemic barriers, advocate for equitable policies, and challenge institutional structures that perpetuate disparities.

The APA Multicultural Guidelines explicitly state that psychologists should "strive to use organizational change processes to support culturally informed policy development and practices." This represents a shift from viewing cultural competence as solely an individual clinical skill to recognizing it as a professional and ethical obligation with systemic implications.
