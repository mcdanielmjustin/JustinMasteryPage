Prosocial Behavior & Prejudice

Altruism, bystander effect, stereotypes, discrimination
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Prosocial Behavior

Prosocial behavior encompasses any action intended to benefit others, including helping, sharing, cooperating, and comforting. Understanding why people help (or fail to help) has been a central concern of social psychology since the 1960s.

Altruism vs. Egoism

Altruism

Helping behavior motivated by genuine concern for the welfare of others, without expectation of personal benefit. Batson's (1991) empathy-altruism hypothesis proposes that empathic concern for a person in need produces truly altruistic motivation. When we feel empathy, we are motivated to help regardless of whether we can escape the situation.

Egoistic Alternatives

Several theories propose that apparently altruistic behavior is actually motivated by self-interest: (1) Negative state relief model (Cialdini): we help to reduce our own distress at witnessing someone's suffering. (2) Social exchange theory: we help when the perceived rewards outweigh the costs. (3) Reciprocal altruism (Trivers): we help because we expect reciprocation in the future.

Empirical research supports the idea that genuinely altruistic motivations have distinct behavioral consequences compared with egoistic ones. In a study examining charitable responses following the September 11, 2001 attacks, Piferi, Jobe, and Jones found that donors reported a range of motivations for giving money and providing other forms of assistance to victims. These motives included self-focused reasons (e.g., feeling better about oneself, social pressure) as well as other-focused reasons centered on improving victims' well-being. Critically, the altruistic motivation — the desire to genuinely enhance the welfare of those affected — was the strongest predictor of continued donations and sustained helping over time. This finding is consistent with Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis: when helping is driven by authentic concern for others rather than by self-oriented benefits, it tends to be more durable and less contingent on situational incentives or emotional relief.

The Bystander Effect

Having explored the motivations behind prosocial behavior — from genuine empathy-driven altruism to more self-focused reasons for helping — we now confront a deeply unsettling paradox: even when people care about others' welfare, they often fail to act. The most surprising part? The presence of other caring people can actually make things worse. Consider a scene that has become one of psychology's most haunting case studies: In 1964, a young woman named Kitty Genovese was attacked and ultimately murdered near her apartment in Queens, New York. Early reports suggested that dozens of neighbors witnessed or heard portions of the assault, yet no one intervened or called the police in time. While later investigations revealed the original account was somewhat exaggerated, the case ignited a firestorm of public soul-searching and launched an entire research program into a phenomenon that would come to be called the bystander effect — the finding that individuals are less likely to help in an emergency when other bystanders are present.

This effect is counterintuitive precisely because it defies our expectations. Surely, we think, more witnesses should mean a greater chance that someone will step in. But the pioneering research of social psychologists Bibb Latané and John Darley told a different story — one in which the mere knowledge that others are present can paralyze each individual's impulse to act. Their work revealed that the bystander effect is not about apathy or moral failure; it is about predictable psychological processes that unfold when humans find themselves in ambiguous, high-stakes situations surrounded by other people. Understanding these processes is essential not only for the EPPP but for anyone who wants to design interventions — in schools, workplaces, and public spaces — that actually increase the likelihood of helping.

Latane and Darley's Decision Model of Helping

Rather than treating the failure to help as a single moral lapse, Latané and Darley (1970) recognized that helping in an emergency is actually the end result of a complex chain of decisions. They proposed a five-step sequential model in which a potential helper must successfully pass through each stage — like clearing a series of hurdles — before assistance is rendered. Failure at any single step results in no helping, which means there are multiple psychological "exit ramps" that lead a well-meaning person to remain passive. Think of it as a decision tree: at every branch point, something can go wrong.

  1. Notice the event: The bystander must first perceive that something is happening. This may sound trivial, but in a busy urban environment — with headphones in, eyes on a phone, or attention consumed by the day's worries — people routinely fail to register events occurring right in front of them. A person slumped on a subway bench might be sleeping, resting, or in medical distress, and a distracted passerby may never even register the scene.
  2. Interpret as emergency: The bystander must interpret the situation as one requiring help. This is where one of the most insidious group-level processes enters the picture. Pluralistic ignorance — looking to others for cues and misinterpreting their inaction as evidence that no emergency exists — can block this step. Imagine you hear a loud crash from an adjacent room while sitting in a waiting area with several other people. You glance around; everyone else appears calm, perhaps slightly puzzled but not alarmed. You think, "If it were serious, someone would be reacting." But everyone else is thinking the same thing about you. The group collectively talks itself out of recognizing the emergency — not through words, but through shared inaction that each person reads as reassurance.
  3. Assume responsibility: Even when a bystander correctly identifies an emergency, they must feel personally responsible for helping. Here, diffusion of responsibility — the assumption that someone else will help — reduces the sense of personal obligation as the number of bystanders increases. When you are the only person who witnesses someone collapse on a hiking trail, the full weight of moral responsibility falls squarely on your shoulders. But in a crowded shopping mall, it is easy to think, "Surely someone with more training, or someone closer, or someone who saw it more clearly, will handle this." Each bystander diffuses their sense of obligation across the group, and the tragic result can be that no one acts at all.
  4. Know how to help: The bystander must have the knowledge or skills to provide assistance. A person who witnesses a choking incident but has never learned the Heimlich maneuver may freeze — not from indifference, but from genuine uncertainty about what to do. This step highlights why training programs (such as CPR certification or psychological first aid courses) can meaningfully increase helping rates.
  5. Decide to help: The bystander must weigh the costs and benefits of intervening and decide to act. Even a knowledgeable, responsible bystander might hesitate if the perceived costs are high — fear of physical danger, embarrassment if the situation turns out to be benign, or legal liability. A clinician who encounters an apparent domestic dispute in a parking lot, for example, might weigh the risk of escalation against the urgency of the victim's need before deciding whether and how to intervene.

The elegance of this model lies in its explanatory power: it shows that non-helping is not a single phenomenon but a family of failures, each with a different psychological mechanism. This has direct implications for intervention design — if you want to increase helping, you need to know which step is breaking down so you can target the right process.

Key Experiment: Seizure Study (Darley & Latane, 1968)

In one of the most elegant demonstrations of diffusion of responsibility, Darley and Latané placed participants in what they believed were discussion groups communicating via intercom. During the conversation, a confederate (an actor working with the researchers) began showing signs of having a seizure — gasping, calling for help, and then falling silent. The critical manipulation was simple: participants were led to believe they were either the only listener, one of two listeners, or one of six listeners. When participants believed they were the only one who could hear the emergency, a striking 85% rushed to help. But when they believed four others were also listening, only 31% helped — and those who did help took significantly longer to respond. This was not a case of cruel indifference; many of the non-helpers appeared visibly distressed and agitated. They cared — but the psychological weight of knowing others were present was enough to keep them in their seats. This study demonstrated diffusion of responsibility directly: more bystanders led to less individual helping, even when helping required nothing more than leaving the room to alert the experimenter.

While diffusion of responsibility is best known for its role in bystander situations, it is also the construct most useful for explaining social loafing — the phenomenon in which individuals reduce their effort when working collectively on a task. In group work settings, each member may assume that others are contributing sufficiently, leading to a diminished sense of personal accountability. Because responsibility for the group's output feels shared among all members, no single individual feels compelled to exert maximum effort. This parallels the bystander dynamic: just as the presence of multiple witnesses dilutes the felt obligation to intervene, the presence of multiple contributors dilutes the felt obligation to work hard. Strategies that counteract social loafing — such as making individual contributions identifiable or assigning specific roles — work precisely because they restore personal responsibility and eliminate the diffusion effect.

Factors That Increase Helping

If the bystander effect paints a somewhat bleak picture of human behavior in groups, the research also offers a more hopeful complement: a well-documented set of conditions that reliably increase the likelihood that someone will step forward. Knowing these factors is clinically and practically relevant — they inform everything from how emergency scenes are managed to how community programs are designed to encourage prosocial behavior. As you review this list, notice how many of these factors work by strengthening one or more of the steps in Latané and Darley's decision model.

  • Clarity of emergency: Unambiguous emergencies produce more helping. When a situation is clearly dangerous — visible bleeding, screaming, a car flipped on its roof — bystanders are far less susceptible to pluralistic ignorance because there is little room for alternative interpretations. This is why safety training often emphasizes using direct, explicit language ("Someone call 911 — this person is not breathing!") rather than vague appeals for help.
  • Perceived similarity: People help more when the victim is perceived as similar to themselves. Shared group membership — whether based on ethnicity, university affiliation, or even something as trivial as wearing the same sports jersey — activates empathic concern and a sense of in-group obligation.
  • Being alone: Lone bystanders help more than those in groups, precisely because diffusion of responsibility cannot occur when there is no one else to diffuse it to. If you are the only person on the street when someone falls, the responsibility is unambiguously yours.
  • Feeling competent: People with relevant skills (e.g., medical training) help more. An off-duty paramedic who witnesses a cardiac arrest is far more likely to intervene than a bystander with no medical knowledge — not just because they can help, but because their training has given them a clear script for action.
  • Good mood: Positive mood states increase helping (Isen & Levin, 1972). In a now-classic study, people who had just found a dime in a phone booth coin return were significantly more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers. Good moods appear to broaden attention, increase feelings of social connection, and lower the perceived costs of helping.
  • Rural vs. urban: Helping rates tend to be higher in rural settings, possibly due to less stimulus overload. City dwellers are constantly bombarded with competing demands on their attention and may develop what Stanley Milgram called "psychic overload" — a habitual filtering of social cues that, while adaptive for daily functioning, can inadvertently screen out genuine emergencies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that helping is not simply a matter of character — it is profoundly shaped by the situation. The same person who walks past a stranger in distress on a crowded city sidewalk might be the first to rush to that stranger's aid on an isolated country road. For the EPPP, remember that the bystander effect and its moderators illustrate one of social psychology's most enduring lessons: situational forces often exert far more influence on behavior than we intuitively expect.

Stereotypes, Prejudice, and Discrimination

Having explored how the presence of others can diffuse our sense of personal responsibility — whether in emergencies or group work — we now turn to one of the most consequential topics in social psychology: how people think about, feel toward, and act upon their perceptions of social groups. Consider a hiring manager who, upon reviewing a résumé with an unfamiliar ethnic name, unconsciously assumes the applicant is less qualified. She may not feel any hostility toward the applicant's group, and she may not ultimately reject the candidate. Yet a generalized belief has already shaped her initial perception. Now imagine a different manager who does harbor resentment toward that group, and a third who actually discards the résumé. These three responses — the generalized belief, the negative feeling, and the discriminatory action — are related but psychologically distinct processes. Understanding their differences is essential for psychologists who assess intergroup dynamics, design interventions, and serve diverse clients.

Social psychologists have long organized intergroup attitudes into three components that map neatly onto the classic tripartite model of attitudes: cognition, affect, and behavior. Stereotypes represent the cognitive component — what we believe about a group. Prejudice captures the affective component — how we feel about a group. And Discrimination reflects the behavioral component — how we act toward members of that group. Critically, these three do not always travel together. A person may hold a stereotype without feeling prejudice, or feel prejudice without acting on it. Recognizing which component is operating — and under what conditions each converts into the others — is central to both research and clinical practice.

Stereotypes (Cognitive)

Stereotypes are generalized beliefs about the characteristics of members of a social group. Functioning as cognitive schemas, stereotypes simplify an overwhelmingly complex social world by allowing us to quickly categorize people and predict their behavior. They can be positive (e.g., "Members of group X are hardworking") or negative (e.g., "Members of group Y are aggressive"), and research consistently shows they are activated automatically — often within milliseconds — upon encountering a group member. Once activated, stereotypes act as a perceptual filter, shaping what we notice, how we interpret ambiguous behavior, what we remember, and how we make judgments. For instance, the same assertive behavior might be remembered as "confident leadership" when performed by a member of one group but "aggressive pushiness" when performed by a member of another.

Prejudice (Affective)

Prejudice is a negative attitude toward members of a social group, based solely on their group membership. Unlike the coolly cognitive nature of stereotypes, prejudice carries an emotional charge — hostility, contempt, fear, disgust, or even a patronizing warmth that masks underlying disrespect. Gordon Allport (1954), in his landmark work The Nature of Prejudice, defined it as "an antipathy based upon a faulty and inflexible generalization." The key features are its rigidity and its basis in group membership rather than individual characteristics. A clinician might encounter prejudice when a client expresses blanket distrust of an entire cultural group after a single negative interaction — the emotion generalizes far beyond what the evidence warrants.

Discrimination (Behavioral)

Discrimination refers to unjustified negative behavior toward members of a group based on their group membership. It is the behavioral expression of prejudice — the point at which internal attitudes translate into real-world consequences. Discrimination can operate at the individual level, as when a landlord refuses to rent to someone because of their ethnicity, or at the institutional level, where policies and practices embedded in social institutions systematically disadvantage certain groups even without conscious intent. Understanding this distinction is critical, because institutional discrimination can persist long after individual-level prejudice declines, perpetuating inequities through structures rather than personal animus.

Theories of Prejudice

If prejudice were simply a character flaw of a few bad actors, it would be far less pervasive than it is. The reality is that prejudice emerges from multiple psychological processes — existential anxiety, social identity needs, intergroup competition, frustrated aggression, personality dynamics, and cultural learning. Each major theory of prejudice illuminates a different piece of this puzzle, and together they explain why prejudice is so remarkably persistent across cultures and historical periods. For the EPPP, understanding these theories means recognizing not just their key claims but also the types of prejudice each is best positioned to explain.

Terror Management Theory and Prejudice

Why would simply thinking about death make someone more hostile toward people who are different from them? The answer lies in one of social psychology's most provocative frameworks. Terror Management Theory (TMT), developed by Greenberg, Pyszczynski, and Solomon (1986), is rooted in the existential ideas of cultural anthropologist Ernest Becker. The theory begins with a profound observation: humans are the only species aware of their own inevitable mortality. This awareness creates the potential for paralyzing, existential anxiety — what Becker called "the terror of death." To manage this mortality salience, people invest deeply in cultural worldviews — shared belief systems that provide meaning, order, and a sense of symbolic or literal immortality (through legacies, religious beliefs, national identities, or enduring creative works). Self-esteem, in this framework, functions as an anxiety buffer because it signals that one is successfully living up to the standards of one's valued cultural worldview — that one matters within a meaning system that transcends individual death.

Here is where prejudice enters the picture. When mortality is made psychologically salient — whether through experimental reminders of death or real-world events such as terrorist attacks, pandemics, or natural disasters — individuals show a pronounced increase in worldview defense. This manifests as stronger favoritism toward those who share one's cultural values and beliefs (ingroup members) and heightened hostility, derogation, or prejudice toward those who hold different worldviews (outgroup members). The psychological logic is revealing: outgroup members are threatening precisely because their differing beliefs, practices, and values implicitly challenge the validity of one's own anxiety-buffering worldview. If another group lives by completely different rules and thrives, what does that say about the "truth" of my own system? This implicit challenge destabilizes the very structure that keeps existential terror at bay.

A large body of experimental research supports these predictions with remarkable consistency. In the typical mortality salience paradigm, participants who write about their own death subsequently evaluate ingroup members more positively and outgroup members more negatively compared to control participants who write about a neutral or mildly aversive topic (such as dental pain). These effects have been replicated across dozens of cultures and extend well beyond simple attitude ratings — they include increased nationalism, harsher punishment recommendations for moral transgressors, greater physical aggression toward worldview-threatening others, and even reluctance to use cultural symbols (like flags) in inappropriate ways. The breadth of these findings underscores that TMT is not merely about death per se, but about how existential vulnerability amplifies the fundamental human tendency to protect meaning systems through intergroup bias.

Example

In a classic study, municipal court judges who were reminded of their own mortality set significantly higher bail amounts for an alleged prostitute — a person who violated their moral worldview — compared to judges in a control condition. The average bail amount jumped from $50 to $455, a ninefold increase driven solely by a brief mortality reminder. Similarly, American participants primed with death-related thoughts showed increased preference for a pro-American author and increased dislike of an anti-American author, relative to controls. These findings powerfully illustrate how existential threat amplifies both ingroup solidarity and outgroup prejudice, often without the individual's conscious awareness that death anxiety is driving their judgments.

Clinical Note

TMT has important implications for understanding prejudice spikes following collective threats such as pandemics, wars, and terrorist events — periods when mortality is salient for entire populations. Clinicians working with clients experiencing grief, life-threatening illness, or trauma should be aware that heightened mortality awareness can intensify intergroup biases and ideological rigidity, sometimes manifesting as sudden hostility toward groups the client previously tolerated. Therapeutic approaches that help clients develop a secure sense of meaning and self-worth — whether through existential therapy, values exploration, or meaning-making interventions — may reduce the need for defensive worldview protection and the prejudice it fuels. In group therapy settings, facilitators should be especially attuned to how shared experiences of loss or threat can paradoxically increase divisiveness along cultural or ideological lines.

Beyond existential concerns, several other major theories explain why prejudice develops and persists. The table below summarizes the key frameworks you are most likely to encounter on the EPPP. Notice how each theory locates the root cause of prejudice in a different psychological process — identity needs, resource competition, displaced frustration, personality structure, or social learning — and consider how these mechanisms might interact in real-world settings. For example, an economic recession (Realistic Conflict Theory) may heighten frustration (Scapegoat Theory) while simultaneously threatening cultural worldviews (TMT), creating a perfect storm for escalating prejudice.

Each of these theories captures something genuine about the origins of prejudice, and no single framework tells the whole story. Social Identity Theory explains why even arbitrary group divisions produce bias; Realistic Conflict Theory accounts for the intensification of prejudice when groups compete for jobs, territory, or political power; Scapegoat Theory illuminates why disadvantaged outgroups bear the brunt of collective frustration during hard times; the Authoritarian Personality approach highlights individual differences in susceptibility to prejudiced thinking; and Social Learning Theory reminds us that prejudice is culturally transmitted — children absorb biases from parents, peers, and media long before they can critically evaluate them. In the next section, we turn to one of psychology's most influential ideas about how to reduce prejudice: the Contact Hypothesis.

To operationalize the construct of the authoritarian personality, Adorno and colleagues developed the F Scale (where "F" stands for Fascism). This self-report instrument was designed to measure an individual's susceptibility to antidemocratic and authoritarian ideologies without directly referencing any specific ethnic or racial group. The scale assesses a cluster of personality tendencies including conventionalism (rigid adherence to middle-class values), authoritarian submission (uncritical deference to idealized authority figures), authoritarian aggression (hostility toward those who violate conventional norms), anti-intraception (opposition to introspection and psychological-mindedness), superstition and stereotypy, preoccupation with power and toughness, destructiveness and cynicism, projectivity (believing that dangerous forces operate in the world), and exaggerated concern with sexual behavior. Although the F Scale was groundbreaking in linking personality dynamics to political attitudes and prejudice, it has been criticized for acquiescence bias — all items are worded in the authoritarian direction, meaning that individuals with a tendency to agree with any statement will score artificially high. Subsequent researchers, notably Bob Altemeyer, refined the construct through the Right-Wing Authoritarianism (RWA) Scale, which addressed several psychometric shortcomings of the original measure while focusing on three core dimensions: authoritarian submission, authoritarian aggression, and conventionalism.

Contact Hypothesis

Allport's (1954) contact hypothesis proposes that intergroup contact can reduce prejudice, but only under specific conditions:

  1. Equal status: Members of both groups must have equal status in the contact situation.
  2. Common goals: The groups must work toward shared objectives.
  3. Intergroup cooperation: Achieving the goals must require cooperation rather than competition.
  4. Support of authorities: The contact must be endorsed by institutional authorities, laws, or customs.

Pettigrew and Tropp's (2006) meta-analysis of over 500 studies confirmed that intergroup contact generally reduces prejudice, even when all four conditions are not met, though the effect is stronger when conditions are optimal. The mechanism appears to involve increased empathy and reduced anxiety about the outgroup.

Robbers Cave Experiment (Sherif et al., 1961)

In this classic field experiment, two groups of boys at summer camp developed intense intergroup hostility through competitive activities. Simple contact between the groups did not reduce prejudice. Only when the researchers introduced superordinate goals -- goals that required cooperation between both groups -- did prejudice decrease. This supports both realistic conflict theory and the contact hypothesis.

Implicit Bias

Consider a therapist who genuinely values diversity, has completed cultural competency training, and consciously rejects racial stereotypes — yet during an intake assessment, she finds herself rating a Black male client's risk of aggression as higher than an identically presenting White male client's risk. She would be horrified to learn this, and she might not even recognize the discrepancy if it were pointed out. How can someone who sincerely holds egalitarian beliefs behave in ways that contradict those beliefs? The answer lies in a phenomenon that has reshaped how psychologists understand prejudice: implicit bias.

We have just explored how intergroup contact — particularly under conditions of equal status and shared goals — can reduce explicit prejudice. But decades of research now reveal that prejudice operates on two distinct tracks. One track is conscious, deliberate, and accessible to self-report; the other runs beneath awareness, influencing our snap judgments, our body language, and even our clinical decisions without our knowledge or consent. Understanding this second track is essential for any psychologist who wants to practice ethically in a diverse society.

Implicit bias refers to unconscious attitudes and stereotypes that influence judgment and behavior outside of conscious awareness. Unlike explicit prejudice — the kind a person might openly endorse or at least recognize in themselves — implicit biases operate automatically and may directly conflict with a person's stated egalitarian values. A physician who consciously believes all patients deserve equal care may still unconsciously associate certain racial groups with noncompliance. A teacher who champions equity may still unconsciously call on boys more frequently than girls. The hallmark of implicit bias is precisely this gap between what we intend and what we do, between the values we hold consciously and the associations that fire automatically in our minds.

Implicit Association Test (IAT)

If implicit biases are, by definition, outside conscious awareness, how can we measure them? You cannot simply ask people to report attitudes they do not know they hold. This measurement challenge led to one of the most widely discussed tools in modern social psychology. The IAT (Greenwald, McGhee, & Schwartz, 1998) measures implicit attitudes by exploiting a simple principle: the mind is faster at pairing concepts that are already strongly associated. In a typical race-focused IAT, participants rapidly sort stimuli — Black faces, White faces, pleasant words, unpleasant words — using keyboard keys. When the task pairs Black faces with "good" words on the same key (and White faces with "bad" words on the other), most participants slow down. When the pairings are reversed — Black faces with "bad," White faces with "good" — responses speed up. That difference in reaction time provides the IAT score, a window into the automatic associations operating below the surface of conscious thought.

Research consistently shows that most Americans, regardless of their own racial identity, show some degree of implicit pro-White bias on the IAT. This finding underscores a crucial point: implicit biases are not simply a marker of personal bigotry. They reflect the cultural environment in which we are embedded — the media images, historical narratives, and social patterns we absorb from childhood onward. A Black participant who shows some pro-White bias on the IAT is not necessarily harboring self-hatred; they may instead be reflecting the evaluative associations that pervade American culture.

Beyond the IAT, researchers have used more ecologically valid paradigms to study how implicit bias affects real-world decisions. One influential line of research involves the shooter bias paradigm, in which participants must rapidly decide whether to shoot or not shoot targets who are holding either a weapon or a harmless object (e.g., a cell phone or wallet). Imagine sitting at a computer screen as images flash before you: a person in a park holding something in their hand. You have less than a second to press "shoot" if it is a gun or "don't shoot" if it is a cell phone. The paradigm is designed to simulate the split-second decisions that police officers face, and the results are sobering. Across multiple studies, participants — both civilians and law enforcement officers — show a pattern of faster decisions to shoot armed Black targets and slower decisions to refrain from shooting unarmed Black targets, compared to White targets in identical conditions.

A particularly noteworthy study by Correll and colleagues (2007) examined both trained police officers and civilian community members using this paradigm. The findings revealed an important dissociation between speed and accuracy — a distinction that carries significant implications for how we understand the relationship between implicit attitudes and overt behavior. Police officers demonstrated shooter bias in their response times — they were quicker to correctly shoot armed Black targets and slower to correctly withhold fire on unarmed Black targets. However, officers did not show racial bias in their accuracy: they were no more likely to mistakenly shoot an unarmed Black target than an unarmed White target. Civilian participants, by contrast, showed racial bias in both speed and accuracy — they not only responded more slowly but also made more errors, disproportionately "shooting" unarmed Black targets. This pattern suggests that law enforcement training may help officers override the influence of stereotypes on the final shoot/don't-shoot decision, even though the underlying implicit associations still affect the speed of information processing. In other words, the bias is present in the cognitive machinery, but training creates a checkpoint that can catch and correct the biased impulse before it becomes a biased action.

Clinical Note

The Correll et al. findings illustrate a distinction frequently relevant in applied settings: implicit bias may influence the efficiency of cognitive processing (reaction time) without necessarily determining the behavioral outcome (accuracy). This dissociation has implications for how psychologists conceptualize and measure bias, suggesting that training and structured decision protocols can attenuate the behavioral expression of implicit stereotypes even when the stereotypes themselves remain active at an automatic level. For clinicians, this is both cautionary and hopeful — cautionary because automatic biases persist despite good intentions, but hopeful because deliberate, structured practices can serve as effective safeguards against biased outcomes.

Criticisms and Limitations

Despite its enormous influence, the IAT and the broader concept of implicit bias have drawn significant — and legitimate — criticism. No tool that claims to reveal hidden prejudice should escape rigorous scrutiny, and several important limitations have emerged as the research literature has matured.

  • Moderate test-retest reliability raises questions about whether the IAT measures a stable trait or fluctuating state.
  • The relationship between IAT scores and discriminatory behavior is modest (meta-analytic r approximately .24).
  • Debate about whether the IAT measures personal attitudes, cultural knowledge, or both.
  • Implicit bias training programs have shown limited long-term effectiveness in changing behavior.

The test-retest reliability concern is particularly important for the EPPP: if a person takes the IAT on Monday and again on Friday and receives meaningfully different scores, what exactly is being measured? A stable dispositional tendency, or a momentary fluctuation influenced by context, mood, or recent experiences? The modest correlation with actual discriminatory behavior (r ≈ .24) further complicates matters. While this correlation is not trivial — it is roughly comparable to many well-established psychological predictors — it does mean that a high IAT score is a poor predictor of any individual's behavior in a specific situation. Additionally, some researchers argue that the IAT may capture knowledge of cultural stereotypes rather than personal endorsement of those stereotypes. You can know that society associates certain groups with certain traits without personally holding those associations as your own attitudes. These debates remain active and unresolved, making it essential for psychologists to interpret IAT results — and implicit bias research more broadly — with nuance and appropriate caution.

Clinical Implications

Psychologists should be aware of their own implicit biases that may affect clinical judgment, including diagnosis, treatment planning, and the therapeutic relationship. Research shows that clinicians may inadvertently make different clinical decisions based on patient race — for example, underdiagnosing PTSD in Black clients or overdiagnosing psychotic disorders in the same population. A clinician who never consciously thinks a prejudiced thought may nonetheless show a pattern of differential diagnosis that mirrors cultural stereotypes. Self-awareness, ongoing education, and structured clinical decision-making — such as using standardized assessment tools and decision rubrics rather than relying solely on clinical intuition — can help mitigate the influence of implicit bias. Just as the Correll study showed that police training could uncouple implicit associations from behavioral outcomes, structured clinical practices can serve a similar protective function in therapeutic settings.

Reducing Prejudice: Additional Approaches

Beyond the contact hypothesis, several additional strategies for reducing prejudice have received empirical support.

Jigsaw Classroom (Aronson et al., 1978)

Description

The jigsaw classroom is a cooperative learning technique that satisfies all four conditions of the contact hypothesis within an educational setting. Each student in a small group is assigned a unique piece of material that they must teach to the other group members. Because every student's contribution is essential for the group to learn the complete material, interdependence and equal status are built into the structure. Research shows that the jigsaw technique reduces prejudice, increases empathy, improves self-esteem, and enhances academic performance among minority students.

Recategorization and Common Ingroup Identity

Gaertner and Dovidio's (2000) Common Ingroup Identity Model proposes that prejudice can be reduced by encouraging members of different groups to recategorize themselves as members of a single, superordinate group. When former outgroup members are recategorized as ingroup members, they benefit from ingroup favoritism rather than suffering from outgroup derogation.

Infrahumanization

Infrahumanization refers to the tendency to attribute full humanity — particularly the capacity for complex, uniquely human emotions such as love, hope, contempt, and remorse — to one's own group while denying these same qualities to outgroups. As described by Leyens and colleagues (2000), this bias involves both ingroup favoritism and outgroup derogation. Ingroup members are seen as capable of experiencing nuanced "secondary" emotions that distinguish humans from animals, whereas outgroup members are perceived as limited to basic "primary" emotions (e.g., fear, anger, pleasure) shared across species.

Infrahumanization is distinct from explicit dehumanization in that it does not require conscious hostility or overt hatred. Individuals engaging in infrahumanization may hold no overtly negative attitudes toward the outgroup yet still subtly deny outgroup members the full depth of emotional experience they readily attribute to their own group. This bias has been demonstrated across numerous intergroup contexts and appears to operate even when groups are not in active conflict. The effect has significant implications for intergroup empathy: if outgroup members are perceived as incapable of complex suffering, observers feel less moral obligation to respond to their distress.

Clinical Note

Infrahumanization can operate in therapeutic settings when clinicians unconsciously perceive clients from certain cultural or ethnic backgrounds as having less emotional complexity. This may lead to oversimplified case conceptualizations or reduced empathic attunement. Awareness of this subtle bias is an important component of culturally responsive practice.

Stereotype Threat

Definition

Stereotype threat (Steele & Aronson, 1995) is the risk of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group, which creates anxiety that impairs performance. African American students performed worse on a verbal test when it was described as diagnostic of intellectual ability (activating the stereotype) than when it was described as a non-diagnostic problem-solving exercise. Stereotype threat has been demonstrated across many groups and domains: women in math, older adults on memory tests, and White men on athletic tasks.

Reducing Stereotype Threat

  • Reframing the task as non-diagnostic of ability
  • Encouraging a growth mindset (intelligence is malleable)
  • Self-affirmation exercises before the task
  • Emphasizing that the test is fair across groups
  • Providing role models who have succeeded despite stereotypes

EPPP Exam Tip

Stereotype threat is a commonly tested EPPP topic. Remember that it affects performance (not actual ability), occurs when a negative stereotype is made salient, and can be mitigated by reframing the situation. Also know the distinction between stereotype threat (fear of confirming a stereotype) and self-fulfilling prophecy (behaving in ways that confirm others' expectations). Both can impair performance, but through different mechanisms.

Aggression

Although aggression is sometimes covered separately, it is closely related to prejudice and prosocial behavior as it represents the opposite end of the social behavior spectrum.

Culture of Honor

A set of social norms prevalent in certain regions or communities in which threats to one's reputation — particularly regarding strength, loyalty, or masculinity — demand aggressive retaliation. Individuals socialized within a culture of honor perceive insults and challenges as requiring a forceful response to maintain social standing.

The culture of honor framework, developed by Nisbett and Cohen (1996), provides a sociocultural explanation for regional differences in violence within the United States. Historically, the American South was settled by herding populations (primarily Scotch-Irish immigrants) whose livelihoods depended on livestock that could be easily stolen. In such economies, cultivating a reputation for toughness and swift retaliation served as a deterrent against theft. Over generations, these norms became deeply embedded in Southern social fabric, persisting long after the economic conditions that originally gave rise to them had changed.

Empirical research has demonstrated that homicide rates among White males are significantly higher in Southern states than in Northern states, particularly for argument-related and honor-related killings rather than felony-related homicides. In laboratory studies, Cohen, Nisbett, Bowdle, and Schwarz (1996) found that Southerners who were insulted showed greater increases in cortisol and testosterone levels, were more likely to interpret subsequent ambiguous behavior as aggressive, and responded with more dominant and aggressive behavior compared to their Northern counterparts. Northerners who were insulted showed little change on these measures.

Example

In a well-known experiment, male participants were bumped and called a vulgar name by a confederate in a narrow hallway. Southern participants showed elevated stress hormones, angrier facial expressions, and more physically assertive behavior in subsequent interactions. Northern participants were relatively unaffected by the insult. These findings illustrate how culturally transmitted norms about reputation and masculinity shape physiological and behavioral responses to perceived threats.

The culture of honor perspective complements biological and social learning accounts of aggression by highlighting how macro-level cultural norms interact with individual-level processes. It underscores that aggression is not simply a product of personality traits or situational frustrations but is also shaped by the values and expectations of one's sociocultural environment.

Theories of Aggression

  • Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis (Dollard et al., 1939): Frustration always leads to some form of aggression, and aggression is always preceded by frustration. This original formulation has been revised -- frustration increases the readiness for aggression but does not always produce it.
  • Social Learning Theory (Bandura, 1973): Aggression is learned through observation and reinforcement. The Bobo doll experiments demonstrated that children imitated aggressive models, especially when the model was rewarded.
  • Excitation Transfer Theory (Zillmann, 1971): Residual physiological arousal from one source can be misattributed to a new stimulus, intensifying emotional and aggressive responses.
  • Cognitive Neoassociation Theory (Berkowitz, 1989): Aversive events (not just frustration) produce negative affect, which activates aggressive thoughts, memories, and motor responses through associative networks.

Clinical Integration

Understanding prosocial behavior and prejudice is essential for culturally competent practice. Clinicians must recognize how stereotypes, implicit bias, and systemic discrimination affect their clients' mental health and their own clinical judgment. Knowledge of the bystander effect informs community interventions for violence prevention, while understanding prosocial motivations can inform group therapy and community-building strategies.

Modern Forms of Prejudice

In the preceding section, we examined aggression and the broader landscape of prosocial behavior and prejudice — including how stereotypes, implicit bias, and systemic discrimination shape both client well-being and clinical judgment. But here's a puzzle worth sitting with: survey after survey shows that explicit endorsement of racist and sexist attitudes has declined dramatically over the past several decades. Does that mean prejudice is fading? Not exactly. What researchers have discovered is something more insidious — prejudice hasn't disappeared so much as it has gone underground, adopting subtler forms that are harder to detect, harder to confront, and in some ways harder to change.

Consider a hiring manager who genuinely believes in diversity and would never consciously discriminate. Yet when reviewing two equally qualified résumés — one with a traditionally White-sounding name and one with a traditionally Black-sounding name — she consistently rates the White candidate as "a slightly better fit." She can't articulate why; the preference feels like intuition, not bias. This scenario, replicated in numerous research studies, illustrates why understanding modern forms of prejudice is clinically and socially essential. As psychologists, we encounter these dynamics in our clients' lived experiences, in organizational consulting, and — if we're honest — in our own decision-making. The forms of prejudice described in this section are among the most frequently tested concepts on the EPPP precisely because they capture the gap between what people believe about themselves and how they actually behave.

Aversive Racism (Gaertner & Dovidio)

Imagine a White emergency room physician who has devoted her career to serving underserved communities. She considers herself deeply committed to racial equality. Yet research suggests that in ambiguous clinical situations — where a patient's symptoms could be interpreted as serious or benign — her diagnostic decisions may be subtly influenced by the patient's race, even though she would be genuinely horrified to learn this. This is the paradox at the heart of one of the most important theories in modern prejudice research.

Definition

Aversive racism characterizes individuals who consciously endorse egalitarian values and believe they are non-prejudiced, but who harbor unconscious negative feelings and stereotypes. These individuals do not discriminate in situations where the normatively correct behavior is clear (helping is obviously appropriate), but they do discriminate in ambiguous situations where bias can be attributed to non-racial factors. For example, an aversive racist would help a Black person whose car has broken down on a deserted road (the social norm to help is unambiguous), but might fail to intervene in a crowded setting where responsibility is diffuse — and attribute the inaction to the presence of other bystanders rather than to racial bias.

What makes aversive racism so clinically relevant — and so difficult to address — is that the individuals exhibiting it are not being disingenuous. They truly believe in equality and would score low on explicit measures of prejudice. Their bias emerges only when the situation provides plausible non-racial explanations for discriminatory behavior. Gaertner and Dovidio's extensive experimental work demonstrated this pattern repeatedly: when norms were clear, aversive racists behaved in perfectly egalitarian ways; when the situation was ambiguous, their behavior shifted in racially biased directions. For clinicians, this framework is a powerful reminder that good intentions do not inoculate anyone — including therapists — against the influence of implicit attitudes. It also helps explain why clients from marginalized groups may report experiencing discrimination that the perpetrators would sincerely deny.

Symbolic/Modern Racism

While aversive racism operates largely outside conscious awareness, another modern form of prejudice hides in plain sight — wrapped in the language of political values and personal responsibility. Symbolic racism (Sears, 1988) combines anti-Black affect with conservative political values. It is expressed through opposition to policies perceived as giving unfair advantages to minorities, belief that discrimination is no longer a significant barrier, and resentment of perceived special treatment. Unlike old-fashioned racism, symbolic racism does not endorse biological inferiority but attributes racial inequality to cultural deficiencies or lack of effort.

A person expressing symbolic racism might say something like, "I'm not racist — I just think everyone should succeed on their own merits, and these special programs aren't fair to anyone." On the surface, this sounds like a principled stance about fairness. What Sears and colleagues demonstrated, however, is that these attitudes are not simply abstract political philosophies — they are reliably predicted by racial affect and racial stereotypes, even after controlling for political ideology. In clinical settings, symbolic racism can surface in a therapist's case conceptualization: a clinician might attribute a Black client's unemployment to "motivational issues" while framing an identical presentation in a White client as "contextual stressors." Recognizing symbolic racism helps clinicians interrogate whether their clinical judgments are truly race-neutral or subtly colored by culturally embedded assumptions about effort and deservingness.

Ambivalent Sexism (Glick & Fiske, 1996)

Prejudice doesn't always look hostile. Sometimes it arrives with a smile, a compliment, and an offer to carry your bags — and that is precisely what makes it so effective at maintaining inequality. Ambivalent sexism comprises two components: hostile sexism (antipathy toward women who challenge traditional gender roles) and benevolent sexism (seemingly positive attitudes that idealize women in traditional roles, e.g., "women should be cherished and protected"). Benevolent sexism, despite its positive tone, reinforces gender inequality by rewarding women who conform to traditional roles and undermining their agency and competence.

Consider a clinical supervisor who tells a female trainee, "You're so naturally warm and nurturing — you'd be perfect for working with children," while steering her away from forensic or neuropsychological work. The comment feels flattering, even supportive. But Glick and Fiske's research reveals that benevolent sexism and hostile sexism work as a coordinated system: benevolent sexism rewards women who stay within prescribed roles, while hostile sexism punishes those who step outside them. Cross-cultural research using the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory has found this pattern across more than twenty countries, suggesting it is not a culturally specific phenomenon. For the EPPP, remember that benevolent sexism is particularly insidious because recipients often fail to recognize it as prejudice — and may even find it appealing — while it quietly constrains their aspirations and self-concept. In therapy, both male and female clients may internalize benevolent sexist beliefs without recognizing their impact on relationship dynamics, career decisions, and self-worth.

Empathy and Prosocial Development

Picture a newborn in a hospital nursery who begins to wail the moment another infant starts crying — not because anything has happened to them, but seemingly in resonance with another's distress. Now fast-forward fifteen years: a teenager reads a news story about refugee children separated from their families and feels a deep, aching sadness — not for any single child they have met, but for an entire group enduring chronic hardship. Something profound has happened in the intervening years. The raw, undifferentiated emotional contagion of infancy has been transformed into a sophisticated capacity to understand, share, and respond to the suffering of others. That transformation is the story of empathy development, and it sits at the heart of why humans help one another at all.

Having explored the darker side of social cognition — prejudice, stereotyping, and discrimination — we now turn to one of the most powerful forces that counteract these tendencies: empathy. Empathy is considered a key motivator of prosocial behavior and an important developmental achievement, one that shapes everything from a toddler's earliest attempts at comforting a friend to a clinician's ability to form a therapeutic alliance. Understanding how empathy unfolds across development is essential not only for the EPPP, but for any psychologist who works with children, assesses social-emotional functioning, or designs interventions to promote helping behavior.

Hoffman's Stages of Empathy Development

How does a child move from reflexive crying in a nursery to genuine concern for the welfare of strangers? Developmental psychologist Martin Hoffman (2000) offered one of the most influential answers to this question, proposing that empathy unfolds through a predictable series of stages that track closely with the child's growing cognitive sophistication — particularly their emerging ability to distinguish self from other and to take another person's perspective. Rather than viewing empathy as a single skill that either exists or doesn't, Hoffman showed that it is a capacity that deepens and broadens across childhood, becoming increasingly nuanced as cognitive development permits.

  1. Global empathy (newborn): Reactive crying in response to another infant's distress. No differentiation between self and other. The newborn experiences another's pain almost as if it were their own — a phenomenon sometimes called emotional contagion. The baby in the nursery who cries when a neighboring infant cries is not "choosing" to feel distress; they are flooded by it, lacking the cognitive architecture to separate "my feelings" from "your feelings."
  2. Egocentric empathy (1 year): Awareness that another person is in distress, but the child offers comfort that they themselves would find soothing (e.g., bringing their own mother to comfort another child). This stage represents a crucial leap: the child now recognizes that the distress belongs to someone else. However, their response remains filtered through their own preferences. A one-year-old who hands their beloved stuffed bear to a crying adult is demonstrating genuine concern — but also revealing the limits of their perspective-taking ability.
  3. Empathy for another's feelings (2-3 years): Growing awareness that others' feelings may differ from one's own. Ability to respond more appropriately to another's specific emotional state. At this stage, a child might notice that a sad friend wants a hug rather than a toy — an important shift from projecting one's own needs onto others to genuinely reading and responding to what the other person is experiencing. This stage aligns with the broader emergence of theory of mind.
  4. Empathy for another's life condition (late childhood): Empathy extends beyond immediate situations to encompass another person's general life condition. Can feel empathy for groups of people experiencing chronic hardship. A ten-year-old who feels moved by the plight of people experiencing homelessness — not just a single person they pass on the street, but the broader social condition — has reached Hoffman's most mature stage. This abstract, generalized empathy draws on advanced cognitive abilities like abstract reasoning and the capacity to imagine experiences far removed from one's own.

Notice the elegant trajectory: from global, undifferentiated emotional flooding → to self-other distinction with egocentric helping → to accurate emotional perspective-taking → to abstract empathy for unseen others. Each stage builds on the cognitive gains of the previous one. For the EPPP, it is especially important to remember that the stages are tied to cognitive development, not simply to age, and that the earliest stage involves no self-other differentiation whatsoever.

Clinical Integration

Hoffman's model is relevant to developmental assessments and to understanding prosocial deficits in conditions such as conduct disorder and antisocial personality disorder, where empathy development may be impaired. When a clinician evaluates a child who shows no distress at a peer's pain, Hoffman's framework helps locate where the developmental process may have stalled — is the child failing to recognize the other's distress (a stage-two deficit), or can they recognize it but fail to respond appropriately (a later-stage issue)? This distinction has direct treatment implications. Interventions that build empathy skills (perspective-taking exercises, social-emotional learning programs) draw on this developmental framework. For example, programs like "Roots of Empathy," which brings infants into classrooms so children can observe and discuss the baby's emotional states, are essentially scaffolding movement through Hoffman's stages. In group therapy, fostering empathy among group members is a key therapeutic mechanism — Yalom identified it as one of the core therapeutic factors — and a therapist who understands Hoffman's model can better appreciate why some clients struggle to move beyond egocentric responses to genuine perspective-taking within the group.
