Conformity

Chapter 5 showed how persuasion operates through messages, messengers, and cognitive processing routes. But not all social influence travels through arguments and evidence -- some of the most powerful pressure comes not from what people say but from what they silently expect. In 1955, a teenager named Emmett Till was murdered in Mississippi after allegedly violating a social norm. Whatever the full story, the tragedy underscored a truth that social psychologists were simultaneously documenting in laboratories: the pressure to conform to group expectations is one of the most powerful forces in human behavior, capable of overriding individual judgment, moral conviction, and even self-preservation. This chapter examines three landmark research programs -- Asch on conformity, Milgram on obedience, and Zimbardo on social roles -- that collectively reveal just how susceptible ordinary people are to situational pressures.

Asch's Line Studies (1951, 1956)

Solomon Asch designed an elegantly simple experiment to test whether people would deny the evidence of their own eyes under group pressure. Participants judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard line — an objectively easy task with an obviously correct answer. The twist was that the participant was surrounded by confederates who, on critical trials, unanimously gave the same wrong answer. The participant had to decide: trust their own perception or go along with the group.

The results were startling. Approximately 75% of participants conformed at least once across the 12 critical trials, and the overall conformity rate across all trials was about 37%. One in four participants never conformed, demonstrating that individual differences in susceptibility to social pressure are real — but the majority yielded to a unanimous, obviously incorrect group at least some of the time.

Two Types of Social Influence

Normative influence: Conforming to be liked and accepted by the group (going along to get along). Driven by the desire for social approval. Produces public compliance without private acceptance.
Informational influence: Conforming because we believe the group has better information than we do. Driven by the desire to be correct. Produces private acceptance (true attitude change).

Example: Normative vs. Informational Influence in Practice

A medical resident who agrees with an attending physician's questionable diagnosis during rounds — despite privately disagreeing — is yielding to normative influence (fear of professional consequences). A first-year graduate student who changes her theoretical orientation after hearing persuasive arguments from more experienced peers is yielding to informational influence (she genuinely believes they know better). The distinction matters clinically: normative conformity can be undone by changing the social context, while informational conformity requires new evidence or arguments.

Key findings from Asch's research on factors that affect conformity:

  • Conformity increases with group size — but only up to about 3–4 members; beyond that, additional members add little
  • Conformity drops sharply when there is even one dissenter (ally), even if the dissenter gives a different wrong answer
  • Conformity increases when the task is more difficult or ambiguous (shifting from normative to informational influence)
  • Conformity increases when responses are public (vs. private written responses)
  • Conformity varies by culture — meta-analyses show higher conformity in collectivist cultures, where group harmony is valued

Obedience: Milgram's Studies (1963)

If Asch showed that people will deny the evidence of their eyes to fit in with a group, Stanley Milgram asked an even more disturbing question: Will ordinary people inflict serious harm on an innocent person simply because an authority figure tells them to? The answer, replicated across cultures and decades, shook the foundations of how psychologists understood human morality.

Milgram's Obedience Paradigm

Stanley Milgram recruited participants through newspaper advertisements for a study ostensibly on "the effects of punishment on learning." Each participant was assigned the role of "teacher" and instructed to administer electric shocks of increasing intensity (15V to 450V, labeled from "Slight Shock" to "XXX") to a confederate "learner" each time the learner gave a wrong answer on a memory task. The shocks were not real, but participants believed they were. In the baseline condition, 65% of participants administered the maximum 450-volt shock, despite hearing pre-recorded screams, pleas to stop, complaints of a heart condition, and eventually ominous silence from the learner.

Milgram conducted over 20 variations of the experiment, systematically manipulating situational variables to identify the conditions that increase or decrease obedience. These variations constitute some of the most elegant parametric research in social psychology:

Factors That Reduced Obedience in Milgram's Variations

Obedience decreased when: the learner was in the same room (40% obeyed) or had to be physically touched (30%), another "teacher" refused to continue (only 10% obeyed — the single most powerful manipulation), the experimenter gave orders by telephone rather than in person, or the study was conducted in a less prestigious setting (a run-down office building vs. Yale University). Obedience increased when the participant played only an indirect role (e.g., reading word pairs while another person delivered the shock — 93% obeyed).

Clinical & Ethical Note: Obedience in Therapeutic Settings

Milgram's findings carry direct implications for clinical practice. The power differential inherent in the therapist-client relationship creates conditions structurally similar to those in Milgram's paradigm: an authority figure (therapist) directs a less powerful person (client) to engage in behaviors they might otherwise resist. Ethical practitioners must remain vigilant about this dynamic — particularly with highly compliant or authority-deferential clients from high power-distance cultures — ensuring that therapeutic directives serve the client's welfare rather than exploiting the authority gradient. Milgram's study also transformed research ethics: participants' significant distress contributed directly to the development of stronger ethical guidelines, including the APA's requirement for informed consent and debriefing (Standard 8.07).

Milgram, S. (1963). Behavioral study of obedience. Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 67(4), 371–378.

The Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971)

Milgram demonstrated that authority commands obedience; Philip Zimbardo asked whether social roles alone -- without explicit orders -- could produce equally dramatic behavioral shifts. He randomly assigned psychologically healthy male college students to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a simulated prison at Stanford, with no pre-existing personality differences between the two groups. Within 36 hours, guards began devising creative punishments and using solitary confinement; prisoners became passive, distressed, and showed signs of acute psychological breakdown. The planned two-week study was terminated after only 6 days. Zimbardo's conclusion -- that situational forces and assigned social roles can override individual dispositions -- extended Milgram's lesson from authority to role identity. (Chapter 9 revisits the SPE in the context of group development and role escalation.)

Example: Zimbardo's Relevance Beyond the Lab

When the Abu Ghraib prisoner abuse scandal emerged in 2004, Zimbardo testified as an expert witness, arguing that the abusive behavior of U.S. military guards was driven by the same situational forces he had documented decades earlier — deindividuation, role immersion, lack of oversight, and dehumanization of out-group members. This application illustrates why the SPE remains relevant: it reminds clinicians and forensic psychologists to look beyond individual character when explaining harmful behavior.

The study has faced significant methodological criticism, including demand characteristics (guards may have behaved as they believed the experimenters wanted), Zimbardo's conflicted dual role as researcher and "prison superintendent," and the fact that not all guards became abusive. Despite these limitations, it remains one of the most cited experiments in social psychology and is frequently tested on the EPPP.

Zimbardo, P. G. (1971). The power and pathology of imprisonment. Congressional Record (Serial No. 15, October 25, 1971).

Reactance Theory

A central contribution of McGuire's inoculation theory is its demonstration that a refutational defense is the most useful strategy for building resistance to persuasive attempts. In this approach, individuals are first presented with counterarguments that challenge their existing attitude and then given clear refutations that dismantle those challenges. This process serves two functions: it signals that one's beliefs may come under attack (creating a motivational readiness to defend) and it equips the person with practiced counterarguing responses. Compared to approaches that simply pile on additional supportive evidence for a held belief, the refutational method produces substantially greater resistance because it directly engages with the form of threat the person will later face. The practical implication is that the best way to protect attitudes is not to shield people from opposing viewpoints but to expose them to weakened versions of those viewpoints in a controlled manner.

Clinical Note

Inoculation principles have been applied in psychoeducation, relapse prevention, and health promotion. For instance, a therapist working with a client in recovery might role-play scenarios in which the client encounters social pressure to use substances, presenting mild challenges and helping the client rehearse effective refusal responses. This "cognitive rehearsal" functions as an inoculation—building the client's resistance to real-world persuasive pressure before it occurs.

Attitude Inoculation (McGuire, 1964)

Drawing an analogy to biological immunization, William McGuire proposed that people can be made resistant to persuasion by pre-exposing them to weakened forms of counterarguments along with refutations of those arguments. Just as a vaccine introduces a mild version of a pathogen to build immune defenses, attitude inoculation exposes individuals to a diluted attack on their existing beliefs and then provides them with the tools to counter it. This two-step process—threat plus refutation—prepares the person to defend against stronger persuasive attempts encountered later.

McGuire's research focused primarily on widely accepted beliefs (called cultural truisms) that people hold strongly but have rarely been called upon to defend—beliefs such as "it is good to brush your teeth after every meal." Because these attitudes have never been challenged, individuals lack practice in generating counterarguments, making them surprisingly vulnerable to persuasive attacks. The inoculation procedure remedies this by (1) alerting the person that their belief may be challenged, creating a motivational "threat," and (2) providing exposure to mild counterarguments together with their refutations, giving the person both the motivation and the cognitive ammunition to resist future persuasion.

Research has consistently shown that this refutational defense is more effective at building resistance than a supportive defense, which simply provides additional reasons to maintain the original attitude without addressing potential counterarguments. The refutational approach works because it activates deeper processing and rehearsal of defensive responses, whereas merely adding supportive bolstering leaves people unprepared for novel attacks on their position.

Example

A public health campaign aims to prevent adolescents from taking up smoking. Rather than only presenting reasons not to smoke (supportive defense), the inoculation approach first exposes teens to the kinds of weak pro-smoking arguments they might hear from peers—"smoking makes you look cool" or "everyone experiments"—and then walks them through why these arguments are flawed. When the teens later encounter real peer pressure, they have already practiced rejecting similar appeals, making them significantly more resistant to influence.

Psychological Reactance (Brehm, 1966)

When people perceive that their freedom to choose is being threatened or eliminated, they experience reactance — a motivational state directed toward restoring the threatened freedom. This often results in doing the opposite of what is demanded. The "forbidden fruit" effect and the "Romeo and Juliet effect" (parental opposition intensifying romantic attraction) are examples of reactance.

Clinical Application

Reactance is clinically relevant in working with resistant clients. Paradoxical interventions (prescribing the symptom) and motivational interviewing (rolling with resistance) are both strategies that leverage or reduce reactance. When a therapist says "You probably aren't ready to change yet," the client's reactance may motivate them to prove the therapist wrong.

Minority Influence (Moscovici)

The research reviewed so far might suggest that social influence flows in only one direction — from the powerful majority to the vulnerable individual. But Serge Moscovici's groundbreaking work demonstrated that minorities can also change the majority's mind, albeit through a different psychological pathway.

In his famous blue-green slide study (1969), Moscovici showed participants a series of clearly blue slides. When two confederates (a consistent minority) repeatedly called the slides "green," approximately 8% of majority participants also called the slides green — and, critically, subsequent color-perception tests revealed that even participants who never publicly agreed had shifted their private perception of ambiguous blue-green stimuli. The minority had produced genuine cognitive change below the surface of public agreement.

Moscovici identified consistency as the single most important factor in minority influence. A minority that wavers or contradicts itself loses credibility instantly. But a minority that maintains its position firmly and consistently over time forces the majority to engage in deeper processing — to genuinely grapple with the minority's arguments rather than dismissing them. This deeper processing (akin to central-route processing in the ELM) is what produces the durable, private attitude change that distinguishes minority influence from the superficial compliance that majority pressure typically elicits.

Clinical Application: Minority Influence in Group Therapy

In group therapy, a single member who consistently voices an unpopular but therapeutically important perspective — for example, challenging the group's tendency to avoid difficult emotions — can gradually shift the entire group's norms. The therapist's role is to protect the dissenter from being silenced while allowing the group to process the minority viewpoint, recognizing that the most valuable therapeutic insights often emerge from positions that initially make the group uncomfortable.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • ELM: Central route = deep processing, durable change; Peripheral route = superficial cues, temporary change. Know what determines which route is used (motivation and ability).
  • Sleeper effect: Low-credibility source gains persuasive power over time as source is forgotten.
  • Cialdini's 6 principles: Reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity. Be able to match techniques to principles.
  • Asch conformity: ~75% conformed at least once; one dissenter dramatically reduces conformity.
  • Normative vs. informational influence: Normative = acceptance by group; informational = accuracy.
  • Milgram obedience: 65% obeyed to maximum shock. Know factors that increase and decrease obedience.
  • Compliance techniques: Know foot-in-the-door (small then large), door-in-the-face (large then small), and lowball.
  • Reactance theory: Threatened freedom motivates opposing behavior. Relevant to paradoxical interventions in therapy.
  • Minority influence: Consistency is the key to a minority changing the majority's views.
