Cognitive restructuring & rational thinking
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Beck's Cognitive Therapy

Aaron T. Beck developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s, originally for depression. Beck proposed that psychological distress results not from events themselves but from individuals' interpretations of those events. The cognitive model holds that distorted thinking influences mood and behavior, and that modifying dysfunctional cognitions produces improvements in affect and functioning.

Levels of Cognition

Beck identified three levels of cognition: (1) Automatic thoughts -- spontaneous, rapid cognitions that arise in response to situations (e.g., "I'm going to fail"); (2) Intermediate beliefs -- conditional rules and assumptions (e.g., "If I don't get an A, I'm worthless"); and (3) Core beliefs (schemas) -- deep, fundamental beliefs about self, others, and the world (e.g., "I am incompetent"). Therapy works from the surface level inward.

Cognitive Distortions

Beck and his colleagues identified systematic errors in thinking called cognitive distortions. These are habitual patterns of biased information processing:

DistortionDefinitionExample
All-or-nothing thinkingViewing situations in only two categories"If I'm not perfect, I'm a total failure."
CatastrophizingPredicting the worst possible outcome"If I make a mistake, I'll be fired and lose everything."
Mind readingAssuming you know what others think"She thinks I'm boring."
OvergeneralizationDrawing broad conclusions from a single event"I failed this test; I'll never pass anything."
Selective abstractionFocusing on one detail while ignoring the big pictureDwelling on one critical comment despite many compliments
Magnification/minimizationInflating negatives or shrinking positivesMagnifying a mistake while minimizing achievements
PersonalizationAttributing external events to oneself without basis"My friend is upset; it must be something I did."
Emotional reasoningUsing feelings as evidence of truth"I feel like a fraud, so I must be one."

One additional distortion worth noting is arbitrary inference (also called "jumping to conclusions"), which involves drawing a specific conclusion in the absence of supporting evidence or even in the face of contradictory evidence. Arbitrary inference is a broader category that encompasses both mind reading and fortune telling, but it also applies when a person reaches a sweeping negative self-judgment based on minimal or ambiguous information. For example, a new instructor who concludes she must be "incredibly boring and incompetent" because two out of sixty-five students left the lecture early has drawn a conclusion that is not supported by the evidence — the students may have left for any number of reasons unrelated to the quality of instruction. This distortion differs from overgeneralization (which extends a single event to a broad pattern) and from selective abstraction (which focuses on one detail while ignoring the larger context). In arbitrary inference, the defining feature is that the conclusion itself lacks adequate evidentiary basis.

Example

A psychology instructor notices that 2 of her 65 students leave the lecture hall before class ends. She concludes, "I must be an incredibly boring and incompetent teacher." This represents arbitrary inference because she has jumped to a negative conclusion without sufficient evidence — the vast majority of students remained, and the two who left may have had appointments, felt ill, or had other reasons entirely unrelated to the quality of her teaching.

One cognitive distortion not always listed separately from overgeneralization is arbitrary inference (also called jumping to conclusions). This distortion involves reaching a specific conclusion without adequate supporting evidence — or even in the face of contradictory evidence. Arbitrary inference encompasses both mind reading and fortune telling, but it is broader: any conclusion drawn in the absence of facts qualifies. For example, a student named Anna who walks into class and immediately decides that her classmates already view her as unintelligent — despite having no actual evidence for this belief — is engaging in arbitrary inference. She has constructed a definitive interpretation out of thin air. Recognizing arbitrary inference in clinical vignettes requires checking whether the client's conclusion is supported by the available data; when it is not, this distortion is likely at play.

Arbitrary Inference

Drawing a specific conclusion from a situation when there is no evidence to support that conclusion, or when the available evidence actually contradicts it. This is one of the original cognitive distortions catalogued by Beck and is sometimes considered the umbrella term under which mind reading and fortune telling fall.

CBT Techniques

Core techniques in Beck's cognitive therapy include:

  • Socratic questioning -- Guided discovery through a series of questions that help the client examine evidence for and against their beliefs
  • Thought records -- Structured worksheets documenting the situation, automatic thought, emotion, evidence for/against, and alternative balanced thought
  • Behavioral experiments -- Testing predictions derived from dysfunctional beliefs in real-world situations
  • Activity scheduling -- Planning and monitoring daily activities to combat avoidance and increase mastery/pleasure
  • Downward arrow technique -- Probing beneath automatic thoughts to uncover intermediate and core beliefs

Imagery rescripting is a cognitive-behavioral technique designed to modify the content of involuntary, distressing mental images that are associated with a client's psychological difficulties. Many disorders—including PTSD, social anxiety, depression, and eating disorders—involve recurrent intrusive images (e.g., flashbacks to a traumatic scene, vivid images of social humiliation, or images tied to early adverse experiences). These images carry intense emotional charge and often maintain maladaptive beliefs and affective states.

In imagery rescripting, the therapist guides the client through a structured process of first vividly recalling the distressing image, then actively transforming elements of that image into a more adaptive, empowering version. For instance, a client who repeatedly visualizes a childhood scene of helplessness might be guided to reimagine the scene with their adult self intervening to protect the child. The goal is not to erase or suppress the original image but to create a competing mental representation that reduces the emotional impact and updates the meaning encoded in the imagery.

Unlike simple imaginal exposure, which relies on habituation through repeated confrontation with the feared image, imagery rescripting directly targets the meaning of the image. By altering the narrative embedded within the mental image, the technique facilitates changes in associated core beliefs and emotional responses. Research supports its efficacy across multiple conditions, and it is increasingly integrated alongside traditional verbal cognitive restructuring methods within CBT protocols.

Example

A client with social anxiety experiences intrusive images of an embarrassing childhood incident in which classmates laughed at her during a presentation. In imagery rescripting, the therapist first asks the client to describe the scene vividly, then guides her to reimagine the scenario: her adult self enters the classroom, comforts the younger version of herself, and assertively addresses the classmates. After several sessions, the intrusive image loses much of its emotional intensity, and the client's belief that she is "always humiliated in public" begins to shift.

Collaborative Empiricism

The therapeutic relationship in Beck's cognitive therapy is characterized by collaborative empiricism. In this framework, the therapist and client function as a team — sometimes described as "co-investigators" — who jointly examine the client's cognitions, test their validity against real-world evidence, and develop strategies for addressing identified problems. The therapist does not assume an authoritative or confrontational stance; instead, both parties contribute to setting treatment goals, designing behavioral experiments, and evaluating outcomes. The "empiricism" component reflects the emphasis on treating the client's beliefs as hypotheses to be tested rather than truths to be accepted or errors to be corrected. The therapist guides the client through structured inquiry (e.g., Socratic questioning) so the client discovers discrepancies between their automatic thoughts and available evidence. This collaborative stance enhances client engagement, fosters a sense of shared responsibility for therapeutic progress, and differentiates Beck's approach from more directive methods such as Ellis's REBT, where the therapist more actively confronts irrational beliefs.

Clinical Note

On the EPPP, collaborative empiricism is a frequently tested concept that distinguishes Beck's approach from other cognitive therapies. When a question stem describes a therapist who works with the client to gather evidence about a belief and jointly evaluate it, the best answer typically involves Beck's cognitive therapy. By contrast, a therapist who directly challenges or debates a client's belief is more consistent with Ellis's REBT.

Thought Record Example

Situation: Gave a presentation at work. Automatic Thought: "Everyone thought I was terrible." Emotion: Anxiety (85%). Evidence For: One person was on their phone. Evidence Against: Several people asked good questions; boss said "nice job." Balanced Thought: "Most people seemed engaged. One person being distracted doesn't mean I was terrible." Emotion after: Anxiety (35%).

Beck's Cognitive Triad of Depression

Beck proposed that depression involves a negative cognitive triad: negative views of (1) the self ("I'm worthless"), (2) the world ("Everything is terrible"), and (3) the future ("Things will never get better"). These negative schemas are activated by stressful events and maintained through systematic cognitive distortions.

Ellis's Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT)

Albert Ellis developed REBT (originally called Rational Emotive Therapy) in 1955, making it one of the earliest cognitive-behavioral approaches. REBT is based on the premise that emotional disturbance is largely caused by irrational beliefs about events, not by the events themselves.

The ABC Model

A (Activating event) -- The situation or trigger. B (Belief) -- The rational or irrational interpretation. C (Consequence) -- The emotional and behavioral result. Ellis added D (Disputing) -- Challenging the irrational belief, and E (Effective new belief) -- The rational replacement. The core insight: A does not cause C; B causes C.

Irrational Beliefs

Ellis identified several categories of irrational beliefs characterized by rigid, absolutistic demands (musts, shoulds, oughts):

  • Demandingness -- "I absolutely must perform well and win approval" / "Others must treat me fairly" / "Conditions must be comfortable"
  • Awfulizing -- "It's awful, horrible, and terrible when things don't go my way"
  • Low frustration tolerance (LFT) -- "I can't stand it" or "I can't bear this discomfort"
  • Global rating -- Condemning the total self, others, or life based on single instances

Beck vs. Ellis: Key Distinctions for the EPPP

While both are cognitive approaches, there are important differences. Ellis is more directive and confrontational, actively disputing irrational beliefs. Beck uses Socratic questioning and guided discovery. Ellis focuses on irrational beliefs (musts/shoulds); Beck focuses on automatic thoughts, intermediate beliefs, and schemas. Ellis's approach is more philosophical; Beck's is more empirical and research-driven. Both are considered founders of the cognitive-behavioral tradition.

Stress Inoculation Training (SIT)

Stress Inoculation Training was developed by Donald Meichenbaum as a cognitive-behavioral approach to help clients build resilience to stress by developing coping skills before, during, and after stressful events. SIT follows a medical inoculation analogy -- exposure to manageable doses of stress builds resistance to future stress.

Three Phases of SIT

  1. Conceptualization phase -- Educating the client about stress and its effects; identifying stressors and current coping strategies; establishing a collaborative relationship
  2. Skills acquisition and rehearsal phase -- Teaching coping skills (relaxation, cognitive restructuring, self-instructional training, problem-solving); practicing skills through imagery and role-play
  3. Application and follow-through phase -- Gradually applying skills to increasingly stressful situations; booster sessions for maintenance

Meichenbaum's Self-Instructional Training

Meichenbaum also developed self-instructional training (SIT), originally for impulsive children. The client learns to guide their own behavior through internal speech. The sequence follows Vygotsky's developmental model: (1) an adult model performs the task while talking aloud, (2) the child performs the task with the adult's verbal guidance, (3) the child performs while talking aloud to themselves, (4) the child whispers instructions, and (5) the child uses covert (internal) self-instructions. This approach bridges behavioral and cognitive interventions.

Problem-Solving Therapy

Problem-solving therapy (PST), developed by Thomas D'Zurilla and Marvin Goldfried, is a cognitive-behavioral intervention that teaches clients a structured approach to identifying and implementing effective solutions to everyday problems. PST is based on the premise that psychopathology is often related to ineffective problem-solving skills or a negative problem orientation.

Five Steps of Problem Solving

  1. Problem orientation -- Recognizing problems as normal and solvable; adopting an optimistic, rational approach rather than an avoidant or impulsive one
  2. Problem definition and formulation -- Clearly defining the problem in specific, concrete terms; identifying goals
  3. Generation of alternatives -- Brainstorming multiple possible solutions without evaluating them (quantity over quality initially)
  4. Decision making -- Evaluating each alternative based on feasibility and likely consequences; selecting the best option
  5. Solution implementation and verification -- Carrying out the chosen solution, monitoring the results, and adjusting if necessary

EPPP Focus: Commonly Tested Concepts

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale, integrates traditional cognitive therapy principles with mindfulness meditation practices adapted from Jon Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program. The central therapeutic objective of MBCT is to help clients disengage from ruminative cognitive patterns — habitual cycles of negative thinking, emotional reactivity, and maladaptive behavioral responses that perpetuate psychological distress.

MBCT was originally developed to prevent relapse in individuals with recurrent major depressive disorder. The underlying rationale draws on the differential activation hypothesis, which proposes that individuals who have experienced multiple depressive episodes develop increasingly accessible associations between sad mood and negative thinking patterns. A minor dip in mood can reactivate an entire depressive cognitive repertoire, triggering a full relapse. MBCT teaches clients to recognize these early warning signs and to relate to distressing thoughts and feelings as transient mental events rather than accurate reflections of reality. Through practices such as the body scan, sitting meditation, and the three-minute breathing space, clients develop a decentered, observational stance toward their inner experience.

Clinical Note

MBCT is typically delivered as an 8-week group-based program. Research has shown it is particularly effective for individuals who have experienced three or more prior depressive episodes, reducing relapse rates by approximately 50% compared to treatment as usual. For the EPPP, distinguish MBCT's emphasis on disengagement and decentering from Beck's emphasis on directly restructuring cognitive content and from ACT's focus on values-driven action.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy has demonstrated efficacy beyond traditional psychiatric and psychological disorders, extending into chronic medical conditions. Research on rheumatoid arthritis (RA) indicates that CBT used as an adjunctive intervention alongside standard medical treatment produces meaningful reductions in both perceived pain intensity and fatigue. These benefits appear to be most pronounced when CBT is introduced during the earlier stages of the disease course, before maladaptive cognitive and behavioral patterns become deeply entrenched. Patients with RA often develop catastrophizing thoughts about their pain, fear-avoidance behaviors, and helplessness cognitions that exacerbate symptom burden. CBT addresses these patterns through cognitive restructuring of pain-related beliefs, behavioral activation, pacing strategies, and relaxation training. The timing effect — with early intervention yielding stronger outcomes — is consistent with broader CBT principles suggesting that cognitive and behavioral patterns are more amenable to modification before they become chronic and self-reinforcing. This finding underscores the value of integrating psychological interventions into multidisciplinary care for autoimmune and inflammatory conditions from the point of diagnosis.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that CBT's application to rheumatoid arthritis targets pain and fatigue specifically, and that early implementation in the disease trajectory maximizes therapeutic gains. This represents an example of CBT's growing role in health psychology and behavioral medicine.

Dirty Discomfort in ACT

In Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), a distinction is drawn between clean discomfort and dirty discomfort. Clean discomfort refers to the natural pain that accompanies difficult life experiences — grief after loss, anxiety before a challenge, sadness during hardship. Dirty discomfort, by contrast, is the additional layer of suffering that arises from an individual's unwillingness to experience clean discomfort. When a person struggles against, avoids, or attempts to suppress normal emotional pain, this experiential avoidance paradoxically amplifies distress, creating a secondary form of suffering that is often more debilitating than the original pain.

For example, a person experiencing normal anxiety before a job interview (clean discomfort) may begin to catastrophize about the anxiety itself — thinking "I shouldn't feel this way" or "Something is wrong with me for being anxious." This resistance and self-judgment generates dirty discomfort that compounds the original experience. ACT practitioners help clients recognize that the unwillingness to sit with unavoidable distress is frequently the source of their greatest psychological difficulties. Rather than targeting the content of distressing thoughts or attempting to reduce the frequency of unpleasant emotions, ACT aims to increase willingness and acceptance, thereby reducing dirty discomfort while acknowledging that clean discomfort is an inherent part of human experience.

Example

A client grieving a relationship breakup (clean discomfort) begins drinking heavily to numb the sadness, then feels shame about the drinking, then avoids friends to hide the shame. Each layer of avoidance and unwillingness creates additional dirty discomfort far exceeding the original grief.

The Stanley/Brown Safety Planning Intervention

The Stanley/Brown Safety Planning Intervention, developed by Barbara Stanley and Gregory Brown, is a brief, structured clinical tool designed for individuals at elevated risk for suicide. Unlike a no-suicide contract (which simply asks a client to promise not to attempt suicide), the safety plan is a collaborative, action-oriented document that provides a hierarchical sequence of coping strategies the individual can use during a suicidal crisis. Research supports safety planning as an evidence-based intervention that reduces suicidal behavior and increases treatment engagement.

The safety plan consists of six sequential steps, each representing an escalating level of intervention:

Step Description
1. Recognizing warning signs Identifying the personal thoughts, feelings, moods, images, or situations that signal an approaching crisis
2. Internal coping strategies Using self-directed techniques to manage distress without contacting anyone (e.g., distraction, relaxation, physical activity)
3. Social contacts for distraction Reaching out to people or visiting social settings that provide a sense of normalcy and distraction from the crisis
4. Family members or friends Contacting specific trusted individuals who can offer support during a suicidal crisis
5. Mental health professionals and crisis services Contacting therapists, psychiatrists, crisis hotlines, or emergency services
6. Reducing access to lethal means Identifying and implementing strategies to limit availability of firearms, medications, and other means

Clinical Note

The Stanley/Brown Safety Plan is explicitly distinguished from "no-suicide contracts" (also called "contracts for safety"), which lack empirical support and are not considered best practice. No-suicide contracts simply involve an agreement not to engage in self-harm and do not equip the client with actionable coping strategies. The safety plan, by contrast, provides a concrete, personalized, step-by-step guide that the client can carry and reference during moments of acute distress. EPPP questions may test whether a particular element (such as signing a no-suicide contract) is part of the safety plan — it is not.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Experiential Avoidance

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), developed by Steven Hayes, is a "third wave" cognitive-behavioral approach that diverges from traditional CBT by emphasizing psychological flexibility rather than direct modification of thought content. A foundational concept in ACT is experiential avoidance, which refers to an unwillingness to remain in contact with unwanted internal experiences — including distressing thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, and memories — and the resulting engagement in counterproductive behaviors aimed at controlling, suppressing, or escaping those experiences.

According to ACT, experiential avoidance is a core pathological process. When individuals attempt to eliminate or control unpleasant psychological experiences, these efforts frequently backfire, amplifying the very distress they were designed to reduce. ACT distinguishes between clean discomfort — the natural pain that accompanies difficult life circumstances — and dirty discomfort, which is the additional layer of emotional distress and suffering generated by unsuccessful attempts to avoid or suppress unwanted internal states. Dirty discomfort is, in this framework, largely self-inflicted through the struggle against inevitable psychological pain.

ACT addresses experiential avoidance through six core processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values clarification, and committed action. Together, these processes promote psychological flexibility — the capacity to be fully present, open to internal experience, and engaged in value-driven behavior even in the presence of distress.

Dirty Discomfort vs. Clean Discomfort

Clean discomfort is the unavoidable pain that naturally arises from life's challenges — grief after a loss, anxiety before a major exam, or sadness during a difficult transition. Dirty discomfort is the secondary suffering that results from struggling against clean discomfort through experiential avoidance. For instance, a person who experiences anxiety and then becomes anxious about being anxious, drinks to numb the feeling, or avoids situations entirely adds layers of dirty discomfort on top of the original pain.

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT), developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale, integrates principles of cognitive therapy with mindfulness meditation practices adapted from Jon Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program. MBCT was originally designed as a relapse prevention intervention for individuals with recurrent major depression.

The central theoretical premise of MBCT is that individuals who have experienced prior depressive episodes are vulnerable to relapse because mild dysphoric mood states can reactivate patterns of negative, ruminative thinking that escalate into full depressive episodes. Rather than targeting the content of negative thoughts (as in traditional CBT), MBCT teaches clients to recognize the early warning signs of depressive relapse and to adopt a decentered stance toward their thoughts and feelings. Decentering involves observing thoughts as transient mental events rather than treating them as accurate reflections of reality. By shifting their relationship to negative cognitions at the earliest indication of a mood shift, clients can interrupt the downward spiral before it gains momentum.

MBCT is typically delivered as an eight-session group program. Research has demonstrated its effectiveness in significantly reducing relapse rates among individuals who have experienced three or more prior depressive episodes. The approach represents a "third wave" cognitive-behavioral therapy that emphasizes changing one's relationship to thoughts rather than changing thought content directly.

Clinical Note

MBCT is primarily a relapse prevention strategy, not a treatment for acute depression. The target population is individuals in remission who have a history of recurrent depressive episodes. The mechanism of change centers on adopting a decentered perspective at the first signs of returning depression, thereby preventing ruminative cycles from triggering a full relapse.

Mindfulness-based interventions — including Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) — have been applied to a wide range of conditions, from depression and anxiety to chronic pain and cardiovascular disease. However, research evidence indicates that these approaches are more effective for treating psychological and psychiatric disorders than for physical or medical conditions. Meta-analytic reviews show robust effect sizes for mindfulness-based therapies in reducing symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders. When applied to medical conditions such as chronic pain, hypertension, or cancer-related symptoms, the benefits tend to be smaller and more variable. While patients with medical conditions may experience improvements in psychological well-being and quality of life through mindfulness practice, direct effects on physical disease parameters are generally more modest. For the EPPP, it is worth remembering that the strongest evidence base for mindfulness-based therapy lies in the psychological domain, particularly in relapse prevention for recurrent depression (MBCT), where it has been shown to be comparable to maintenance antidepressant medication.

Clinical Note

MBCT was specifically designed to prevent depressive relapse in individuals with three or more previous episodes. It combines mindfulness meditation practices with elements of cognitive therapy to help clients disengage from ruminative thought patterns that trigger depressive episodes. This targeted psychological application represents the intervention's strongest evidence base.

Mindfulness Techniques in ACT

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) incorporates mindfulness-based techniques as a core component of treatment. Unlike traditional cognitive therapy, which seeks to modify the content of maladaptive thoughts, ACT uses mindfulness to change the client's relationship to their thoughts and symptoms. Through exercises in present-moment awareness, clients learn to observe their internal experiences — including distressing thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations — without judgment or attempts at control. The goal is for clients to come to perceive these symptoms as transient psychological events that are uncomfortable but ultimately harmless, rather than as accurate reflections of reality or signals requiring immediate avoidance. This process, known as cognitive defusion, helps clients "step back" from their thoughts and recognize them as passing mental activity rather than literal truths. For example, rather than being consumed by the thought "I am a failure," a client might learn to reframe the experience as "I am having the thought that I am a failure." This subtle but powerful shift reduces the behavioral impact of distressing cognitions and supports greater psychological flexibility. Mindfulness in ACT is not practiced for relaxation per se but as a means of fostering openness and willingness to experience whatever arises internally while continuing to act in accordance with one's values.

Example

An ACT therapist asks a client with chronic anxiety to close their eyes and simply notice each worried thought as it arises, labeling it ("there's a worry about work") and imagining it as a leaf floating down a stream. The client is not asked to evaluate whether the worry is rational or to replace it with a more balanced thought. Instead, the exercise teaches the client to experience anxiety-related cognitions as fleeting mental events that do not require a response, thereby reducing their grip on behavior.

Psychological Inflexibility in ACT

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) conceptualizes depression not as the product of distorted cognitions (as Beck would argue) but as a consequence of psychological inflexibility — the inability to remain in contact with the present moment while pursuing valued life directions. Psychological inflexibility arises when individuals become entangled with their thoughts (cognitive fusion), attempt to avoid or suppress unwanted internal experiences (experiential avoidance), lose touch with their personal values, and act in rigid, habitual patterns rather than adapting to situational demands. From the ACT perspective, these interconnected processes create a narrowing of behavioral repertoire that underlies depression and a wide range of other psychological difficulties. Rather than challenging the content of negative thoughts, ACT targets the function those thoughts serve and the rigid relationship the individual has developed with them. Psychological inflexibility is considered a transdiagnostic risk factor, meaning it contributes not only to depression but also to anxiety disorders, substance use problems, and chronic pain conditions. Treatment aims to cultivate psychological flexibility — the capacity to be open to experience, present-focused, and guided by personal values even in the presence of distressing thoughts and emotions.

Example

A client experiencing depressive symptoms reports spending most of the day in bed to avoid feelings of sadness and self-doubt. An ACT therapist would identify this pattern as experiential avoidance contributing to psychological inflexibility. Rather than disputing the client's negative self-evaluations, the therapist would help the client notice these thoughts without fusing with them and take committed action toward meaningful activities aligned with the client's values — even while uncomfortable feelings persist.

  • Know the major cognitive distortions and be able to identify them in clinical vignettes
  • Ellis's ABC model: A (Activating event), B (Belief), C (Consequence), D (Disputing), E (Effective new belief)
  • Beck uses Socratic questioning; Ellis uses direct disputation
  • Automatic thoughts are surface-level cognitions; schemas are the deepest level
  • Meichenbaum's Stress Inoculation Training has three phases: conceptualization, skills acquisition, application
  • Problem-solving therapy (D'Zurilla & Goldfried) teaches a structured five-step approach to everyday problems
