Overview of Assessment Ethics

Imagine a psychologist asked to provide a diagnostic opinion about a child she has never met — perhaps a custody evaluation where one parent refuses to bring the child in for testing. Or consider a forensic psychologist pressured by an attorney to render a definitive conclusion about a defendant's competency based solely on a review of medical records. In both cases, the psychologist faces a fundamental ethical tension: the demand for professional opinions must be balanced against the adequacy of the information on which those opinions rest. How psychologists navigate these situations — and countless others like them — is the domain of assessment ethics.

Ethical practice in psychological assessment is not simply about following rules; it is about protecting the individuals whose lives are affected by our conclusions. A misinterpreted IQ score can funnel a child into the wrong educational track for years. A poorly validated personality measure used in a hiring decision can unjustly derail a career. A forensic report based on inadequate data can influence whether someone loses custody of their children or spends years in prison. The stakes are enormous, and the ethical standards that govern assessment exist precisely because the potential for harm is so significant.

The framework guiding these practices is found primarily in the APA Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct, with Standard 9 (Assessment) and Standard 10 (Therapy) serving as the most directly relevant sections. Together, these standards establish the obligations psychologists hold when selecting, administering, scoring, interpreting, and communicating test results. They address questions that arise at every stage of the assessment process: Who is qualified to administer a given instrument? Under what conditions can you offer a professional opinion without conducting a face-to-face evaluation? How should test data be released, and to whom? What responsibilities do you carry when working with individuals from diverse linguistic or cultural backgrounds?

For the EPPP, understanding assessment ethics requires more than memorizing rule numbers — you must grasp the reasoning behind each mandate and recognize how these principles apply in complex, real-world clinical scenarios. A question might describe a school psychologist choosing between two instruments for a bilingual student, or a clinician deciding whether to release raw test data to an attorney. In each case, you will need to identify which ethical standard applies, what it requires, and how to balance competing obligations such as client welfare, legal demands, and professional responsibility.

As we move through this chapter, we will examine the specific provisions of Standard 9 in detail — beginning with the foundational requirement that professional opinions be grounded in adequate information. This principle, outlined in Standard 9.01, serves as the ethical bedrock upon which all other assessment standards rest. It establishes a straightforward but powerful expectation: if you are going to make a professional statement about someone's psychological characteristics, you need sufficient data to back it up.

Core Principle:

Psychologists provide opinions about the psychological characteristics of individuals only after conducting an examination adequate to support their statements or conclusions. When an individual examination is not practical, psychologists document their efforts to obtain one, clarify the impact of limited information on the reliability and validity of their opinions, and appropriately limit the nature and extent of their conclusions (Standard 9.01). In clinical practice, this means that a psychologist who is asked to comment on a patient's cognitive functioning after reviewing only a brief screening measure should explicitly acknowledge the limitations of that data and refrain from overstating the certainty of any conclusions drawn from it.

This core principle has far-reaching implications. Consider a neuropsychologist consulted to evaluate whether an elderly patient shows signs of dementia. If the patient is hospitalized and too medically fragile to complete a full battery, the psychologist does not simply decline to help — nor does she issue a definitive diagnosis based on a bedside screening alone. Instead, she conducts what assessment is feasible, documents the constraints, specifies how those constraints limit the conclusions she can draw, and communicates all of this transparently in her report. This is ethical assessment in action: rigorous, honest, and appropriately humble about what the data can and cannot tell us.

With this foundational principle in mind, we turn next to the specific provisions of APA Standard 9, which translate this overarching commitment to adequacy and integrity into concrete, actionable requirements for every phase of the assessment process.

APA Standard 9: Assessment

9.01 Bases for Assessments

Psychologists must base their assessment opinions on information and techniques sufficient to substantiate their findings. This standard applies across forensic, clinical, educational, and organizational settings. When a personal examination is not possible (e.g., in record review or forensic consultation), the psychologist must clearly state the limitations of the resulting opinion.

  • Adequate examination means using procedures appropriate to the referral question, the individual being assessed, and the context of the evaluation
  • If a psychologist cannot conduct a direct examination, they must identify the sources of information used and qualify their conclusions accordingly
  • Opinions must not extend beyond what the data support

9.02 Use of Assessments

Psychologists must administer, adapt, score, interpret, and use assessment instruments in a manner consistent with the research on and evidence for the instrument's usefulness and proper application. Key provisions include:

  • Instruments must be used for purposes for which validity evidence exists
  • When validity or reliability has not been established for a specific population, the psychologist must describe the strengths and limitations of results and interpretation
  • Assessments must be administered in the standardized manner prescribed in the test manual

9.03 Informed Consent in Assessments

Psychologists must obtain informed consent for assessments, except when: (a) testing is mandated by law or regulation, (b) informed consent is implied because testing is a routine educational or organizational activity, or (c) the purpose of testing is to evaluate decisional capacity. Informed consent for assessment should include:

  • The nature and purpose of the assessment
  • Fees and billing arrangements
  • Involvement of third parties (e.g., referring attorneys, insurance companies)
  • Limits of confidentiality, including who will receive results
  • The examinee's right to receive an explanation of results (in language reasonably understandable to the person)
EPPP Exam Tip: When testing is court-ordered, informed consent is not required, but the psychologist is still obligated to inform the individual about the nature and purpose of the evaluation, including the limits of confidentiality and who will receive the report.

9.04 Release of Test Data

Standard 9.04 distinguishes between test data and test materials. Test data includes raw and scaled scores, client responses, and psychologist notes about client statements and behavior during an examination. Test materials include manuals, instruments, protocols, and test questions.

  • Psychologists must release test data to the client or other persons identified in a client release, unless substantial harm or misrepresentation is anticipated
  • In the absence of a client release, psychologists provide test data only when required by law or court order
  • Test materials are protected by copyright and are generally not released to non-qualified individuals
Key Distinction: Test data (the client's actual responses and scores) may be released with client consent. Test materials (the copyrighted items, stimuli, and manuals) are protected under Standard 9.11 and by copyright law. The EPPP frequently tests this distinction.

A critical ethical consideration arises when psychologists wish to use assessment data gathered during clinical evaluations for research purposes. The informed consent obtained at the time of the original assessment covers the clinical use of test information—diagnosis, treatment planning, or responding to the referral question. It does not automatically extend to secondary uses such as inclusion in a research study, publication, or teaching demonstration. Under Standards 8.01 (Institutional Approval) and 8.02 (Informed Consent to Research), psychologists must obtain separate, specific consent from clients before incorporating their assessment data into research, even if the data will be de-identified. This requirement exists because the original consent was given for a particular purpose, and using the data for a different purpose without additional authorization would violate the client's right to control how their personal information is used.

Example

A psychologist administered a comprehensive neuropsychological battery to 40 patients over the past year as part of routine clinical evaluations. She now wants to analyze the data set to investigate patterns of cognitive decline. Before proceeding, she must contact each patient (or their legal representative) to obtain consent specifically for the research use of their test data—even though each patient previously consented to the clinical evaluation itself. If a patient declines or cannot be reached, their data must be excluded from the study unless an IRB grants a waiver based on criteria such as minimal risk and impracticability of obtaining consent.

9.05 Test Construction

Psychologists who develop tests and other assessment techniques use appropriate psychometric procedures and current scientific or professional knowledge for test design, standardization, validation, reduction or elimination of bias, and recommendations for use.

9.06 Interpreting Assessment Results

When interpreting assessment results, psychologists must take into account the purpose of the assessment, various test factors, situational factors, and the test-taking abilities of the person being assessed. Factors that affect interpretation include:

  • The individual's linguistic and cultural background
  • Any situational factors (e.g., testing conditions, fatigue, medication)
  • Disabilities or impairments that may require accommodations
  • The appropriateness of the norms for the individual being assessed

9.07 Assessment by Unqualified Persons

Psychologists do not promote the use of assessment techniques by unqualified persons, except when the use of such instruments is conducted with proper supervision. This means psychologists may delegate scoring and administrative duties to technicians or trainees, but the psychologist retains responsibility for appropriate application, interpretation, and use of results.

9.08 Obsolete Tests and Outdated Test Results

Psychologists do not base their assessment or intervention decisions or recommendations on data or test results that are outdated for the current purpose. They also do not base decisions on measures or procedures that are obsolete and not useful for the current purpose.

Example: Using the original MMPI (published 1943) in current clinical practice would be problematic because the MMPI-2 and MMPI-3 represent substantially revised and re-normed instruments. The original norms are considered outdated and the item content in many cases obsolete.

While Standard 9.08 generally prohibits reliance on outdated measures and results, the standard does not impose an absolute ban. Psychologists may use an older version of a test or previously obtained results when doing so serves a legitimate and defensible purpose. For instance, a psychologist conducting a longitudinal research study may administer the same edition of an instrument across time points to maintain measurement consistency. Similarly, in forensic contexts, a psychologist may need to review historical test data—even from an obsolete instrument—to reconstruct a person's functioning at a prior point in time. The critical ethical requirement is that the psychologist can articulate a sound rationale for why the older instrument or data set remains appropriate for the specific question being addressed, and that any limitations arising from the use of outdated norms or item content are explicitly documented and communicated in the report.

Clinical Note

On the EPPP, the correct answer is typically that obsolete instruments should not be used—unless the question stem provides a specific, defensible justification (e.g., tracking longitudinal change with the same measure, reviewing historical records for forensic reconstruction). When no such justification is present, the psychologist is expected to use the most current edition of the test.

9.09 Test Scoring and Interpretation Services

Psychologists who offer scoring or interpretation services retain responsibility for the accurate application of the procedure, whether the scoring is done by themselves, assistants, or automated services. They must select scoring and interpretation services based on evidence of their validity.

9.10 Explaining Assessment Results

Unless the nature of the relationship precludes it (e.g., organizational screening, forensic evaluation where results go to a third party), psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure that explanations of results are given to the individual or a designated representative. Results should be communicated in language the person can understand.

9.11 Maintaining Test Security

Psychologists make reasonable efforts to maintain the integrity and security of test materials and other assessment techniques consistent with law, contractual obligations, and the APA Ethics Code. This includes not reproducing or publishing copyrighted test items.

APA Standard 10: Therapy (Selected Assessment-Relevant Provisions)

Supervisory Disclosure and Intern Obligations

When a psychology intern or trainee provides services under supervision, ethical and legal standards require that clients be informed of the trainee's status and the supervisory arrangement. Specifically, when the supervisor bears legal responsibility for the trainee's professional activities, the intern must disclose to each client that services are being provided under supervision and must provide the supervisor's name. This requirement flows from multiple ethical principles: the client's right to informed consent (Standard 10.01), the prohibition against deception about one's qualifications, and the general obligation of transparency in professional relationships.

Supervisory disclosure serves several important functions. First, it allows clients to understand who ultimately oversees their care and who they may contact with concerns. Second, it clarifies the limits of confidentiality, since information shared with the trainee will typically be discussed with the supervisor. Third, it ensures that the client can make a genuinely informed decision about whether to proceed with treatment under these conditions.

Supervisory Disclosure Requirements

The trainee must inform clients of: (1) their status as an intern, resident, or trainee; (2) the fact that a licensed psychologist is supervising their work; (3) the supervisor's name and contact information; and (4) any implications for confidentiality arising from supervisory review of case material.

Example

A predoctoral intern at a university counseling center begins seeing a new client for individual therapy. At the outset of the first session, the intern explains that she is completing her doctoral training, that Dr. Martinez serves as her clinical supervisor and holds legal responsibility for the services provided, and that session content will be discussed in supervision. The client is given the opportunity to ask questions and to decline services if preferred.

10.01 Informed Consent to Therapy

Although primarily a therapy standard, 10.01 intersects with assessment when psychological testing is used as part of treatment planning. Clients must be informed about the nature of the therapeutic procedures, including any assessment components used to guide intervention.

10.10 Terminating Therapy

Assessment plays a role in determining when therapy goals have been met or when treatment is no longer beneficial. Outcome monitoring instruments help inform these decisions ethically.

Confidentiality in Group Therapy (Standard 10.03)

The APA Ethics Code specifies that psychologists who conduct group therapy must address confidentiality expectations at the outset of treatment and revisit them as needed throughout the group process. Because confidentiality in group settings cannot be guaranteed by the therapist alone—each member bears responsibility for protecting the disclosures of others—the psychologist has a duty to clearly explain both the value of maintaining confidentiality and the inherent limits of confidentiality within a group context. Similar provisions appear in the Canadian Code of Ethics for Psychologists, which likewise emphasizes the psychologist's obligation to discuss confidentiality with all participants early and repeatedly.

Unlike individual therapy, where the psychologist can directly control the flow of information, group therapy introduces multiple individuals who each have access to sensitive personal disclosures made by fellow members. The psychologist cannot legally or practically enforce confidentiality among group participants, but the ethical obligation is to proactively educate members about its importance. This discussion should occur during the initial session and be reinforced in subsequent sessions, particularly when new members join, when sensitive topics arise, or when a breach is suspected. Psychologists should consider having members sign a group agreement acknowledging their understanding of confidentiality expectations, while also being transparent that the therapist cannot guarantee that all members will honor these commitments.

Clinical Note

In practice, facilitators often establish explicit group rules during the first session, including a "what is said here stays here" guideline. However, ethical psychologists go beyond this by explaining why confidentiality matters for therapeutic trust, describing specific scenarios that might constitute a breach (e.g., discussing a group member's disclosures with a spouse), and reminding the group of these expectations periodically—not just at intake.

Sexual Relationships with Former Clients

The APA Ethics Code addresses sexual intimacies with former clients under Standard 10.08. Psychologists are prohibited from engaging in sexual relationships with former therapy clients for a minimum of two years following the termination of treatment. Even after this two-year period has elapsed, such relationships are permissible only under the most unusual circumstances. The psychologist bears the burden of demonstrating that no exploitation has occurred, taking into account factors such as:

  • The duration and intensity of the therapeutic relationship
  • The amount of time that has passed since therapy ended
  • The circumstances surrounding termination
  • The client's personal history and current mental health status
  • Whether the psychologist's actions suggest the therapeutic relationship was terminated specifically to pursue a sexual relationship
  • Any statements or actions by the therapist during treatment that suggested a future sexual relationship

The ethical rationale centers on the inherent power differential in the therapist-client relationship, which does not automatically dissolve upon termination. Transference dynamics, emotional dependency, and the vulnerability that accompanies self-disclosure in therapy can persist well beyond the final session. The two-year minimum serves as a protective threshold, but it does not create an automatic safe harbor—most ethics committees and licensing boards scrutinize post-termination sexual relationships with considerable skepticism regardless of how much time has passed.

Clinical Note

For the EPPP, remember that sexual contact with current clients is always prohibited with no exceptions. With former clients, the prohibition lasts at least two years, and after that window the burden falls entirely on the psychologist to prove the absence of exploitation. In practice, many ethics scholars recommend that psychologists treat the prohibition as permanent, given the difficulty of demonstrating that a power imbalance no longer exists.

Cultural Considerations in Assessment

Imagine administering a widely used intelligence test to a 10-year-old child who recently immigrated from rural Guatemala. The child struggles with items that reference American cultural icons, freezes when confronted with a timed computerized format she has never encountered, and scores well below average. Does this score reflect her cognitive ability — or does it reflect the profound mismatch between the test's assumptions and her lived experience? This question sits at the heart of culturally competent assessment, and getting the answer wrong can have devastating consequences: misdiagnosis, inappropriate placement, denial of services, or the reinforcement of systemic inequities that psychology has an ethical obligation to dismantle.

Culturally competent assessment is precisely that — an ethical obligation, not merely a best practice or an aspirational ideal. The APA Ethics Code, testing standards, and a growing body of multicultural guidelines all converge on the same principle: assessment that ignores culture risks producing results that are not just inaccurate but actively harmful. Several standards and guidelines shape this domain, and understanding them is essential for ethical practice as well as for the EPPP.

Test Bias and Fairness

When psychologists talk about test bias, they are not using the word casually. Bias in a psychometric sense refers to systematic error that distorts scores for members of particular groups, leading to conclusions that misrepresent their true abilities, traits, or symptoms. Researchers have identified three major categories of bias, each operating at a different level of the assessment process, and each capable of undermining the validity of test results for culturally diverse examinees.

  • Construct bias occurs when the construct measured by the test differs across cultural groups. For instance, a depression inventory developed with Western samples may emphasize cognitive symptoms like guilt and worthlessness, while in some East Asian cultures, depression manifests more prominently through somatic complaints like fatigue and headaches. The test is ostensibly measuring "depression," but the construct itself carries different meaning across groups.
  • Method bias arises from differential familiarity with the testing format or medium. A client who has never used a computer may perform poorly on a computerized adaptive test not because of low ability, but because the medium itself is foreign. Similarly, timed tests may disadvantage individuals from cultures where speed is not valued as a marker of competence.
  • Item bias (differential item functioning, or DIF) occurs when members of different groups with the same ability level have different probabilities of endorsing an item. Consider a vocabulary item that uses the word "regatta" — test-takers from affluent coastal communities may find this trivially easy, while those from inland or lower-income backgrounds may struggle, not because of lower verbal ability but because of differential exposure to the word.

For the EPPP, it is important to distinguish these three types of bias and to recognize that identifying bias requires more than intuition — it demands empirical analysis. A test item that looks culturally loaded on its face may perform equivalently across groups (and vice versa), which is why statistical techniques like DIF analysis are indispensable tools for test developers committed to fairness.

Use of Interpreters

When assessment involves individuals with limited English proficiency, the ideal approach is to use tests that have been validated in the individual's native language, with norms drawn from an appropriate reference population. However, validated translations are not available for every language or every instrument, and in clinical reality, psychologists frequently find themselves relying on interpreters. This is a workable solution — but only when handled with great care. The involvement of a third party in the testing room fundamentally alters the standardized conditions under which the test was normed, and this alteration must be acknowledged rather than ignored. When interpreters are used, the psychologist must:

  • Ensure the interpreter is trained in psychological assessment contexts — a bilingual family member or untrained hospital staff member is not an adequate substitute, as they may inadvertently simplify questions, provide cues, or introduce emotional dynamics that compromise the assessment
  • Note the impact of using an interpreter on the standardization of the test — translated instructions, altered pacing, and the relational triangle between psychologist, interpreter, and client all represent deviations from standardized administration
  • Document any limitations to the conclusions that result from the use of an interpreter, clearly stating in the report that scores should be interpreted with caution and may underestimate (or in some cases overestimate) the client's true functioning

Think of it this way: the psychologist retains full responsibility for the assessment even when an interpreter is present. The interpreter is a tool for communication, but the clinical judgment — including the judgment about how much confidence to place in the resulting data — always rests with the psychologist.

APA Multicultural Guidelines

Beyond the specific mechanics of test selection and administration, the profession has articulated a broader vision for how culture should inform every stage of the assessment process. The APA Multicultural Guidelines (2017) emphasize that psychologists should adopt an ecological approach to context, identity, and intersectionality when conducting assessments. Rather than treating culture as a single demographic variable to be checked off, these guidelines call on practitioners to recognize how power, privilege, and oppression can affect test performance, help-seeking behavior, and the very meaning of assessment results. A low score on an achievement test takes on a fundamentally different significance when the examiner understands that the client grew up in an under-resourced school district shaped by decades of residential segregation.

This ecological perspective also means attending to intersectionality — the way multiple identity dimensions (race, gender, socioeconomic status, disability status, sexual orientation, immigration history) interact to shape a person's experience. A Latina transgender woman seeking a neuropsychological evaluation brings a constellation of social experiences that no single cultural category can capture, and the competent assessor approaches this complexity with curiosity rather than reductive categorization.

Multicultural Assessment-Intervention Process (MAIP): A framework by Dana (1993, 2005) for integrating cultural considerations into assessment, including: (1) determining the cultural orientation of the client, (2) selecting culturally appropriate instruments and norms, (3) using culture-specific service delivery styles, and (4) incorporating emic (culture-specific) versus etic (universal) perspectives in interpretation. The MAIP is particularly useful because it provides a structured, step-by-step process that prevents clinicians from treating cultural competence as an afterthought. By beginning with an assessment of the client's acculturation level and cultural identity, the MAIP ensures that instrument selection and interpretive decisions flow from a genuine understanding of who the client is, not from assumptions based on surface-level demographic characteristics.

Norm Appropriateness

A recurring EPPP theme — and a question that arises constantly in clinical practice — is whether available norms are appropriate for the individual being tested. Norms tell us how an individual's score compares to a reference group, but that comparison is only meaningful if the reference group is relevant to the person sitting across from us. When normative samples do not include adequate representation of the examinee's demographic group, the psychologist must note this limitation and interpret results with caution. For example, if a cognitive test was normed exclusively on English-speaking, college-educated adults, applying those norms to a bilingual elder with a sixth-grade education from a rural community could produce misleadingly low scores that confuse cultural and educational differences with cognitive impairment.

In practice, this means that psychologists must do their homework before selecting an instrument: reviewing the test manual's description of the normative sample, evaluating whether the client's demographic characteristics are reasonably represented, and — when they are not — explicitly documenting this gap in the assessment report. The goal is not to avoid testing altogether when norms are imperfect, but to ensure that consumers of the report (referral sources, courts, schools, clients themselves) understand exactly how much confidence the scores warrant. Transparency about norm limitations is not a sign of weakness in a report; it is a hallmark of ethical, culturally informed practice.

Special Assessment Contexts

Forensic Assessment Ethics

The Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychology (APA, 2013) provide additional ethical guidance for assessment in legal contexts:

  • The psychologist's primary obligation is to the retaining party (court, attorney), not the examinee
  • Informed consent is replaced by notification of purpose when the evaluation is court-ordered
  • Results and reports go to the referring party; the examinee typically does not have unilateral access to the report
  • Psychologists must clearly identify their role (e.g., evaluator vs. treating clinician) and avoid dual relationships

Neuropsychological Assessment

The release of raw neuropsychological test data has been a significant ethical concern. The NAN (National Academy of Neuropsychology) Position Paper on test security emphasizes that releasing raw test data to non-qualified individuals can compromise the validity of the instruments for future use.

Assessment of Children and Adolescents

When assessing minors, informed consent is obtained from the parent or legal guardian, while assent is obtained from the child. Assent means the child agrees to participate in the evaluation with an age-appropriate understanding of what will occur. Special considerations include:

  • Results may be shared with schools, courts, and other agencies depending on the purpose of the evaluation
  • The child's developmental level affects which instruments and norms are appropriate
  • Cultural and linguistic factors may be more pronounced in pediatric populations

When providing individual therapy to a minor, ethical practice requires the psychologist to secure written informed consent from the child's parent or legal guardian as early as possible in the treatment process—ideally before services begin. Simultaneously, the psychologist should obtain verbal assent from the child, using developmentally appropriate language to explain what therapy involves, what kinds of activities might occur in sessions, and how information will and will not be shared with parents. Assent reflects the child's willingness to participate and acknowledges their emerging autonomy, even though they lack the legal capacity to consent independently. If circumstances require that therapy begin before written parental consent can be obtained (e.g., a crisis situation), the psychologist should secure that consent at the earliest feasible opportunity and document the reasons for any delay. Throughout treatment, the psychologist must navigate the sometimes competing interests of the child's therapeutic needs and the parents' legal right to information, clearly establishing these boundaries at the outset of the relationship.

Competence in Assessment

Imagine a psychologist who completed extensive training in cognitive assessment during graduate school — administering the WAIS and WISC dozens of times under close supervision. Years later, a referral arrives requesting a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation for a patient with suspected traumatic brain injury. The psychologist has never administered the Halstead-Reitan Battery, has no formal training in neuropsychological interpretation, and has not supervised or been supervised on such cases. Can she simply read the test manual and proceed? The answer, grounded in both ethical principles and common sense, is no — and the reasons illuminate one of the most fundamental standards governing psychological assessment.

Having explored special assessment contexts involving minors and vulnerable populations, we now turn to a broader principle that cuts across every assessment situation a psychologist will ever encounter: the obligation to practice within the boundaries of one's competence. This principle may sound straightforward, but the EPPP tests its nuances extensively, because the consequences of incompetent assessment are profound — misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatment recommendations, harm to clients, and erosion of public trust in the profession.

Standard 2.01 of the APA Ethics Code requires psychologists to provide services only within the boundaries of their competence, based on their education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study, or professional experience. When applied to the assessment domain specifically, this standard carries several critical implications that deserve careful consideration:

  • Psychologists should only administer and interpret tests for which they have received specific training
  • Competence in administering a test does not automatically confer competence in interpreting it (and vice versa)
  • Psychologists must stay current with revisions to instruments, new normative data, and evolving research on test validity
  • When using computerized or automated interpretive reports, the psychologist must be competent to evaluate the quality and clinical utility of the output

The second point in this list deserves special emphasis because it surprises many trainees. A psychologist may be perfectly capable of following standardized administration procedures for the Rorschach Inkblot Test — presenting cards, recording responses, timing latencies — yet lack the advanced training needed to score responses using the Exner Comprehensive System or the R-PAS and to draw clinically meaningful inferences from the resulting data. Administration and interpretation are distinct competencies, and possessing one does not guarantee the other. Similarly, a seasoned clinician who is expert at interpreting MMPI-3 profiles may never have personally administered the instrument in a proctored setting, relying instead on technicians or online platforms for administration. That clinician's interpretive competence does not extend to claims about proper administration procedures.

The obligation to stay current is equally important and often tested on the EPPP. When the WISC-IV was replaced by the WISC-V, the new edition introduced changes to subtest composition, factor structure, and normative data. A psychologist who continued using the WISC-IV without awareness of these changes — or who applied WISC-V interpretive frameworks to WISC-IV data — would be practicing outside the bounds of competence. The same logic applies when new research reveals that a test has poor validity for a particular population. Competence is not a static achievement earned at graduation; it is a dynamic, ongoing professional responsibility that demands continuous learning.

Perhaps the most frequently tested facet of assessment competence on the EPPP involves the use of computerized testing platforms and automated interpretive reports. Modern software can score a 567-item MMPI-3 in seconds and generate a multi-page narrative report complete with diagnostic hypotheses and treatment recommendations. The convenience is undeniable — but the ethical responsibility remains entirely with the psychologist. An automated report may flag an elevated Scale 6 (Ideas of Persecution) and suggest paranoid ideation, but only the psychologist can determine whether that elevation reflects genuine paranoid thinking, a realistic response to a hostile work environment, cultural mistrust of institutions, or an invalid profile produced by a "True" response bias. The computer generates output; the psychologist provides interpretation.

Practice Point: The EPPP frequently tests the principle that the psychologist, not the computer, is responsible for the interpretation. Even when using computerized scoring and narrative reports, the psychologist must integrate results with clinical observations, background data, and the specific referral question.

What should a psychologist do when a referral falls outside their area of competence? Standard 2.01 provides clear guidance: the psychologist should either refer the client to a qualified colleague, obtain appropriate training and supervision before proceeding, or — in emergency situations where no qualified professional is available — provide services with the understanding that they will seek consultation and additional training as quickly as possible. This last provision acknowledges real-world clinical realities, particularly in rural or underserved settings, but it is the exception rather than the rule. On the EPPP, the best answer will almost always prioritize referral or consultation over attempting an unfamiliar assessment independently.

Record Keeping and Documentation

Imagine this scenario: A psychologist conducted a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation three years ago, diagnosing a child with a specific learning disability and recommending special education services. Now the family has moved to a new state, and the receiving school district is challenging the diagnosis. The parents call the original psychologist requesting documentation — but she changed practices, and her records were incomplete or discarded. Without the original test data, behavioral observations, and interpretive rationale, the family has no way to substantiate the diagnosis, and the child's educational accommodations hang in the balance. This is not a hypothetical rarity; it is exactly the kind of situation that makes thorough record keeping one of the most consequential — and most frequently underestimated — ethical obligations in assessment practice.

Having just discussed competence as the foundation of ethical assessment, we now turn to what happens after the clinician has competently selected, administered, and interpreted psychological tests. The results of that work must be documented with the same rigor and care that went into producing them. Documentation is not merely a bureaucratic afterthought; it is the tangible evidence that an assessment was conducted ethically, that clinical decisions were grounded in data, and that the psychologist can account for every step of the evaluative process if ever called upon to do so — whether by a client, a court, a licensing board, or a colleague assuming future care.

Standard 6.01 of the APA Ethics Code requires psychologists to create and maintain documentation of their professional and scientific work. In assessment contexts, this standard translates into specific documentation practices that serve multiple purposes: protecting the client, supporting continuity of care, enabling peer review, and providing a defensible record should questions arise about the assessment's validity or the psychologist's decision-making. The essential elements of assessment documentation include:

  • Complete records of all tests administered, including raw data and scored protocols
  • Documentation of the rationale for the selection of instruments
  • Notes on behavioral observations during testing
  • Records of all interpretive procedures used (including computerized reports)
  • Copies of the written report and any feedback provided to the client or referring party

Each of these elements deserves attention. Consider the requirement to document instrument selection rationale: if a psychologist chose the WISC-V over the Stanford-Binet 5 for a particular child, the record should reflect why — perhaps because the child's primary language, the referral question, or the availability of local norms made one instrument more appropriate than the other. This kind of reasoning, captured in the moment, becomes invaluable when the assessment is later reviewed. Similarly, behavioral observations during testing — noting that a client appeared fatigued during the second hour, requested frequent breaks, or seemed highly anxious — provide critical context for interpreting scores that might otherwise appear anomalous or misleading.

The documentation of interpretive procedures is equally important in an era when many psychologists rely on computerized scoring and narrative report generators. If a computer-generated interpretive report was used, the record should indicate this, along with evidence that the psychologist reviewed, contextualized, and, where necessary, modified the automated output to reflect the unique circumstances of the individual client. Simply printing a computer-generated report and placing it in a file without professional review and integration would fall short of both ethical and documentation standards.

Records must be maintained for the period required by applicable laws and institutional regulations, typically a minimum of seven years after the last date of service (or three years after a minor reaches the age of majority, whichever is later). In practice, many experienced psychologists retain records well beyond these minimums, particularly for evaluations involving children, forensic matters, or disability determinations, where records may be needed years or even decades later. The guiding principle is straightforward: if there is any foreseeable reason a record might be needed in the future, err on the side of preservation. Thorough documentation protects clients, supports the integrity of the profession, and — returning to our opening scenario — ensures that the careful work of a competent assessment can speak for itself long after the testing session has ended.

Emerging Issues in Assessment Ethics

Telepsychology and Remote Assessment

The growth of telepsychology has introduced new ethical considerations for assessment practice. The APA Guidelines for the Practice of Telepsychology (2013) require psychologists to consider whether remote administration alters standardized procedures, whether test security can be maintained in a remote environment, and whether the test's normative data are applicable to a remotely administered version.

  • Some tests have validated telehealth administration protocols; others do not
  • Test publishers have varied in their positions on remote administration
  • Psychologists must document any modifications to standardized procedures and note potential impacts on validity

Use of Psychological Tests in Immigration Proceedings

Assessment in immigration contexts presents unique ethical challenges, including the availability of appropriate norms, the use of interpreters, the potential for coerced evaluation, and the need to understand trauma-related presentations in culturally diverse populations. Psychologists must be especially careful about the cultural validity of instruments used in these settings.

Automated and AI-Assisted Interpretation

The increasing availability of computer-generated interpretive reports raises ethical questions about the role of the psychologist in the interpretive process. Standard 9.09 makes clear that the psychologist remains responsible for the accuracy and appropriateness of any automated interpretation, regardless of who or what generated it.

Commonly Tested EPPP Topics

Having explored the evolving landscape of assessment ethics — from technology-assisted interpretation to the psychologist's enduring responsibility for automated reports — it's time to zoom in on the specific topics that appear most frequently on the EPPP. Think of this section as your field guide to the high-yield distinctions and standards that exam writers return to again and again. These aren't obscure edge cases; they are the foundational ethical principles that shape everyday clinical practice, and getting them wrong on the exam (or in the real world) can have serious consequences.

One of the most reliable patterns in EPPP questions involves presenting a clinical scenario and asking you to identify which ethical standard applies, or which course of action is most appropriate. The topics below represent the distinctions that candidates most commonly confuse or oversimplify. As you review each one, try to picture yourself in the scenario — you'll find that grounding these rules in clinical reality makes them far easier to recall under exam pressure.

Test data vs. test materials:

This is the most frequently tested distinction in assessment ethics, and for good reason — it governs what you can and cannot release when a client, attorney, or third party requests "the test results." Test data refers to the client's own responses, scores, notes you made during testing, and any other information generated by the client's performance. Under Standard 9.04, test data are generally releasable to the client or their authorized representative when accompanied by a valid release of information. Test materials, by contrast, are the copyrighted test items, protocols, scoring keys, and manuals themselves — the intellectual property of the test publisher. Standard 9.11 obligates psychologists to protect these materials from unauthorized disclosure. Imagine a divorce attorney demanding "all testing records" — the ethical psychologist releases the client's scores and response summaries (test data) but does not photocopy and hand over the actual WISC-V stimulus booklet (test materials). Confusing these two categories is the single most common error tested in this domain.

Informed consent exceptions:

In most clinical situations, psychologists must obtain informed consent before conducting an assessment — explaining the purpose, nature, and anticipated use of results. However, consent is not required when testing is (a) mandated by law or governmental regulation, (b) conducted as a routine institutional or educational activity (such as standardized school testing), or (c) performed to assess an individual's decisional capacity. Consider a court-ordered competency evaluation: the defendant does not need to consent for the evaluation to proceed, because it is legally mandated. However — and this is the nuance the EPPP loves to test — even when formal consent is not required, the psychologist must still provide notification of purpose. The evaluee should be informed about the nature of the assessment, who requested it, and how the results will be used. The absence of a consent requirement never eliminates the duty to inform.

Outdated instruments:

Using an obsolete version of a test — for example, administering the WAIS-R when the WAIS-IV is the current edition — violates Standard 9.08, which requires psychologists to use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for the purpose at hand. Outdated norms can lead to inaccurate scores (the "Flynn effect" means older IQ norms tend to inflate scores, which can have real consequences in disability or forensic evaluations). The one important caveat: there may be a specific, defensible clinical or research reason to use an older version — perhaps no updated version exists in the client's language, or longitudinal research requires consistency. In such cases, the psychologist must document the rationale and acknowledge the limitation in the report. On the EPPP, if a question offers "no justification was documented" as a detail, the answer almost always points to an ethical violation.

Cultural competence:

Standard 9.06 requires psychologists to consider the appropriateness of norms, language factors, and broader cultural context when selecting, administering, and interpreting assessment instruments. A Spanish-speaking client assessed with an English-language verbal IQ measure, without any discussion of language proficiency in the report, represents a clear violation. The psychologist must evaluate whether the test's normative sample is representative of the client's demographic background, whether language or cultural factors may have influenced performance, and whether alternative instruments or supplementary methods are needed to ensure valid interpretation. Failure to address these factors doesn't just violate an ethical standard — it can result in misdiagnosis, inappropriate treatment recommendations, or unjust legal outcomes. Cultural competence in assessment is not a courtesy; it is an ethical obligation.

These four topics — the test data versus test materials distinction, informed consent exceptions, the prohibition on outdated instruments, and the mandate for culturally competent assessment — form the ethical backbone of EPPP assessment questions. When you encounter a complex scenario on exam day, start by asking yourself which of these core principles is at stake. More often than not, one of them holds the key to the correct answer.
