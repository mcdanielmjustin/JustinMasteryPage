Overview of Interest Inventories

Imagine a bright college sophomore sitting across from you in a counseling session. She's earning straight A's in organic chemistry, her parents are thrilled about medical school, and she's miserable. She spends every free moment sketching architectural designs, volunteering at Habitat for Humanity build sites, and daydreaming about spatial problem-solving. Her aptitude for medicine is clear — but her heart is somewhere else entirely. This is precisely the kind of disconnect that career counseling professionals encounter daily, and it illustrates why interest inventories occupy such an important place in the clinician's assessment toolkit.

Interest inventories are assessment instruments designed to systematically measure an individual's preferences for various activities, occupations, and work environments. They emerged from the recognition that vocational satisfaction depends not only on what a person can do but on what a person wants to do — and that these two dimensions don't always align. Unlike aptitude or ability tests, which measure capacity for performance, interest inventories capture the motivational side of the equation: the patterns of attraction and engagement that draw someone toward certain kinds of work and away from others.

These instruments are used most commonly in vocational guidance settings — college career centers, rehabilitation counseling, workforce development programs, and private practice with clients navigating career transitions. But their relevance extends further. Clinical psychologists may use interest data when helping clients with depression explore sources of meaning and engagement, or when working with adolescents on identity development. In forensic and rehabilitation settings, interest inventories help guide vocational planning for individuals re-entering the workforce after injury, illness, or incarceration.

For the EPPP, you should know the major interest inventory instruments, their theoretical foundations, and the specific scales and scores they produce. You'll also want to understand what these instruments can and cannot tell you — a distinction that begins with the fundamental difference between interests and aptitudes.

Interests vs. Aptitudes:

Interests refer to what activities a person finds enjoyable, appealing, or intrinsically motivating — the tasks and environments that naturally capture their attention and energy. Aptitudes refer to the capacity to learn or perform a skill — the cognitive, perceptual, or motor abilities that predict how well someone might perform in a given domain. A person may have a strong interest in an area without corresponding aptitude (and vice versa), as in the case of our chemistry student who excels in science but is drawn to architecture. Comprehensive career assessment typically evaluates both dimensions, because lasting vocational satisfaction tends to emerge at the intersection of "I enjoy this" and "I'm capable of doing this well."

Think of it this way: aptitude tests answer the question "What are you good at?" while interest inventories answer the question "What lights you up?" Both questions matter, but they tap fundamentally different psychological constructs. A client who chooses a career based solely on aptitude may succeed but feel unfulfilled; one who follows interest alone may feel passionate but struggle with performance demands. The clinician's job is to help clients integrate both sources of information into wise vocational decision-making.

As we turn to the specific models and instruments that shape modern interest assessment, we begin with the theoretical framework that has had the single greatest influence on the field — John Holland's RIASEC model, which provides the organizing structure for most contemporary interest inventories.

Holland's RIASEC Model

Building on the overview of interest inventories, we now turn to the theoretical framework that underpins most of them. Imagine a career counselor working with a college junior who says, "I like science, but I also love working with people — I can't figure out what to do." How does the counselor organize that information? How do they move from vague preferences to concrete occupational options? The answer, more often than not, begins with a single hexagon — one of the most influential models in vocational psychology.

John Holland's theory of vocational personalities and work environments is the most widely used framework for understanding vocational interests, and it serves as the backbone of several major interest inventories you will encounter on the EPPP, including the Strong Interest Inventory and the Self-Directed Search. Holland's central insight was elegant: people are not randomly scattered across the world of work. Instead, both people and the environments they gravitate toward can be meaningfully classified into six broad types. He arranged these six types in a hexagonal structure — the RIASEC hexagon — where spatial proximity corresponds to psychological similarity. Adjacent types on the hexagon share more features with each other than types that sit across from one another, a principle that has profound implications for career counseling and for interpreting interest inventory results.

The six types form the acronym RIASEC, and each describes both a personality orientation and a corresponding work environment. Think of each type as a "lens" through which a person approaches the world — what they notice, what energizes them, and what feels like a natural fit. The table below summarizes each type with representative occupations that embody its core characteristics.

TypeDescriptionExample Occupations
Realistic (R)Prefers hands-on, physical, mechanical, or outdoor activities; values practical solutionsEngineer, mechanic, carpenter, farmer
Investigative (I)Prefers analytical, intellectual, scientific activities; values research and inquiryScientist, physician, mathematician, researcher
Artistic (A)Prefers creative, unstructured, expressive activities; values aesthetics and imaginationArtist, musician, writer, actor, graphic designer
Social (S)Prefers helping, teaching, counseling, and cooperative activities; values interpersonal relationshipsTeacher, counselor, social worker, nurse
Enterprising (E)Prefers leading, persuading, managing, and selling activities; values influence and statusManager, salesperson, lawyer, politician
Conventional (C)Prefers structured, organized, data-management activities; values accuracy and orderAccountant, banker, clerk, office manager

A helpful way to remember the hexagon's layout is to picture it like a clock: Realistic sits at the top, then Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional proceed around the shape, returning to Realistic. Notice how adjacent types share a natural affinity. For instance, Realistic and Investigative both involve working with things or data in structured ways — a mechanical engineer (R) and a physicist (I) might sit in adjacent labs. Meanwhile, Realistic and Social sit on opposite sides of the hexagon, reflecting their psychological distance: the person who thrives fixing engines in solitude is typically quite different from the person who thrives leading group therapy sessions. This spatial logic is not arbitrary — it has been empirically supported across cultures and decades of research.

Returning to our college junior who likes science and people: a Holland-informed counselor would recognize that the student's interests may cluster around the Investigative-Social region of the hexagon. That combination — IS — points toward occupations like physician, clinical psychologist, or health educator, where scientific inquiry meets interpersonal engagement. This is the practical power of the model: it translates subjective experience into a structured map that guides exploration.

Key Principles of Holland's Theory

Holland's model is more than a simple classification scheme. It rests on five interrelated principles that explain how personality types, work environments, and career outcomes interact. These principles are frequently tested on the EPPP, so let's examine each one carefully — not just what they mean in theory, but what they look like in practice.

  • Congruence: People are most satisfied and productive when their personality type matches their work environment (e.g., an Artistic person in an Artistic job). This is perhaps the most clinically important principle. When a highly Social individual takes a Conventional data-entry position because it pays well, the resulting lack of congruence often manifests as dissatisfaction, disengagement, and eventually turnover. Congruence research consistently shows that person-environment fit predicts job satisfaction, vocational stability, and occupational achievement.
  • Consistency: Some type combinations are more related to each other (adjacent on the hexagon, e.g., RI or SE) than others (opposite, e.g., RA or SC). Consistent profiles are more stable and predictable. A client whose top two codes are Social and Enterprising (SE — adjacent types) has a consistent profile, suggesting coherent interests. A client whose top two are Realistic and Social (RS — opposite types) has an inconsistent profile, which may indicate conflicting pulls that warrant further exploration in counseling.
  • Differentiation: A person who strongly resembles one type and little resembles others is well-differentiated. A flat profile (similar scores across all types) is undifferentiated and harder to interpret. Think of differentiation as the "sharpness" of the profile: a well-differentiated client has a clear peak, making career recommendations straightforward. An undifferentiated client — with roughly equal scores across RIASEC — may be in an early stage of career exploration or may have genuinely broad interests that require a different counseling approach.
  • Identity: The clarity and stability of a person's goals, interests, and talents. Identity is the subjective counterpart to differentiation — it captures how clearly the individual perceives their own vocational self. A client with high identity can articulate their goals and preferences, while a client with low identity may feel confused, directionless, or overwhelmed by options.
  • Calculus: The hexagonal arrangement reflects the relationships among the types — adjacent types are most similar; opposite types are most different. This principle is the geometric foundation of the entire model and is what makes it possible to quantify congruence and consistency. Without the hexagonal calculus, we would have six independent categories rather than an integrated, relational system.

Together, these five principles create a powerful interpretive framework. When you encounter a Holland-based profile, you should be able to assess not only which types are dominant (the three-letter code) but also how congruent the person is with their current or proposed environment, how consistent and differentiated their profile is, and how clearly they understand their own vocational identity. Each of these dimensions carries distinct implications for career counseling interventions.

EPPP Key Point: The hexagonal model arranges types so that adjacent types (e.g., R-I, I-A, A-S, S-E, E-C, C-R) are most similar, and opposite types (R-S, I-E, A-C) are most dissimilar. Congruence predicts job satisfaction, stability, and achievement. On the EPPP, be prepared to identify which type pairs are adjacent versus opposite, to recognize consistent versus inconsistent profiles, and to distinguish differentiated from undifferentiated profiles. A common exam scenario presents a client's three-letter Holland code and asks you to evaluate fit with a described work environment.

Strong Interest Inventory (SII)

Imagine sitting across from a college sophomore who says, "I know I'm interested in science, but I have no idea what kind of career that actually means." A tool that simply tells her she has "investigative interests" confirms what she already senses but leaves her no closer to a decision. What she needs is an instrument that can take that broad self-knowledge and progressively sharpen it — from a general personality-environment fit, through specific clusters of activity preferences, all the way down to particular occupations whose members share her unique pattern of likes and dislikes. That instrument is the Strong Interest Inventory, the most widely used and thoroughly researched interest inventory in the field.

Originally developed by E. K. Strong Jr. in 1927 — making it one of the oldest psychological assessment tools still in active clinical use — the Strong Interest Inventory has undergone numerous revisions, with the current version published in 2012 by CPP (now The Myers-Briggs Company). As we discussed in the preceding section on Holland's RIASEC model, the six vocational personality types provide a powerful organizing framework for understanding how people and work environments fit together. The Strong Interest Inventory adopts Holland's RIASEC hexagon as its structural backbone, layering increasingly specific scales on top of that foundation to give clinicians and career counselors a richly detailed picture of a client's vocational interests.

Three Levels of Scales

The Strong Interest Inventory organizes its results across three hierarchical levels of measurement, each offering progressively greater specificity in linking an individual's interest patterns to potential career directions. Think of it as a zoom lens: the broadest level gives you the panoramic landscape of a person's interests, the middle level focuses on recognizable neighborhoods within that landscape, and the most specific level drops you at the doorstep of individual occupations. Understanding how these three tiers relate to one another is essential for interpreting the profile — and for answering EPPP questions about scale construction methods.

1. General Occupational Themes (GOTs)

The six GOTs correspond directly to Holland's RIASEC types — Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, and Conventional — and they provide the broadest level of information about an individual's interests. When a career counselor glances at a Strong profile and sees that a client's top two themes are Investigative and Artistic, the counselor already has a working hypothesis: this person likely gravitates toward creative problem-solving in knowledge-rich domains, perhaps fields like architecture, industrial design, or scientific journalism.

  • Scores are reported as standard scores with a mean of 50 and SD of 10
  • Scores are compared to a combined-gender or same-gender reference group
  • The GOT profile provides the Holland code (e.g., ISA, SEC) used to identify congruent occupations

2. Basic Interest Scales (BIS)

The 30 Basic Interest Scales provide more specific information within each GOT, breaking broad themes into focused activity clusters. Consider our college sophomore who scored high on the Investigative theme. That tells us she gravitates toward analytical, intellectual pursuits — but does that mean she would thrive in a laboratory running experiments, at a whiteboard solving mathematical proofs, or in a hospital diagnosing rare diseases? Within the Investigative theme, separate BIS scores for Science, Mathematics, and Medical Science help answer exactly that question, guiding her from a general interest area toward a meaningful subset of activities.

  • BIS scores help differentiate within a broad interest area, revealing which specific activity clusters resonate most strongly
  • Also reported as standard scores (M = 50, SD = 10)
  • Useful for identifying specific activity clusters within a general theme, making them especially valuable in career counseling conversations where clients need concrete next steps

3. Occupational Scales (OS)

The 260 Occupational Scales represent the most specific — and arguably the most clinically powerful — level of the Strong Interest Inventory. Each scale was developed using the criterion keying (empirical method) approach, which means that test developers did not sit down and decide which items "should" relate to a given occupation based on theory. Instead, they collected item responses from people already employed and satisfied in a specific occupation and compared those responses to a general reference sample. Items that reliably distinguished, say, satisfied veterinarians from the general population were retained for the Veterinarian scale, regardless of whether those items seemed logically connected to animal care. This empirical strategy — the same one Edward Strong pioneered in 1927 — lets the data, rather than assumptions, define each scale.

  • A high score on an occupational scale indicates that the examinee's pattern of interests closely resembles that of satisfied workers in that occupation — not that the person has the aptitude for the job, but that they share the same constellation of likes and dislikes
  • Scores are reported as standard scores (M = 50, SD = 10) for each occupation
  • Separate male and female norms are available for each occupational scale, reflecting the empirical reality that men and women in the same occupation sometimes endorse different interest patterns

Personal Style Scales

Beyond identifying what areas of work a person finds appealing, the Strong Interest Inventory also asks a subtler question: how does this person prefer to engage with work? The SII includes five Personal Style Scales that assess preferences in work style, capturing dimensions that cut across occupational content. Two clients might both score high on the Social theme and the Counseling occupational scale, yet differ dramatically on these stylistic dimensions — one preferring to lead group therapy sessions with bold directiveness, the other favoring quiet one-on-one reflective work. The Personal Style Scales help counselors anticipate these kinds of differences:

  • Work Style (prefers working with people vs. ideas/data/things)
  • Learning Environment (prefers academic/practical learning)
  • Leadership Style (prefers leading by example vs. directing others)
  • Risk Taking (prefers adventure and risk vs. caution and safety)
  • Team Orientation (prefers working as part of a team vs. independently)

These five dimensions round out the Strong profile, giving clinicians a way to discuss not just career direction but the day-to-day work conditions under which a client is most likely to thrive. A client who scores high on Risk Taking and low on Team Orientation, for example, might flourish as an independent entrepreneur far more than in a structured corporate environment — even if both settings technically match her occupational interests.

EPPP Note: The Strong is constructed primarily through empirical criterion keying -- the Occupational Scales were developed by comparing responses of satisfied workers to a general reference group. This is the same construction method used for the MMPI. The GOTs and BIS use homogeneous item clustering (items that intercorrelate).

Self-Directed Search (SDS)

Having explored the Strong Interest Inventory — with its rich layering of General Occupational Themes, Basic Interest Scales, Occupational Scales, and Personal Style Scales — you might wonder: what if a client needs career exploration but doesn't have access to a clinician to administer and interpret a complex instrument? What if a community college career center needs to assess hundreds of students in a single afternoon? Or what if a client simply wants to begin exploring career options on their own before committing to more in-depth counseling? These are precisely the scenarios that motivated John Holland to create something radically different from the typical professionally administered inventory.

The Self-Directed Search was developed by John Holland himself — the same theorist whose RIASEC model forms the backbone of the Strong Interest Inventory's General Occupational Themes. But whereas the Strong requires professional administration and computerized scoring, Holland designed the SDS as a self-administered, self-scored, and self-interpreted career exploration tool. It is, in essence, the most direct operationalization of Holland's RIASEC theory — a practical instrument that puts the theory directly into the hands of the person exploring their career options. First published in 1970, the SDS has been used by tens of millions of individuals worldwide and remains one of the most widely used career assessments in counseling, educational, and organizational settings.

Think of the difference this way: if the Strong Interest Inventory is like a comprehensive medical workup requiring a specialist to interpret the results, the SDS is more like a well-validated screening tool that an individual can complete in a waiting room and immediately begin using to guide their next steps. Both are valuable; they simply serve different purposes and contexts. Holland believed that career exploration should be accessible and empowering, and the SDS reflects that philosophy at every level of its design.

Structure

The SDS is organized into four sections, each designed to tap into a different facet of how a person relates to the world of work. Rather than relying on a single method of measurement, Holland intentionally built the instrument to capture interests, self-perceived competencies, occupational preferences, and self-estimated abilities — creating a multifaceted portrait of the individual's vocational personality. Understanding these four sections is essential for appreciating how the SDS translates Holland's theory into a practical assessment experience.

  • The SDS consists of four sections: Activities (liked/disliked), Competencies (can/cannot do), Occupations (appealing/not), and Self-Estimates (self-rated abilities)
  • Each section yields scores for all six RIASEC types
  • The three highest scores form the individual's three-letter Holland code (called the Summary Code)
  • The code is then used with the Occupations Finder or Dictionary of Holland Occupational Codes to identify matching occupations

To see how this works in practice, imagine a college sophomore named Marcus who completes the SDS during a career counseling workshop. In the Activities section, he endorses items like "design a poster" and "play a musical instrument" (Artistic), but also "organize files and records" (Conventional) and "teach children how to play a game" (Social). In the Competencies section, he indicates he can sketch a room's layout and write a short story, but cannot repair a car engine. For the Occupations section, he marks "interior designer" and "music teacher" as appealing. Finally, in Self-Estimates, he rates himself highly in artistic ability and teaching ability. When his scores across all four sections are tallied for each of the six RIASEC types, Artistic (A) emerges highest, followed by Social (S) and then Enterprising (E) — giving Marcus a Summary Code of ASE.

Marcus can then turn to the Occupations Finder, a companion booklet that lists over 1,300 occupations organized by their three-letter Holland codes. He looks up occupations coded ASE — and discovers options like art teacher, museum curator, and advertising creative director. He also explores nearby codes (AES, SAE) to broaden his search, a process Holland encouraged because adjacent codes on the hexagonal model represent related interest patterns. In just 20 to 30 minutes, Marcus has moved from vague uncertainty about his future to a concrete list of career possibilities grounded in a well-validated theoretical framework.

Strengths and Limitations

Like any assessment tool, the SDS presents trade-offs that clinicians and career counselors must weigh carefully. Its greatest asset — accessibility — is also the source of its most notable limitations. Understanding both sides helps practitioners decide when the SDS is the right choice and when a more comprehensive instrument like the Strong might better serve the client.

  • Strengths: Self-administered and quick (20-30 minutes); cost-effective for group use; strong theoretical foundation; well-validated
  • Limitations: Less detailed than the Strong; self-scoring can lead to errors; relies heavily on self-estimates that may be biased; less useful for individuals with undifferentiated profiles

The limitation regarding undifferentiated profiles deserves special attention because it frequently appears on examinations. Some individuals produce SDS profiles in which all six RIASEC scores are relatively similar — there is no clear "peak" to identify a dominant type. This pattern, called an undifferentiated or flat profile, makes it difficult to derive a meaningful Summary Code because the three-letter code is essentially arbitrary when scores are nearly equal. A client with an undifferentiated profile may be broadly interested in many activities (which can be a strength) or may be struggling with identity development and career indecision (which is clinically important to explore). In either case, the SDS alone provides limited guidance, and the clinician may need to supplement it with additional assessment or counseling strategies.

The self-scoring feature also warrants a note of caution. Research has shown that a meaningful percentage of individuals make arithmetic errors when tallying their own scores, sometimes resulting in an incorrect Summary Code. In high-stakes counseling situations — such as when a client is making a major career transition — clinicians may wish to verify the client's scoring or consider using the computerized version of the SDS, which eliminates this source of error. Despite these limitations, the SDS remains an invaluable tool for initial career exploration, group workshops, and settings where cost-effectiveness and client empowerment are priorities. When used thoughtfully and in combination with skilled counseling, it provides a remarkably efficient bridge between Holland's elegant theory and the practical challenge of helping people find meaningful work.

Kuder Career Search (KCS)

The Kuder Career Search (Zytowski, 2007) is the latest version of the Kuder interest inventories, which date back to Frederic Kuder's work in the 1930s. The Kuder approach differs from the Strong in its use of forced-choice triads.

Item Format

  • Each item presents three activities, and the examinee selects the most preferred and least preferred
  • This forced-choice format reduces the tendency toward acquiescence (saying "like" to everything) or social desirability
  • Scores are ipsative (within-person comparisons) rather than normative (compared to a reference group)

Activity Preference Scales

The KCS produces scores on six Activity Preference Scales that align with Holland's RIASEC themes, though Kuder uses different labels:

  • Nature (aligns with Realistic)
  • Science/Technical (aligns with Investigative)
  • Art/Communication (aligns with Artistic)
  • Social/Personal Services (aligns with Social)
  • Influence/Sales (aligns with Enterprising)
  • Organization/Management (aligns with Conventional)

Person Match

A unique feature of the KCS is the Person Match system, which compares the examinee's interest profile to those of satisfied individuals in various occupations. This is similar in concept to the Strong's Occupational Scales but uses a different matching algorithm.

Ipsative vs. Normative Scoring: Ipsative scores compare an individual's interests to their own other interests (within-person). Normative scores compare to a reference group (between-person). Ipsative scores from forced-choice instruments cannot be directly compared across individuals. This is a key limitation of the Kuder's forced-choice format.

Other Notable Interest Instruments

Career Assessment Inventory (CAI)

Developed by Charles Johansson, the CAI is structured similarly to the Strong but focuses on non-professional and skilled/technical occupations that typically require two years or less of postsecondary training. It fills a gap because the Strong focuses primarily on professional occupations requiring college education.

Jackson Vocational Interest Survey (JVIS)

Developed by Douglas Jackson using a construct validation approach (rather than empirical keying). It measures 34 basic interest scales organized into themes. It is notable for its strong psychometric properties and theoretical grounding.

Campbell Interest and Skill Survey (CISS)

Developed by David Campbell (who was also a key developer of the Strong), the CISS uniquely measures both interests and self-reported skills across the same domains. This allows for four quadrants of interpretation: Pursue (high interest, high skill), Develop (high interest, low skill), Explore (low interest, high skill), Avoid (low interest, low skill).

Psychometric Considerations

Validity of Interest Inventories

  • Concurrent validity: Individuals in a given occupation tend to score highest on the corresponding scale
  • Predictive validity: Interest scores measured in adolescence or young adulthood predict occupational choice years later at moderate levels (r ~ .30-.40)
  • Hit rates: The Strong correctly predicts occupational group membership in approximately 50% of cases over long follow-up periods

Stability of Interests

Interests are relatively stable over time, particularly after age 25. Test-retest reliabilities for the Strong GOTs range from .80-.92 over periods of 1-5 years. Interests are less stable in adolescence and early adulthood.

Gender Differences

Gender differences in vocational interests are among the largest in psychology. Meta-analyses show substantial differences on Realistic (males higher) and Social (females higher) dimensions, with smaller differences on other RIASEC types. The Strong addresses this by providing both same-sex and combined-sex norms.

EPPP Note: The use of same-sex vs. combined-sex norms on interest inventories is an important consideration. Same-sex norms compare the individual to their own gender group; combined norms compare to both genders. Using same-sex norms can expand the range of suggested occupations for an individual, particularly when gender-atypical interests are present.

Using Interest Inventories in Practice

Integrating Results in Career Counseling

Interest inventories are most useful when combined with other sources of information in a comprehensive career counseling process:

  • Interests + abilities: High interest without corresponding aptitude may lead to frustration; high aptitude without interest may lead to dissatisfaction. Comprehensive career assessment evaluates both.
  • Interests + values: Work values (e.g., autonomy, financial security, altruism) add a dimension beyond what interest inventories measure. Instruments like the Work Values Inventory can supplement interest results.
  • Interests + personality: Personality measures (e.g., NEO-PI-R, MBTI) can help explain why certain interests exist and how they might manifest in different work environments.
  • Interests + developmental context: Consider the client's age, life stage, career development stage (Super's Life-Span model), and prior work experiences when interpreting interest profiles.

Interests Across the Lifespan

Super's Life-Span, Life-Space theory recognizes that career development occurs across the lifespan and is influenced by changing roles and life circumstances. Interest inventories are most commonly used during the Exploration stage (ages 15-24) and at career transition points. However, interests themselves demonstrate remarkable stability -- among the most stable of all individual difference constructs after age 25.

Computer-Based and Online Inventories

Most major interest inventories are now available in computer-administered formats. The O*NET Interest Profiler is a free, government-developed interest inventory based on Holland's RIASEC model and linked to the extensive O*NET occupational database. It provides a convenient, no-cost alternative to commercial instruments, though it lacks the depth of the Strong or CISS.

Commonly Tested EPPP Summary

Now that we have explored the theoretical foundations, individual instruments, and practical applications of interest inventories, it is time to consolidate the most exam-critical points into a focused review. The EPPP frequently tests your ability to distinguish between instruments — their underlying models, scoring methods, and scale structures. What follows is a distillation of the high-yield concepts you are most likely to encounter on exam day. Think of this as your mental "quick-reference card": the essential distinctions that separate a correct answer from a plausible distractor.

One of the most effective strategies for mastering this material is to focus on what makes each instrument unique. The Strong Interest Inventory, the Self-Directed Search, and the Kuder inventories all measure vocational interests, yet they differ fundamentally in how they are constructed, scored, and interpreted. Knowing these differences — not just the instruments' names — is what the EPPP rewards. As you review each summary below, pay particular attention to the scoring methodology and the theoretical basis, as these are the features most commonly tested.

Holland's RIASEC: Realistic-Investigative-Artistic-Social-Enterprising-Conventional. Adjacent types most similar, opposite types most different. Congruence = person-environment match.

Remember Holland's hexagonal model as the organizing framework for nearly every major interest inventory. The RIASEC types are arranged so that adjacent types on the hexagon (e.g., Realistic and Investigative) share more psychological overlap than types positioned across from one another (e.g., Realistic and Social). The concept of congruence — the degree to which a person's interest profile matches the demands and culture of their work environment — is the central prediction of Holland's theory: higher congruence predicts greater job satisfaction, stability, and performance. On the exam, if a question asks about "person-environment fit" in the context of vocational psychology, Holland's congruence principle is almost always the answer.

Strong Interest Inventory: Three scale levels -- GOTs (6 RIASEC themes), BIS (30 specific interest areas), and Occupational Scales (260 scales, criterion-keyed). Also includes Personal Style Scales.

The Strong Interest Inventory is the most structurally complex instrument you need to know for the EPPP, and its layered scale system is a frequent source of test questions. The General Occupational Themes (GOTs) map directly onto Holland's six RIASEC types and provide the broadest view of a client's interests. The Basic Interest Scales (BIS) break those broad themes into 30 more specific clusters — for example, within the Investigative theme, a client might score high on Science but low on Mathematics. The Occupational Scales are criterion-keyed, meaning each scale was developed by comparing the response patterns of satisfied workers in a given occupation against a general reference sample. This empirical criterion-keying strategy — the same logic Edward Strong pioneered nearly a century ago — is what gives these scales their predictive power and distinguishes them from theoretically derived scales.

SDS: Holland's self-administered instrument. Produces a three-letter Holland code. Self-scored. Most direct application of RIASEC theory.

The Self-Directed Search (SDS) is the instrument most directly tied to Holland's theory, and the EPPP may test this link explicitly. Unlike the Strong, which layers multiple scoring strategies, the SDS is built entirely on the RIASEC model and was designed by Holland himself to be self-administered and self-scored — making it uniquely accessible. It produces a three-letter summary code (e.g., SAE for Social-Artistic-Enterprising) that clients can use to explore matching occupations in an accompanying Occupations Finder. If an exam question describes an instrument that a client can complete and interpret independently without professional scoring, the SDS is the most likely answer.

Kuder: Forced-choice triads produce ipsative scores. Person Match feature. Ipsative scores cannot be compared across individuals.

The Kuder inventories stand apart because of their forced-choice format, in which respondents choose the most and least preferred activity from sets of three options. This design produces ipsative scores — scores that reflect the relative strength of interests within a single individual rather than how that person compares to others. This is a critical distinction for the EPPP: because ipsative scores are interdependent (raising one score necessarily lowers another), they cannot be meaningfully compared across individuals the way normative scores can. The Kuder's innovative Person Match feature, which compares a test-taker's full interest profile to the profiles of satisfied workers, represents a creative workaround that extracts rich interpretive information from ipsative data.

Key distinction: The Strong uses empirical criterion keying (Occupational Scales) and homogeneous keying (GOTs, BIS). The SDS is based purely on Holland's theory. The Kuder uses forced-choice format producing ipsative scores.

This final distinction is arguably the single most important takeaway for the exam. When you encounter a question about interest inventories, your first mental move should be to identify the scoring and construction method. The Strong's Occupational Scales use empirical criterion keying (comparing a respondent's answers to those of criterion groups), while its GOTs and BIS use homogeneous keying (grouping items that statistically cluster together). The SDS bypasses empirical scale construction entirely, relying instead on Holland's theoretical model as its organizing structure. And the Kuder's forced-choice triads yield ipsative data that require fundamentally different interpretive rules. Remembering this three-way contrast — criterion-keyed versus theory-driven versus forced-choice ipsative — will equip you to handle the majority of EPPP questions about interest inventories with confidence.
