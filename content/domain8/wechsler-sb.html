Overview of the Wechsler Scales

The Wechsler intelligence scales are the most widely used individually administered intelligence tests in the world. Developed by David Wechsler beginning in 1939, the scales are based on Wechsler's definition of intelligence as "the aggregate or global capacity of the individual to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with his environment." The current editions include measures for adults, children, and preschoolers.

Wechsler's Definition: Intelligence is not a single ability but an aggregate of abilities. Wechsler also emphasized that non-intellective factors (motivation, persistence, emotional regulation) influence intelligent behavior. This holistic view influenced his decision to include both verbal and performance measures.

Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale - Fourth Edition (WAIS-IV)

The WAIS-IV (Wechsler, 2008) is the premier adult intelligence measure for ages 16-90. It yields a Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) and four index scores based on the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theoretical framework.

Subtest Descriptions

Each Wechsler subtest taps distinct cognitive abilities that contribute to one of four index scores. Understanding what each subtest measures is essential for accurate profile interpretation and identification of cognitive strengths and weaknesses.

Verbal Comprehension Subtests

  • Similarities: How are two words alike? Measures verbal abstract reasoning and concept formation
  • Vocabulary: Define words. Measures word knowledge, verbal expression, and crystallized ability
  • Information: General knowledge questions. Measures breadth of acquired knowledge and long-term memory
  • Comprehension (supplemental): Social judgment questions. Measures practical reasoning and social knowledge

Perceptual Reasoning Subtests

  • Block Design: Replicate patterns using red-and-white blocks within time limits. Measures visual-spatial analysis, construction, and nonverbal problem-solving. Often considered the best single nonverbal measure of g.
  • Matrix Reasoning: Select the missing element from a visual pattern. Measures fluid reasoning with minimal motor demands
  • Visual Puzzles: Identify pieces that combine to form a target design. Measures visual-spatial reasoning
  • Figure Weights (supplemental): Quantitative fluid reasoning using visual balance scale

Working Memory Subtests

  • Digit Span: Three conditions -- Forward (attention, short-term memory), Backward (working memory, mental manipulation), and Sequencing (working memory, cognitive flexibility)
  • Arithmetic: Mental math problems. Measures working memory, attention, and numerical reasoning

Processing Speed Subtests

  • Symbol Search: Visual scanning task -- does the target symbol match any in the row? Measures processing speed and visual discrimination
  • Coding: Transcribe symbol-number pairs using a key. Measures processing speed, visual-motor coordination, and incidental learning
EPPP Commonly Tested Subtests: Block Design is considered the best single nonverbal measure of g. Vocabulary is considered the best single verbal measure of g (and the single best overall predictor of FSIQ). Digit Span is the most commonly used subtest for assessing attention and working memory across clinical settings.

WAIS-IV Scoring

The psychometric foundation of the WAIS-IV rests on extensive factor analytic evidence reported in its Technical and Interpretive Manual. Both exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted across the standardization sample, and the results consistently supported a four-factor model corresponding to Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed. In confirmatory analyses, the four-factor solution demonstrated superior fit compared to alternative models, including one-factor (general intelligence only), two-factor (verbal/nonverbal), and three-factor solutions. A hierarchical model with a higher-order g factor and four first-order group factors provided the best overall fit to the data, aligning the WAIS-IV with the Cattell-Horn-Carroll theoretical framework.

These factor analytic findings have direct implications for clinical interpretation. Because the four-factor structure is empirically validated, clinicians can interpret the four index scores as measuring meaningfully distinct cognitive constructs rather than treating them as arbitrary subtest groupings. The factor loadings also confirm that the FSIQ, derived from the 10 core subtests, serves as a robust estimate of general intellectual ability (g). Subtests within each index load most strongly on their designated factor, though most subtests also carry moderate to strong loadings on the general factor, supporting the hierarchical interpretation that both domain-specific abilities and overall cognitive capacity contribute to test performance.

Example

In the confirmatory factor analysis, the four-factor hierarchical model yielded fit indices (e.g., CFI, RMSEA) that met or exceeded conventional standards for good model fit across all age groups in the normative sample. This cross-age consistency strengthens clinicians' confidence that the same four constructs are being measured whether the examinee is 16 or 85 years old.

  • Subtest scaled scores: M = 10, SD = 3 (range 1-19)
  • Index scores and FSIQ: M = 100, SD = 15
  • FSIQ is based on the 10 core subtests
  • Significant discrepancies between indices (e.g., VCI-PRI or WMI-PSI) may have clinical significance but must be interpreted in context

General Ability Index (GAI)

The GAI is composed of VCI + PRI subtests only, excluding WMI and PSI. It provides an estimate of general intellectual ability that is less influenced by processing speed and working memory. The GAI is useful when:

  • WMI or PSI scores are significantly lower than VCI/PRI (e.g., in ADHD, TBI, or processing disorders)
  • The clinician wants to estimate cognitive ability without the confound of processing speed deficits

The GAI is particularly informative when interpreting profiles of individuals with neurodegenerative conditions. In mild Alzheimer's disease, a characteristic WAIS-IV pattern emerges: patients typically achieve their highest scores on the Verbal Comprehension Index and their lowest scores on the Processing Speed Index. This pattern reflects the relative preservation of overlearned verbal knowledge and crystallized abilities in the early stages of dementia, while speeded cognitive processing — which depends heavily on the integrity of white matter pathways and frontal-subcortical circuits — deteriorates more rapidly. Working memory performance also tends to be compromised. Because the GAI excludes WMI and PSI, it can provide a more accurate estimate of premorbid intellectual functioning in Alzheimer's patients than the FSIQ, which would be disproportionately depressed by processing speed and working memory deficits. A large GAI-CPI discrepancy in an older adult may serve as one indicator warranting further neuropsychological evaluation for possible dementia.

Cognitive Proficiency Index (CPI)

The CPI combines WMI + PSI. A significant GAI-CPI discrepancy may indicate that processing efficiency is significantly lower than reasoning ability, which is clinically meaningful in conditions like ADHD, learning disabilities, and neurological impairment.

Nonverbal Learning Disability (NVLD)

A neurodevelopmental condition characterized by deficits in visual-spatial processing, nonverbal reasoning, and psychomotor coordination, alongside relatively intact or even strong verbal abilities. NVLD is not a formal DSM diagnosis but is widely recognized in neuropsychological practice.

Individuals with nonverbal learning disability (NVLD) present a characteristic cognitive profile on the Wechsler scales in which verbal comprehension abilities substantially exceed perceptual and spatial reasoning skills. On the WAIS-IV, this manifests as a significantly elevated Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) relative to the Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI). These individuals typically perform well on subtests such as Vocabulary, Similarities, and Information, reflecting strong crystallized language abilities, but struggle with Block Design, Matrix Reasoning, and Visual Puzzles, which require nonverbal problem-solving and spatial analysis.

This VCI > PRI discrepancy pattern is clinically significant and helps differentiate NVLD from other learning profiles. Clinicians should note, however, that a VCI-PRI split alone is insufficient for diagnosis—behavioral observations, academic history, and adaptive functioning data are also essential. The GAI may overestimate the functional capacity of individuals with NVLD because it includes PRI subtests on which they struggle, yet it still captures their verbal strengths. Conversely, the FSIQ may underrepresent their verbal abilities because it incorporates WMI and PSI scores, which can also be depressed in NVLD due to associated processing speed and graphomotor weaknesses.

Clinical Note

The VCI > PRI profile seen in NVLD contrasts sharply with profiles commonly observed in language-based learning disabilities (e.g., dyslexia), where PRI scores tend to exceed VCI scores. Recognizing these opposing patterns is critical for differential diagnosis. On the WISC-V, NVLD profiles can be examined with greater precision because visual-spatial processing (VSI) and fluid reasoning (FRI) are scored separately, allowing clinicians to determine whether deficits are primarily spatial, reasoning-based, or both.

Clinical Note: Traumatic Brain Injury and WAIS-IV Profile

Adults who have sustained traumatic brain injury (TBI) tend to show a WAIS-IV profile in which the Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI) is their highest score. This occurs because crystallized verbal knowledge — vocabulary, fund of information, and verbal reasoning skills — is typically acquired before the injury and stored in well-consolidated long-term memory networks that are relatively resistant to diffuse brain damage. In contrast, Processing Speed (PSI) and Working Memory (WMI) are highly sensitive to the diffuse axonal injury and frontal-subcortical disruption commonly seen in TBI. Perceptual Reasoning (PRI) may also be reduced due to demands on novel problem-solving and visual-spatial integration. The resulting pattern — elevated VCI relative to suppressed WMI and PSI — produces a significant GAI–CPI discrepancy that is clinically informative. When evaluating TBI patients, clinicians often report the GAI alongside the FSIQ to provide a more accurate estimate of premorbid intellectual capacity that is not artificially lowered by injury-related processing deficits.

Clinical Note: ADHD and WAIS-IV Profile

Individuals with Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) typically display a characteristic pattern on the WAIS-IV in which Verbal Comprehension (VCI) is their strongest index score, while Working Memory (WMI) and Processing Speed (PSI) — the components of the Cognitive Proficiency Index — are relatively depressed. This pattern reflects the core neurocognitive features of ADHD: sustained attention difficulties, reduced processing efficiency, and impaired executive control over working memory resources. Because VCI subtests (Similarities, Vocabulary, Information) rely on accumulated verbal knowledge and reasoning rather than timed performance or attentional control, they are relatively unaffected by ADHD symptoms. In contrast, WMI subtests require holding and manipulating information under attentional demands, and PSI subtests impose strict time limits that penalize individuals with attentional lapses and inconsistent response speed. Therefore, a 20-year-old diagnosed with ADHD would most likely earn their highest score on the VCI. A large GAI–CPI discrepancy in such cases supports the clinical interpretation that general reasoning ability is intact but cognitive efficiency is compromised.

Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children - Fifth Edition (WISC-V)

The WISC-V (Wechsler, 2014) is the children's version for ages 6-16:11. It expanded from four to five index scores:

IndexCore SubtestsCHC Ability
Verbal Comprehension (VCI)Similarities, VocabularyGc (Crystallized)
Visual Spatial (VSI)Block Design, Visual PuzzlesGv (Visual Processing)
Fluid Reasoning (FRI)Matrix Reasoning, Figure WeightsGf (Fluid Reasoning)
Working Memory (WMI)Digit Span, Picture SpanGsm (Short-Term Memory)
Processing Speed (PSI)Coding, Symbol SearchGs (Processing Speed)

Key Change from WISC-IV to WISC-V

The major structural change was splitting the WISC-IV's Perceptual Reasoning Index into two separate indices: Visual Spatial (VSI) and Fluid Reasoning (FRI). This provides better alignment with the CHC model by distinguishing visual-spatial processing (Gv) from fluid reasoning (Gf).

EPPP Comparison: WISC-V has 5 primary indices vs. WAIS-IV's 4. The WISC-V separates visual-spatial and fluid reasoning, while the WAIS-IV combines them in the PRI. Also note: the WISC-V includes Picture Span (a visual working memory task) as a core WMI subtest, expanding working memory assessment beyond the auditory-verbal modality.

Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence - Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV)

The WPPSI-IV (Wechsler, 2012) is designed for children ages 2:6 to 7:7. It has two age bands:

Ages 2:6-3:11

  • Three index scores: VCI, VSI, and Working Memory
  • Core subtests: Receptive Vocabulary, Information, Block Design, Object Assembly, Picture Memory
  • Shorter and more engaging for very young children

Ages 4:0-7:7

  • Five index scores: VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI, PSI
  • Includes Fluid Reasoning subtests (Matrix Reasoning, Picture Concepts)
  • Processing Speed subtests added (Bug Search, Cancellation)

Special Considerations

  • Subtests use manipulatives, colorful stimuli, and game-like formats to maintain the attention of young children
  • Administration time is shorter than the WISC-V or WAIS-IV
  • Test-retest reliability is somewhat lower than for older-child and adult versions (due to developmental variability at young ages)

Stanford-Binet Intelligence Scales, Fifth Edition (SB5)

The SB5 (Roid, 2003) is the latest revision of the oldest intelligence test in continuous use. Originally developed by Binet and Simon in 1905 (adapted by Terman at Stanford in 1916), the SB5 measures five factors across both verbal and nonverbal domains.

Composite Scores

  • Full Scale IQ (FSIQ): Based on all 10 subtests (M = 100, SD = 15)
  • Nonverbal IQ (NVIQ): Based on the 5 nonverbal subtests
  • Verbal IQ (VIQ): Based on the 5 verbal subtests
  • Five Factor Index scores (one for each factor)

Routing Procedure

The SB5 uses a unique routing procedure to establish the appropriate starting point for each subtest. Two routing subtests are administered first:

  • Nonverbal Fluid Reasoning (first nonverbal routing subtest)
  • Vocabulary (first verbal routing subtest)

These routing subtests determine the functional level (starting item set) for the remaining subtests, ensuring that the examinee is tested at an appropriate difficulty level and reducing floor/ceiling effects.

Floor and Ceiling Effects in Testing

A test's floor refers to its capacity to differentiate among examinees at the lowest ability levels. When a test has a limited floor, individuals with very low ability all receive similarly poor scores, making it impossible to distinguish meaningful differences among them. Conversely, a test's ceiling refers to its ability to discriminate among the highest-performing examinees. A limited ceiling means that highly capable individuals all cluster near the maximum score, obscuring genuine differences in ability. Both problems reduce the diagnostic precision of the instrument at the extremes of the ability distribution.

The SB5's routing procedure directly addresses floor and ceiling concerns by tailoring item difficulty to each examinee's functional level. Because examinees begin subsequent subtests at a difficulty level matched to their performance on the routing subtests, very low-ability individuals receive easier item sets that can capture fine-grained differences among them, while very high-ability individuals encounter sufficiently challenging items. The SB5's extended IQ range of 40 to 160 further mitigates these effects compared to instruments with narrower score ranges. This makes the SB5 especially well-suited for evaluating individuals at extreme ends of the distribution, such as those being assessed for intellectual giftedness or intellectual disability.

SB5 vs. Wechsler Scales

  • The SB5 has a broader age range (2-85+) than any single Wechsler scale
  • The SB5 measures each factor in both verbal and nonverbal domains (the Wechsler scales do not systematically do this)
  • The SB5 uses a routing procedure; the Wechsler scales use age-based starting points
  • The SB5 has a wider score range (extended IQ from 40 to 160), which is useful at the extremes
  • The Wechsler scales generally have more subtests and offer more detailed clinical information within each domain
Historical Note: The original Stanford-Binet introduced the concept of the Intelligence Quotient (IQ), originally computed as Mental Age / Chronological Age x 100 (the ratio IQ). Modern intelligence tests, including the SB5 and Wechsler scales, use deviation IQ scores (M = 100, SD = 15) rather than ratio IQs. The deviation IQ was introduced by Wechsler and later adopted by the Stanford-Binet.

The Flynn Effect

The Flynn Effect (Flynn, 1984, 1987) refers to the well-documented finding that IQ scores have been rising approximately 3 points per decade across populations over the past century. Key implications:

  • When a new edition of a test is normed, the normative sample performs better than the previous edition's sample, resulting in slightly lower scores on the new edition
  • Using an outdated test version can inflate IQ scores by approximately 3 points per decade since the norms were established
  • The Flynn Effect is larger for fluid intelligence measures (~5 points/decade) than for crystallized measures (~2 points/decade)
  • This has significant forensic implications, particularly in Atkins v. Virginia (2002) intellectual disability death penalty cases, where the version of the test used can affect whether an individual falls above or below the IQ 70 cutoff
EPPP Application: If a person was tested with the WAIS-III (normed in 1997) and obtained an FSIQ of 73, retesting with the WAIS-IV (normed in 2008) might yield an FSIQ of approximately 70 due to the Flynn Effect. This difference can have legal consequences in intellectual disability determinations.

Factor Structure and CHC Theory

Modern intelligence tests are organized around the Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) theory, which integrates Cattell and Horn's Gf-Gc theory with Carroll's three-stratum model:

  • Stratum III: General intelligence (g)
  • Stratum II: Broad abilities -- Gf (Fluid Reasoning), Gc (Crystallized Ability), Gv (Visual Processing), Gsm (Short-Term Memory), Gs (Processing Speed), Glr (Long-Term Retrieval), Ga (Auditory Processing), Gq (Quantitative Knowledge), and others
  • Stratum I: Narrow abilities (specific skills measured by individual subtests)

Gf vs. Gc

Research on cognitive training has revealed a notable relationship between working memory training and fluid intelligence. Studies, most notably by Jaeggi and colleagues (2008), demonstrated that when adults practiced intensive working memory tasks (such as the dual n-back paradigm) over multiple sessions, they showed measurable gains on fluid intelligence assessments — even when those Gf measures were structurally dissimilar to the training exercises. This finding was significant because it challenged the longstanding assumption that fluid reasoning capacity was essentially fixed and resistant to environmental intervention.

The theoretical explanation centers on the substantial cognitive overlap between working memory and fluid reasoning. Both abilities require maintaining and manipulating information in real time, inhibiting irrelevant stimuli, and managing attentional resources. By strengthening the executive control processes underlying working memory, training appears to produce transfer effects that generalize to novel problem-solving tasks characteristic of Gf measurement. The gains tended to be dose-dependent, with longer training periods producing larger improvements.

Clinical Note

While initial findings generated considerable enthusiasm, subsequent meta-analyses have produced mixed results regarding the durability and magnitude of far-transfer effects from working memory training to fluid intelligence. Clinicians should be cautious about overstating the benefits of commercial "brain training" programs. The evidence is strongest for structured, adaptive working memory interventions delivered over sustained periods, and even then, the extent to which gains persist after training cessation remains debated.

  • Fluid Intelligence (Gf): The ability to reason, solve novel problems, and identify patterns without relying on previously acquired knowledge. Largely biologically determined. Peaks in young adulthood and declines with age.
  • Crystallized Intelligence (Gc): Accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, and skills acquired through education and experience. Largely culturally and educationally influenced. Continues to grow throughout the lifespan (or at least remains stable into late life).
Aging Pattern: Gf declines with age (particularly after age 60-70), while Gc remains stable or increases through middle and later adulthood. This produces the "classic aging pattern" on the Wechsler scales: relatively preserved VCI with declining PSI and WMI scores. Processing speed (Gs) shows the earliest and most pronounced age-related decline.

Research on the lifespan trajectories of these two broad abilities reveals distinct developmental curves. Fluid intelligence (Gf) reaches its peak during the mid-20s and begins a gradual decline around age 30 to 40, with the rate of decline accelerating after age 60. This trajectory reflects the biological underpinnings of Gf — neuronal efficiency, processing speed, and the integrity of prefrontal cortical networks — all of which are vulnerable to normal age-related neurological changes. In contrast, crystallized intelligence (Gc) follows a markedly different course: it continues to accumulate throughout adulthood as individuals gain knowledge, expand vocabulary, and deepen expertise through ongoing education and life experience. Gc typically does not reach its peak until approximately age 60 to 70, after which it may plateau or show only modest decline, particularly in the absence of neurodegenerative disease. This divergence between Gf and Gc is the foundation of the classic aging pattern seen on intelligence tests and explains why older adults often perform well on knowledge-based verbal tasks while showing reduced scores on speeded, novel problem-solving measures.

The age-related decline in fluid intelligence is not simply a unitary phenomenon — research has identified processing speed and working memory as key mediating mechanisms. Salthouse's processing speed theory proposes that the slowing of basic cognitive operations with age creates a cascade effect: when mental processing takes longer, relevant information may decay before it can be utilized, and less information can be simultaneously activated. Similarly, age-related reductions in working memory capacity limit the ability to hold multiple elements in mind while reasoning through novel problems. Structural equation modeling studies have shown that when processing speed and working memory are statistically controlled, much of the age-related variance in Gf is accounted for. On the WAIS-IV, this is reflected in the observation that PSI and WMI scores show earlier and steeper declines than VCI, and that these declines closely track the trajectory of fluid reasoning losses across the lifespan.

Commonly Tested EPPP Summary

WAIS-IV: Ages 16-90. Four indices (VCI, PRI, WMI, PSI) + FSIQ. Subtest M = 10, SD = 3. Index/FSIQ M = 100, SD = 15. GAI = VCI + PRI (excludes working memory and speed).
WISC-V: Ages 6-16. Five indices (VCI, VSI, FRI, WMI, PSI). Separates Visual Spatial from Fluid Reasoning. Includes Picture Span for visual working memory.
SB5: Ages 2-85+. Five factors, each measured verbally and nonverbally. Uses routing procedure. Wider score range (40-160). Introduced the original IQ concept (ratio IQ, now replaced by deviation IQ).
Flynn Effect: ~3 IQ points per decade increase. Using outdated norms inflates scores. Forensically significant (Atkins v. Virginia). Larger for Gf than Gc.
Classic aging pattern: Gf and Gs decline with age; Gc remains stable or increases. On WAIS-IV: VCI preserved, PSI declines most.
