Overview of Personality Measures

Imagine you're a clinician meeting a new client for the first time. The referral question is broad — "personality assessment" — and you need to decide which tools to pull from your assessment toolkit. Do you hand the client a structured questionnaire with clear-cut response options, trusting that their self-report will reveal meaningful personality patterns? Or do you present an ambiguous inkblot or an unfinished sentence, hoping that what the client projects onto the stimulus will illuminate dynamics they couldn't — or wouldn't — articulate directly? This fundamental choice between structured and unstructured approaches has shaped the field of personality assessment for over a century, and understanding when and why to use each approach is essential to competent clinical practice.

Throughout your EPPP preparation, you've likely already encountered the MMPI family in depth — the gold standard of structured personality assessment with its extensive validity scales and empirically derived clinical profiles. This chapter moves beyond the MMPI to explore the broader landscape of personality measurement, covering both objective (self-report) personality inventories and projective (performance-based) techniques. Each instrument carries its own theoretical heritage, structural logic, and clinical niche — and for the EPPP, you'll need to know not just what these tests measure, but why they were designed the way they were, how they are scored and interpreted, and when one approach might be preferred over another.

The distinction between these two broad categories of personality assessment is more than academic — it reflects deep philosophical differences about how personality is best understood and measured. Objective inventories assume that people can meaningfully report on their own traits, behaviors, and emotional patterns when given well-constructed questions. Projective techniques, by contrast, were born from psychodynamic traditions that emphasize the role of unconscious processes — material that clients may not have direct access to through self-reflection alone. A client completing the NEO-PI-R might endorse items about their tendency toward anxiety or openness to new experiences, while the same client responding to a Rorschach inkblot might reveal patterns of thought organization or emotional regulation that operate below the surface of conscious awareness. Both kinds of data can be clinically valuable, but they answer different questions and carry different psychometric strengths and vulnerabilities.

Objective vs. Projective: Objective personality tests use structured, standardized items (typically true/false or Likert-scale) with empirically derived scoring, allowing for efficient and reliable quantification of personality traits and psychopathology. Projective tests present ambiguous stimuli — such as inkblots, incomplete sentences, or vague pictures — and assume that responses reveal underlying personality dynamics, unconscious conflicts, and thought organization. Because the stimuli lack a single "correct" interpretation, the way a person structures their response is thought to reflect their characteristic ways of perceiving and relating to the world. Modern terminology increasingly refers to projective tests as "performance-based" measures to reduce the theoretical assumptions embedded in the original name and to emphasize that these instruments assess how a person performs a perceptual or cognitive task rather than simply what they say about themselves.

As you work through the instruments in this chapter — from the trait-based NEO-PI-R to the deeply interpretive Rorschach and TAT — keep the ongoing debate about reliability and validity in mind. Objective measures tend to shine in psychometric rigor: high test-retest reliability, clear normative data, and straightforward administration. Projective measures, on the other hand, have faced persistent questions about interrater reliability and predictive validity, though structured scoring systems like the Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS) have substantially improved the empirical standing of some instruments. Understanding where each tool excels — and where its limitations lie — will not only prepare you for exam questions but also make you a more thoughtful, evidence-informed clinician. We begin with one of the most widely used and well-validated trait-based inventories in contemporary psychology.

NEO Personality Inventory-Revised (NEO-PI-R) and NEO-FFI-3

The NEO-PI-R (Costa & McCrae, 1992) is the standard measure of the Five-Factor Model (FFM) of personality, also known as the "Big Five." The NEO-FFI-3 is the abbreviated 60-item version.

The Five Factors (OCEAN/CANOE)

A major source of empirical support for the five-factor structure comes from the lexical approach to personality research. This approach is grounded in the lexical hypothesis, which holds that the most significant and universally recognized personality characteristics become encoded as single words in natural language. The reasoning is straightforward: traits that are important for social interaction and survival will be discussed frequently across cultures, and languages will develop specific terms to describe them. By systematically analyzing the personality-descriptive adjectives found in dictionaries and everyday language, researchers can identify the fundamental dimensions along which people differ.

Pioneering work by Allport and Odbert (1936) catalogued nearly 18,000 English-language trait terms, which were subsequently reduced through rational and statistical methods. Later researchers, including Norman, Goldberg, and others, applied factor analysis to large sets of trait adjectives rated by participants describing themselves or others. Across multiple independent studies and diverse languages — including English, German, Dutch, Czech, Turkish, and others — five broad factors consistently emerged. These factors correspond to the dimensions captured by the NEO-PI-R: Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness, and Conscientiousness.

Example

In a typical lexical study, researchers extract all personality-relevant adjectives from a comprehensive dictionary, have large samples of participants rate themselves or peers on these adjectives, and then factor-analyze the ratings. The consistent emergence of five factors across languages and cultures provides cross-cultural validation that the Big Five represent fundamental dimensions of human personality rather than artifacts of a particular language or theoretical framework.

Test Characteristics

  • NEO-PI-R: 240 items on a 5-point Likert scale; 5 domain scores and 30 facet scores
  • NEO-FFI-3: 60 items (12 per domain); domain scores only, no facets
  • Both self-report (Form S) and observer-report (Form R) versions available
  • Scores reported as T-scores (M = 50, SD = 10)
  • Includes three validity indices: Acquiescence, Nay-Saying, and Random Responding
EPPP Note: The NEO-PI-R is used for normal-range personality assessment, not psychopathology. It measures personality traits on continua, not diagnostic categories. Very high Neuroticism and very low Agreeableness or Conscientiousness may correlate with personality pathology, but the NEO was not designed as a clinical diagnostic tool.

Sixteen Personality Factor Questionnaire (16PF)

The 16PF (Cattell, Cattell, & Cattell, 1993, 5th ed.) was developed by Raymond Cattell using factor analysis to identify the fundamental dimensions (source traits) of personality.

The theoretical and developmental origins of the MBTI are rooted in two converging contributions. Jung's 1921 work Psychological Types proposed that individual differences in personality arise from innate preferences in how people perceive the world and make decisions. Jung described the attitudes of extraversion and introversion, along with four cognitive functions: sensing, intuition, thinking, and feeling. Katherine Cook Briggs had been independently studying personality differences before encountering Jung's typology, which she found aligned with her own observations. Together with her daughter, Isabel Briggs Myers, she spent decades translating Jung's theoretical framework into a practical self-report instrument. Myers conducted extensive empirical work during the 1940s through 1960s, field-testing items and refining the measure. The first MBTI manual was published in 1962 by the Educational Testing Service, and the instrument was later distributed by Consulting Psychologists Press (now CPP, Inc.). The Judging-Perceiving dimension was an original addition by Myers and Briggs that extended beyond Jung's original framework, reflecting an individual's preferred way of engaging with the external environment.

Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a widely used personality instrument rooted in Carl Jung's theory of psychological types. Developed by Isabel Briggs Myers and her mother, Katherine Cook Briggs, the MBTI classifies individuals along four bipolar dimensions:

Dimension Pole 1 Pole 2 Description
Attitude toward the world Extraversion (E) Introversion (I) Preference for focusing on the external world of people and activity versus the internal world of ideas and reflection
Perceiving function Sensing (S) Intuition (N) Preference for concrete, factual information versus patterns, possibilities, and abstract meaning
Judging function Thinking (T) Feeling (F) Preference for logical analysis versus personal values and impact on people when making decisions
Orientation to the outer world Judging (J) Perceiving (P) Preference for planned, structured approaches versus flexible, spontaneous approaches to life

An individual's preferences across these four dimensions combine to produce one of 16 personality types, each designated by a four-letter code (e.g., INTJ, ESFP). The MBTI is a typological rather than trait-based instrument — it categorizes people into discrete types rather than placing them along continuous dimensions. This typological approach is a key distinction from trait measures like the NEO-PI-R and 16PF.

The MBTI is one of the most frequently administered personality instruments in organizational, educational, and career counseling contexts. It is commonly used for team building, leadership development, and self-understanding exercises. However, the instrument has drawn substantial psychometric criticism. Test-retest reliability is moderate, with research showing that a significant percentage of individuals receive a different four-letter type upon retesting, particularly when their scores fall near the midpoint of a dimension. Additionally, the forced dichotomization of continuous preference distributions results in lost information, and the underlying type structure has not been consistently supported by factor-analytic research. Validity evidence for predicting job performance or clinical outcomes is limited.

Clinical Note

The MBTI is not appropriate for clinical diagnosis, psychopathology assessment, or personnel selection decisions. It is best used as a tool for self-exploration and understanding interpersonal differences. On the EPPP, recognize that the MBTI is based on Jungian typology, measures four bipolar preference dimensions yielding 16 types, and has notable limitations in reliability and construct validity compared to trait-based measures of personality.

Structure

  • 185 items measuring 16 primary factor scales and 5 global (second-order) factors
  • The 16 primary factors include: Warmth, Reasoning, Emotional Stability, Dominance, Liveliness, Rule-Consciousness, Social Boldness, Sensitivity, Vigilance, Abstractedness, Privateness, Apprehension, Openness to Change, Self-Reliance, Perfectionism, Tension
  • The 5 global factors approximate the Big Five: Extraversion, Anxiety, Tough-Mindedness, Independence, Self-Control
  • Scores are reported as sten scores (standard ten scores, M = 5.5, SD = 2, range 1-10)

Uses

  • Personnel selection and industrial-organizational psychology
  • Clinical assessment of personality style (not primarily a clinical/pathology instrument)
  • Career counseling and vocational guidance
  • Research on personality structure
EPPP Tip: The 16PF uses sten scores (1-10), not T-scores. Scores of 1-3 are low, 4-7 are average, and 8-10 are high. Cattell used factor analysis to derive the scales, making the 16PF a prime example of the factor-analytic approach to test construction.

Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-IV (MCMI-IV)

The MCMI-IV (Millon, Grossman, & Millon, 2015) is a self-report inventory specifically designed to assess personality disorders and clinical syndromes. It is directly tied to Theodore Millon's theory of personality and to the DSM diagnostic system.

When using the MCMI-IV and other self-report personality inventories in clinical practice, it is essential to recognize their role within a multi-method assessment approach. Self-report inventories such as the MCMI are most appropriately employed as initial screening tools for personality pathology. When a self-report measure yields elevated scores suggesting the presence of a personality disorder, best practice calls for following up with a semistructured diagnostic interview — such as the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Personality Disorders (SCID-5-PD) or the Personality Disorder Interview (PDI) — to confirm or rule out the diagnosis. Self-report inventories alone are insufficient for definitive diagnosis because they are subject to response biases, limited self-awareness, and item overlap across scales. A semistructured interview allows the clinician to probe for behavioral examples, assess duration and pervasiveness of traits, and apply clinical judgment to determine whether full diagnostic criteria are met.

Structure

  • 195 true/false items
  • Three types of scales:
    • Clinical Personality Pattern scales (15): Correspond to DSM personality disorders (e.g., Schizoid, Avoidant, Dependent, Histrionic, Narcissistic, Antisocial, Compulsive, Negativistic, Masochistic, Sadistic, Schizotypal, Borderline, Paranoid)
    • Clinical Syndrome scales (7): Generalized Anxiety, Somatic Symptom, Bipolar Spectrum, Persistent Depression, Alcohol Use, Drug Use, Post-Traumatic Stress
    • Severe Clinical Syndrome scales (3): Schizophrenic Spectrum, Major Depression, Delusional
  • Validity indices: Invalidity, Inconsistency, Disclosure, Desirability, Debasement

Scoring

  • Uses Base Rate (BR) scores rather than T-scores
  • BR scores are anchored to the prevalence (base rate) of each disorder in clinical populations
  • BR score of 75 = presence of the trait/syndrome; BR score of 85 = prominence (clinically significant)
  • This approach accounts for the fact that different disorders have different base rates in clinical settings
BR Scores vs. T-Scores: T-scores assume a normal distribution and compare an individual to a normative mean. BR scores are calibrated to the actual prevalence of disorders in the target population. Because personality disorders vary in prevalence, using BR scores helps avoid overdiagnosis of rare disorders and underdiagnosis of common ones.

Important Limitations

  • Normed only on clinical (psychiatric) populations -- should not be used with non-clinical samples
  • Tied specifically to Millon's theory; may not map perfectly onto all DSM conceptualizations
  • Scales overlap substantially (items shared across scales), which inflates inter-scale correlations
EPPP Commonly Tested: The MCMI is designed for use with clinical populations only. Using it with non-clinical populations (e.g., personnel selection) is inappropriate and may lead to over-pathologizing. Also remember: BR scores, not T-scores; cutoffs of 75 (present) and 85 (prominent).

Rorschach Inkblot Method

The Rorschach (Rorschach, 1921) consists of 10 symmetrical inkblots (5 achromatic, 2 red-and-black, 3 multicolored) presented one at a time. The examinee describes what they see in each blot. Two major scoring systems are used today.

Exner's Comprehensive System (CS)

The Comprehensive System (Exner, 2003) standardized Rorschach administration, scoring, and interpretation by integrating elements from earlier systems (Beck, Klopfer, Hertz, Piotrowski, Rapaport).

  • Administration: Two phases -- Response Phase (free association to each card) and Inquiry Phase (examiner clarifies what and where the examinee saw each percept)
  • Scoring categories: Location (W = whole, D = common detail, Dd = unusual detail), Determinants (form, color, shading, movement), Content (human, animal, anatomy, etc.), Popular responses, Organizational Activity (Z-scores)
  • Structural Summary: A quantitative compilation of all coded scores, including ratios, percentages, and derived indices

Key CS Indices

  • Erlebnistypus (EB): Ratio of human movement (M) to weighted color responses (WSumC) -- reflects whether the individual is introverted (M > WSumC), extraverted (WSumC > M), or ambitent
  • Lambda (L): Ratio of pure form responses to all other responses -- high Lambda suggests avoidant, simplistic approach to complexity
  • X-% and X+%: Measures of perceptual accuracy (reality testing). X+% = conventional form quality; X-% = distorted form quality. Elevated X-% suggests impaired reality testing.
  • SCZI / PTI (Perceptual-Thinking Index): Identifies thought disorder and perceptual distortion associated with psychotic processes
  • DEPI (Depression Index): Identifies features associated with affective disturbance
  • S-CON (Suicide Constellation): Risk index for suicidal behavior (one of the best-validated indices)

Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS)

The R-PAS (Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard, & Erdberg, 2011) was developed to address psychometric criticisms of the CS. Key improvements include:

  • Standardized response process: Prompts examinees to give 2-3 responses per card (reduces variability in number of responses)
  • International normative reference data (more diverse than CS norms)
  • Evidence-based variable selection (retained variables with the strongest empirical support; dropped poorly validated ones)
  • Standard score profiles (similar to MMPI profiles) for easier interpretation
  • Updated scoring guidelines and reduced complexity

Confabulation on the Rorschach

Confabulation is a special score applied when an examinee perceives a specific detail of the inkblot accurately but then inappropriately generalizes that perception to encompass the entire blot. For example, a respondent might correctly identify a small area as resembling claws and then declare that the whole blot is a giant crab, despite the overall contour bearing no resemblance to that percept.

This response pattern reflects a significant breakdown in perceptual-cognitive processing. The individual fails to check the initial impression against the remaining visual data, instead allowing a single detail to dominate and distort the broader percept. Confabulatory responses are clinically significant because they are associated with thought disorder stemming from either organic brain pathology (e.g., traumatic brain injury, dementia, or other neurological conditions) or functional psychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia). In both cases, the underlying mechanism involves impaired reality testing and deficient cognitive control — the inability to regulate, inhibit, and correct an initial perceptual hypothesis when it does not fit the available stimulus information.

Confabulation should be distinguished from other Rorschach coding categories. A DW response (detail-to-whole) is the formal scoring designation for confabulation, where a detail (D) location is extended to a whole (W) response without adequate justification. This contrasts with a simple poor-form whole response (W with minus form quality), in which the examinee attempts to use the entire blot from the start but produces a distorted percept. The presence of multiple confabulatory responses in a protocol significantly raises concern about organic or psychotic processes and warrants further neuropsychological or diagnostic evaluation.

Clinical Note

When confabulatory responses appear alongside other indicators of disordered thinking — such as elevated X-%, contamination responses, or an elevated Perceptual-Thinking Index — clinicians should consider both neurological and psychiatric etiologies and pursue appropriate differential diagnostic procedures.

EPPP Note: The Rorschach has been highly controversial. Meta-analyses (Mihura et al., 2013) found that some Rorschach variables have validity comparable to MMPI scales (e.g., Thought Disorder Index, M- for disordered thinking) while others lack sufficient support. The R-PAS specifically retained only empirically supported variables.

Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

The TAT (Murray, 1943) consists of 31 cards (20 administered per session) depicting ambiguous interpersonal scenes. The examinee tells a story about each card, including what is happening, what led up to it, what the characters are thinking and feeling, and what the outcome will be.

Theoretical Basis

The TAT is based on Murray's need-press theory. Responses are interpreted in terms of:

  • Needs: Internal drives and motives of the protagonist (who is assumed to represent the examinee)
  • Press: Environmental forces acting on the protagonist (reflecting the examinee's perceived environment)
  • Thema: The interaction between needs and press -- the story's central conflict and resolution

Scoring and Interpretation

  • The TAT lacks a universally accepted scoring system, which is a major psychometric limitation
  • Notable systems include: Murray's need-press analysis, Bellak's system, McClelland's need for achievement (nAch) scoring, Westen's SCORS (Social Cognition and Object Relations Scale)
  • SCORS (Westen): The best-validated TAT scoring system; assesses complexity of representations of people, affective quality of relationships, emotional investment in relationships, and understanding of social causality
EPPP Key Point: The TAT is primarily used to assess interpersonal themes, motivations, and object relations rather than for psychodiagnosis. McClelland's nAch scoring system is the most empirically supported specific scoring method. Westen's SCORS has the best support for clinical use.

Sentence Completion Tests

Sentence completion tests present the examinee with sentence stems (e.g., "I wish...", "My mother...", "I feel guilty about...") to be completed. They are considered semi-projective because the stimuli provide more structure than inkblots or pictures.

Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS)

The Edwards Personal Preference Schedule (EPPS), developed by Allen Edwards (1959), is a self-report personality inventory grounded in Henry Murray's theory of needs. The instrument measures 15 fundamental psychological needs that Murray proposed as drivers of human behavior. These needs include achievement (the drive to accomplish difficult tasks), autonomy (the desire for independence), affiliation (the need for close interpersonal connections), dominance (the wish to direct and influence others), deference (willingness to follow authority), nurturance (caring for others), succorance (seeking support from others), exhibition (attracting attention), order (preference for structure and organization), abasement (self-blame and guilt), endurance (persistence on tasks), intraception (analyzing one's own motives and feelings), change (seeking novelty), heterosexuality (interest in romantic/sexual relationships), and aggression (expressing hostility or criticism).

A distinctive feature of the EPPS is its use of a forced-choice (ipsative) format. Each item presents two statements reflecting different needs, and the respondent must select the one that is more personally characteristic. This ipsative design was specifically intended to reduce the influence of social desirability bias, since paired statements are matched for their social desirability value. However, the forced-choice format produces ipsative scores, meaning that each person's need scores are relative to one another rather than to a normative group. A high score on one need necessarily lowers scores on others. This limits the ability to make between-person comparisons and restricts the use of certain statistical analyses (e.g., factor analysis, correlation with external criteria).

Clinical Note

Because the EPPS yields ipsative (within-person) rather than normative scores, it is best used to identify an individual's relative hierarchy of needs — which motives are strongest compared to their own other motives — rather than to compare one person's need strength to that of a reference group. This distinction is critical for interpreting EPPS profiles correctly and is a commonly tested psychometric concept on the EPPP.

Major Instruments

  • Rotter Incomplete Sentences Blank (RISB): 40 stems scored on a 7-point scale from positive adjustment (0-1) to maladjustment (4-6). Yields a total adjustment score. Has adequate psychometric support.
  • Washington University Sentence Completion Test (WUSCT): Developed by Loevinger to measure ego development. Responses are scored for developmental stage (Impulsive, Self-Protective, Conformist, Conscientious, Autonomous, Integrated).

Projective Drawings

Projective drawing techniques ask the examinee to draw specific subjects. Interpretation is based on features of the drawing (size, placement, detail, pressure, omissions). These techniques are among the most controversial in clinical assessment.

Draw-A-Person (DAP)

  • Developed by Karen Machover (1949) based on the assumption that drawings reflect self-concept and body image
  • Features such as size, detail, placement, and erasures are interpreted qualitatively
  • The DAP:SPED (Draw-A-Person: Screening Procedure for Emotional Disturbance) is a standardized scoring system with better psychometric properties than clinical interpretation

House-Tree-Person (HTP)

  • Developed by John Buck (1948)
  • The examinee draws a house, a tree, and a person; each drawing is then discussed in a post-drawing inquiry
  • The house is said to represent family/home life, the tree represents growth and development, and the person represents self-concept
  • Interpretation is primarily qualitative and lacks standardized scoring

Kinetic Family Drawing (KFD)

  • Developed by Burns and Kaufman (1972)
  • The examinee draws their family doing something together
  • Used primarily with children to assess family dynamics, roles, and relationships
EPPP Critical Point: Projective drawings have generally poor reliability and validity for personality or diagnostic assessment. However, they remain widely used in clinical practice, particularly with children. On the EPPP, you should recognize that drawing techniques have limited empirical support and are best used as clinical tools to generate hypotheses rather than as diagnostic instruments.

Commonly Tested EPPP Summary

Having explored projective drawing techniques and the controversies surrounding their interpretation, we now shift to a broader perspective — pulling together the key personality measures and assessment tools most frequently tested on the EPPP. This section serves as both a consolidation of what you've learned throughout this chapter and a quick-reference guide that highlights the essential facts, distinctions, and clinical nuances you'll need at exam time. We begin with a measure that may seem out of place in a personality chapter but appears regularly on the EPPP: an infant cognitive assessment with surprising predictive power.

Fagan Test of Infant Intelligence (FTII)

The Fagan Test of Infant Intelligence is a measure designed for infants aged 3 to 12 months that assesses visual recognition memory by measuring preferential attention to novel versus previously seen stimuli. Developed by Joseph Fagan, the test is based on the observation that cognitively intact infants naturally look longer at unfamiliar images after being habituated to familiar ones.

Imagine a six-month-old sitting on a caregiver's lap, gazing at a photograph of a face. After a brief exposure period — long enough for the infant to encode the image — the examiner presents two photographs side by side: the familiar face and a brand-new one. Where does the baby look? That simple behavioral choice turns out to be remarkably revealing. During administration, infants are first shown a photograph of a face for a standardized exposure period, allowing them to encode and habituate to the stimulus. They are then presented with a paired display containing both the familiar face and a novel face. The key metric is novelty preference — the proportion of total looking time directed toward the new stimulus. Higher novelty preference scores indicate more efficient information processing and encoding, reflecting the infant's ability to form mental representations and discriminate between stored and new information.

Why does this matter for clinical practice and the EPPP? Because longitudinal research has demonstrated that selective attention to novelty during infancy is a meaningful predictor of later cognitive performance. Studies have confirmed that infants who show stronger preferences for novel stimuli tend to score higher on standardized IQ measures during childhood, adolescence, and into young adulthood. This finding is significant because traditional sensorimotor-based infant assessments (e.g., the Bayley Scales) show notoriously poor predictive validity for later intelligence — a frustrating limitation that clinicians have grappled with for decades. The FTII's information-processing approach captures something different: encoding efficiency, a cognitive capacity that has genuine continuity with later intellectual functioning. For the EPPP, remember this key distinction: sensorimotor infant tests predict later IQ poorly, but the FTII's novelty preference paradigm predicts it meaningfully better.

Clinical Note

The Fagan Test is particularly useful as a screening tool for cognitive risk because it does not depend on motor responses, making it appropriate for infants with physical disabilities. Reduced novelty preference may serve as an early indicator of intellectual disability or developmental delay, prompting referral for further evaluation and early intervention services. This motor-free quality also makes it valuable for assessing infants with cerebral palsy or other conditions that would compromise performance on tasks requiring reaching, grasping, or manipulation.

Now let's turn to the personality measures themselves. The following summaries distill the most EPPP-relevant facts about each instrument. As you review them, pay special attention to what differentiates each measure: the population it was designed for, its scoring approach, its theoretical foundation, and its primary clinical use. These distinctions are precisely what the exam tests.

NEO-PI-R: Big Five (OCEAN): Neuroticism, Extraversion, Openness, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness. 240 items, 30 facets. Normal-range personality, not pathology.

The NEO-PI-R is the gold standard for assessing normal-range personality dimensions. Remember that it was developed by Costa and McCrae to measure the Big Five — not to diagnose psychopathology. Each of the five broad domains breaks down into six specific facets (yielding 30 facets total across 240 items), allowing for a nuanced personality profile. A critical EPPP distinction: if the question describes a non-clinical population and asks about normal personality traits, the NEO-PI-R is your answer. If the question involves clinical or pathological personality, look elsewhere.

16PF: Cattell's factor-analytic test. 16 primary factors, 5 global factors. Uses sten scores (1-10). Primarily used in I/O and career settings.

Raymond Cattell built the 16PF from the ground up using factor analysis, distilling thousands of personality descriptors into 16 primary source traits. Results are reported as sten scores — a standardized ten-point scale ranging from 1 to 10, with a mean of 5.5. On the EPPP, you should associate the 16PF with industrial/organizational and career counseling contexts rather than clinical diagnosis. If a question mentions sten scores or career-related personality assessment, think Cattell's 16PF.

MCMI-IV: Clinical populations only. Uses Base Rate scores (75 = present, 85 = prominent). Personality disorder + clinical syndrome scales. Tied to Millon's theory.

The MCMI-IV is Theodore Millon's instrument, and two facts about it appear on the EPPP with striking regularity. First, it is designed exclusively for clinical populations — administering it to non-clinical individuals is inappropriate and will yield misleading results. Second, it uses Base Rate (BR) scores rather than T-scores, calibrated to reflect the actual prevalence of disorders in clinical settings. A BR score of 75 suggests a trait or syndrome is present, while 85 indicates it is prominent. The entire instrument is anchored to Millon's evolutionary theory of personality, making it unique among personality inventories in its tight theoretical integration.

Rorschach: 10 inkblots. CS (Exner) and R-PAS scoring systems. Key variables: EB, Lambda, X-%, S-CON. R-PAS uses standardized response process and evidence-based variable selection.

The Rorschach Inkblot Test consists of 10 symmetrical inkblots and has been scored using two major systems: Exner's Comprehensive System (CS) and the newer Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS). For the EPPP, know these key variables: the Experience Balance (EB) compares human movement to weighted color responses, Lambda reflects psychological complexity versus simplification, X-% indexes distorted perception, and S-CON is a suicide constellation. R-PAS was developed to address criticisms of the Comprehensive System by using standardized administration (including an optimal response range), selecting variables based on empirical evidence, and providing international reference data.

TAT: Murray's need-press theory. 20 ambiguous scenes. No standard scoring system. McClelland's nAch and Westen's SCORS are best validated.

Henry Murray's Thematic Apperception Test asks examinees to tell stories about ambiguous scene cards, revealing underlying needs (internal motivations) and press (environmental forces). A frequent EPPP point: unlike the Rorschach, the TAT has no universally accepted standard scoring system. However, two research-validated approaches stand out — McClelland's system for scoring need for Achievement (nAch), and Westen's Social Cognition and Object Relations Scale (SCORS), which assesses the quality of mental representations of self and others.

Projective drawings (DAP, HTP): Poor reliability and validity. Hypothesis-generating only. Should not be used for diagnosis in isolation.

As discussed in the preceding section, projective drawing techniques such as the Draw-A-Person (DAP) and House-Tree-Person (HTP) remain among the most commonly used yet most poorly supported assessment tools in clinical practice. The research consensus is clear: these techniques demonstrate poor reliability and limited validity. They may serve a useful role as rapport builders or as hypothesis-generating tools early in an assessment, but they should never be used as the sole basis for diagnostic conclusions. On the EPPP, if a question asks about the psychometric standing of projective drawings, "poor reliability and validity" is almost always the correct answer.
