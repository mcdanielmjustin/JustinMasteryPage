Exposure-Based Treatments for Anxiety

Imagine a client who hasn't driven on a highway in three years — not since a terrifying near-accident that left her gripping the steering wheel with white knuckles at every merge lane. She knows, intellectually, that millions of people drive safely every day. Yet every time she approaches an on-ramp, her heart pounds, her palms sweat, and she takes the next side street instead. Avoidance has become her default coping strategy, and it has progressively shrunk her world. How do we help her reclaim it? The answer, supported by decades of research across virtually every anxiety disorder, centers on one deceptively simple principle: she must face what she fears.

Exposure therapy stands as the gold-standard behavioral intervention for anxiety disorders precisely because it targets the mechanism that maintains fear — avoidance. The core principle requires systematic confrontation with feared stimuli in the absence of the feared consequence. When our client drives on the highway and no catastrophic accident occurs, two powerful learning processes unfold. First, habituation takes hold: her fear response diminishes both within the session (she notices her anxiety dropping from a 9 to a 4 after 20 minutes of highway driving) and between sessions (each subsequent drive starts at a lower anxiety level). Second, inhibitory learning occurs as her brain forms new, non-threat associations that compete with the original fear memory — the highway becomes linked with "I drove safely" rather than exclusively with "I almost died."

These processes rest on classical conditioning principles that you likely remember from earlier chapters. At some point, the feared stimulus (CS — the highway) became paired with a threatening or aversive experience (US — the near-accident), producing a conditioned fear response (CR — panic at the on-ramp). Exposure works by allowing extinction of this learned association. The client encounters the CS repeatedly without the US, and the conditioned fear gradually weakens. Understanding this mechanism matters not only for treatment planning but also for explaining the rationale to anxious clients who understandably ask, "Why would I deliberately do the thing that terrifies me?"

Types of Exposure

Clinicians draw on several distinct exposure formats, each suited to different clinical presentations and practical constraints. A therapist treating a specific phobia of dogs might walk with the client to a dog park, while a therapist helping a combat veteran process traumatic memories might guide detailed visualization of the index trauma. The table below summarizes the four primary modalities and their most common applications.

Notice that these modalities are not mutually exclusive. A comprehensive treatment plan for panic disorder with agoraphobia might combine interoceptive exposure (to address fear of bodily sensations), in vivo exposure (to address avoidance of crowded stores), and imaginal exposure (to process a particularly frightening past panic attack). Selecting the right combination requires careful functional assessment of what, exactly, the client fears and avoids.

Systematic Desensitization vs. Flooding

Two classic approaches to structuring exposure sessions represent opposite ends of the intensity spectrum, and understanding their differences is essential for both the EPPP and clinical decision-making. Systematic desensitization, developed by Joseph Wolpe in 1958, takes a gentle, graduated approach. Wolpe built the method on the principle of reciprocal inhibition — the idea that anxiety and deep relaxation are mutually incompatible physiological states. If a client can maintain relaxation while imagining a feared scenario, the anxiety response is inhibited and gradually weakened. In practice, the client first learns progressive muscle relaxation over one or more sessions, then collaborates with the therapist to construct a fear hierarchy — a rank-ordered list of feared situations rated using Subjective Units of Distress (SUDS) scores from 0 to 100. Treatment progresses step by step: the client imagines the lowest item on the hierarchy while remaining deeply relaxed, advancing to the next item only after anxiety at the current level has subsided. Picture a client with a spider phobia who starts by imagining a tiny spider across a large room (SUDS = 15) and, over several sessions, works up to holding a tarantula in her hands (SUDS = 95).

Flooding takes the opposite approach — it involves prolonged exposure to the most anxiety-provoking stimulus without any graduated hierarchy, relying on habituation through extended, uninterrupted contact with the feared stimulus. Unlike systematic desensitization, flooding does not incorporate relaxation training and begins at high anxiety levels. A therapist using flooding with our spider-phobic client might begin the very first session by placing a live spider on the table in front of her and keeping it there for an extended period. Here is the critical clinical point: the therapist must ensure that flooding sessions last long enough for the client's anxiety to decrease substantially. Terminating a flooding session prematurely — while anxiety remains at its peak — can actually sensitize the client and increase fear through a process called incubation. In other words, cutting short a high-intensity exposure teaches the client that escape was necessary, reinforcing the fear rather than extinguishing it. This is one of the most important clinical cautions on the EPPP.

Implosive therapy, developed by Stampfl, represents an unusual variant that uses imaginal flooding infused with psychodynamic content. The therapist describes the feared scenario in vivid, exaggerated detail, sometimes weaving in hypothesized unconscious themes — for instance, asking a client with a snake phobia to imagine a snake crawling into her mouth while linking the imagery to unresolved aggressive impulses. Because of its theoretical eclecticism and limited empirical support, implosive therapy is rarely used in contemporary clinical practice, but it still appears on licensing examinations as a historical reference point.

Graduated vs. Intensive Exposure:

Research comparing graduated and intensive (massed) exposure formats generally shows equivalent outcomes, though intensive formats may produce faster results. The critical variable is that exposure must be of sufficient duration and intensity to promote new learning. Brief exposure sessions that are terminated prematurely (before habituation or expectancy violation occurs) may worsen anxiety. Session duration of at least 60-90 minutes is typically recommended for prolonged exposure exercises.

The clinical note above highlights a theme that runs through all exposure work: the dose matters. Think of exposure like an antibiotic — a partial course can leave the infection (or in this case, the fear) stronger than before. Whether a clinician chooses a graduated or intensive format, the commitment to sufficient duration and intensity determines whether the client experiences therapeutic extinction or iatrogenic sensitization.

Inhibitory Learning Model:

For decades, clinicians assumed that successful exposure "erased" the original fear memory through habituation. The contemporary inhibitory learning model (Craske et al., 2014) offers a more nuanced and empirically supported account. This model proposes that exposure does not erase the original fear memory at all — rather, it creates a new, competing inhibitory association (CS–no US) that coexists alongside the old one. The original fear memory remains intact, which explains why fear can return after successful treatment (through spontaneous recovery, renewal, or reinstatement). This model has largely replaced the habituation model as the prevailing theoretical account of exposure therapy. To maximize the strength of the new inhibitory learning, clinicians can employ several specific strategies:

  • Expectancy violation: Maximizing the mismatch between expected and actual outcomes during exposure
  • Deepened extinction: Combining multiple fear cues during a single exposure trial
  • Variability: Varying exposure parameters (context, duration, intensity) across sessions
  • Occasional reinforced extinction: Occasionally including the feared outcome during exposure to strengthen new learning
  • Affect labeling: Verbally labeling the emotional experience during exposure
  • Retrieval cues: Using cues from the exposure context in everyday life to trigger the inhibitory memory

The inhibitory learning model carries profound practical implications. Under the older habituation framework, clinicians tracked within-session fear reduction as the primary index of successful exposure — if SUDS dropped during the session, treatment was "working." The inhibitory learning model shifts the focus to expectancy violation: what matters most is whether the client's feared prediction failed to come true. A client whose anxiety stays elevated throughout an exposure session but who learns that the catastrophic outcome never materialized may actually achieve stronger long-term gains than one whose SUDS dropped smoothly to zero. This reframing changes how clinicians design, sequence, and evaluate exposure trials.

CBT for Panic Disorder

Consider a 28-year-old emergency room nurse who presents after her third visit to the ER — this time as a patient, not a provider. She was convinced she was having a heart attack: her heart raced, her chest tightened, and tingling spread through her hands. Cardiac workup was negative, again. She now monitors her heart rate constantly, avoids caffeine and exercise, and has started calling in sick to avoid the stress of work. She is caught in the classic maintenance cycle of panic disorder — and CBT directly targets every link in that chain.

CBT for panic disorder, as developed by Clark (1986) and refined by Barlow and Craske, weaves together four integrated components. Treatment begins with psychoeducation about the nature of panic — specifically, the "fear of fear" model, which helps clients understand that panic attacks represent a misfiring alarm system, not a medical emergency. The therapist then introduces cognitive restructuring to challenge the catastrophic misinterpretations of bodily sensations that fuel the cycle (e.g., "My racing heart means I'm having a heart attack" becomes "My racing heart is an adrenaline response that is uncomfortable but not dangerous"). The treatment's most distinctive element is interoceptive exposure, in which the therapist deliberately induces the very bodily sensations the client dreads. Finally, in vivo exposure addresses avoidance of situations the client has been fleeing — the gym, the coffee shop, the crowded ER shift. Together, these components dismantle the maintenance cycle in which normal bodily sensations trigger catastrophic misinterpretation, producing anxiety that intensifies the sensations, which fuels further misinterpretation in an escalating spiral.

Interoceptive Exposure Exercises:

Common exercises include: hyperventilation (30 seconds) to induce dizziness and tingling; breathing through a thin straw to create air hunger; spinning in a chair to produce dizziness; running in place to elevate heart rate; staring at a spot on the wall to induce derealization. Each exercise is designed to provoke the specific bodily sensations the client fears. The client rates the similarity of each exercise to their natural panic symptoms and begins exposure with exercises most closely matching their feared sensations.

For our ER nurse, interoceptive exposure might begin with running in place for two minutes — the exercise most closely mimicking her feared heart-racing sensation. She discovers that her heart rate increases, just as it does during a panic attack, but that no heart attack follows. With repeated trials, the sensation loses its threat value. This is expectancy violation in action: she predicted catastrophe, and reality delivered mere discomfort.

CBT for Social Anxiety Disorder

While panic disorder centers on fear of internal sensations, social anxiety disorder revolves around a fundamentally interpersonal fear — the dread of being judged, embarrassed, or humiliated in front of others. A graduate student who cannot raise her hand in seminar, a manager who eats lunch alone in his car to avoid the break room, a talented musician who has never performed publicly despite years of training — each represents the profound functional impairment that social anxiety can produce. Cognitive-behavioral treatment for this disorder targets the cognitive distortions, avoidance behaviors, and self-protective strategies that maintain the fear cycle.

Treatment typically integrates three core components: cognitive restructuring targeting fears of negative evaluation ("Everyone will notice my hands shaking and think I'm incompetent"), behavioral experiments designed to test these catastrophic predictions in real-world settings, and graduated in vivo exposure to feared social situations. Among the most well-established protocols is Heimberg's Cognitive Behavioral Group Therapy (CBGT), which harnesses the therapy group itself as a built-in exposure environment. Group members serve as audience, conversation partners, and feedback providers for in-session simulated exposures — a client practices giving a toast while group members observe, then the group collaboratively examines whether the predicted catastrophe (total humiliation, visible trembling, mind going blank) actually occurred. Clark and Wells developed a complementary individual cognitive therapy model that places particular emphasis on reducing self-focused attention and eliminating safety behaviors during social interactions. For example, a client who always holds a cold drink to hide sweaty palms would be asked to enter a social situation without this crutch, discovering that the feared sweating either doesn't occur or goes unnoticed by others.

Exposure and Response Prevention for OCD

Imagine a client who knows — truly knows, at an intellectual level — that her hands are clean. She washed them four minutes ago. But the thought returns, insistent and electric: What if I touched something contaminated? What if I make my children sick? The anxiety mounts until it becomes unbearable, and she washes again. And again. Her hands are raw and cracked, her mornings consumed by rituals that stretch what should be a ten-minute routine into an hour-long ordeal. She desperately wants to stop, but every time she resists the urge to wash, the distress feels catastrophic — and the relief that follows each wash convinces her nervous system that the ritual was necessary. This is the reinforcement trap at the heart of obsessive-compulsive disorder, and it is precisely what the gold-standard treatment for OCD is designed to dismantle.

Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) is the first-line psychotherapy for OCD, supported by decades of research and endorsed by every major clinical guideline for the disorder. Having explored how exposure-based principles operate across anxiety disorders — from the graduated hierarchies of systematic desensitization to the real-world confrontations of in vivo exposure for social anxiety — we now turn to their most precise and structured application. ERP takes the core logic of exposure therapy and pairs it with a second, equally critical element: the deliberate prevention of the rituals that maintain the disorder. The treatment involves two integrated components:

  • Exposure: Systematic confrontation with obsession-triggering stimuli (e.g., touching a "contaminated" object, writing a feared phrase, looking at an asymmetrical arrangement)
  • Response Prevention: Refraining from performing compulsive rituals that typically follow the obsession (e.g., not washing hands after touching the trigger, not checking the lock, not mentally reviewing for reassurance)

Why are both components essential? Consider what happens when a person with contamination OCD touches a doorknob (exposure) but is then allowed to wash her hands immediately afterward. The exposure alone does not break the cycle — the compulsion swoops in to neutralize the distress before habituation can occur, and the reinforcement loop remains intact. It is the combination of sustained contact with the feared stimulus and the prevention of the ritual that allows new learning to take place. ERP works by breaking the reinforcement cycle in which compulsions temporarily reduce obsessional distress, thereby negatively reinforcing both the obsession and the compulsion. Without the ritual, habituation to the distress occurs, and the client learns that feared consequences do not materialize (or that the distress is tolerable). From an inhibitory learning perspective, the client also develops a new, competing memory — "I touched the doorknob, I didn't wash, and nothing terrible happened" — that over time becomes more accessible than the original threat association. ERP produces response rates of 60-80% with significant symptom reduction. Treatment typically involves 12-20 sessions, and gains are well-maintained at follow-up, making it one of the most effective interventions in all of psychotherapy.

Neuroimaging research has identified distinctive patterns of brain activity in individuals with OCD that help explain the disorder's characteristic symptoms and inform understanding of treatment mechanisms. The caudate nucleus, a structure within the basal ganglia that plays a central role in habit formation, procedural learning, and the gating of repetitive behavioral sequences, consistently shows abnormally elevated metabolic activity in people with OCD. The caudate is part of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit — a neural loop connecting the orbitofrontal cortex, striatum (including the caudate), and thalamus. In OCD, dysfunction in this circuit is hypothesized to produce a failure to properly filter and inhibit intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviors, effectively leaving the "error detection" signal perpetually active.

Think of the CSTC circuit as a gatekeeper system. In a healthy brain, the orbitofrontal cortex detects a potential problem ("Did I lock the door?"), the caudate nucleus evaluates whether the concern warrants action, and the thalamus either allows or suppresses the behavioral response. When everything works smoothly, a single check resolves the concern and the circuit quiets. In OCD, the caudate's hyperactivity means the gate never fully closes — the "something is wrong" signal keeps firing even after the behavior has been completed, driving the person to check again and again in a futile effort to achieve a sense of completion that the circuit refuses to deliver.

A landmark finding by Baxter and colleagues demonstrated that successful treatment with cognitive-behavioral therapy — specifically ERP — produced measurable reductions in caudate nucleus metabolic activity, bringing it closer to levels seen in non-clinical populations. Remarkably, these neural changes paralleled those observed in patients who responded to SSRI pharmacotherapy, suggesting that both psychological and pharmacological interventions converge on the same underlying neural circuitry despite operating through different mechanisms of action. This convergence is a powerful rebuttal to any lingering notion that psychotherapy merely changes how people think about their symptoms without altering the biological substrate — ERP literally reshapes brain function.

Caudate Nucleus in OCD

The caudate nucleus is a basal ganglia structure involved in filtering cognitive and behavioral routines. Hyperactivity in the caudate is associated with the intrusive, repetitive quality of obsessions and compulsions. Effective ERP treatment normalizes caudate activity, providing neurobiological evidence that behavioral interventions produce genuine changes in brain function — not merely subjective symptom relief.

ERP Application:

A client with contamination OCD constructs a hierarchy from low-anxiety triggers (touching a doorknob — SUDS 30) to high-anxiety triggers (touching a bathroom floor and then eating — SUDS 95). Sessions involve progressing through the hierarchy while completely refraining from hand washing, use of sanitizer, or other decontamination rituals. Between sessions, the client practices daily exposure exercises with response prevention.

OCD Subtypes and ERP Adaptations

One of ERP's great strengths is its flexibility across the remarkably varied presentations of OCD. While the public imagination often reduces OCD to hand washing and checking, clinicians encounter an enormous range of obsessional content — from fears of inadvertently causing harm, to intrusive blasphemous or sexual thoughts, to an agonizing need for things to feel "just right." Each presentation requires thoughtful adaptation of the exposure hierarchy and the identification of both overt and covert rituals that maintain the cycle. For checking compulsions, exposure involves resisting the urge to check (locks, stove, alarms) after a single verification — the client might lock the front door once, walk away, and sit with the mounting urge to return without acting on it. For symmetry/ordering OCD, exposure involves deliberately creating asymmetry and tolerating the resulting distress — perhaps placing books on a shelf in random order or wearing a watch on the "wrong" wrist. For "pure O" (primarily obsessional OCD), imaginal exposure scripts describing the feared scenario are used when behavioral exposure is not feasible. This subtype presents a particular clinical challenge because the compulsions are entirely mental — a client troubled by intrusive thoughts about harming a family member may engage in rapid-fire mental reviewing, silent counting, or internal prayer to neutralize the thought, and these covert rituals can be easy for both client and therapist to overlook. For harm-related obsessions, exposure may involve holding a knife near a loved one while resisting mental rituals — an exercise that can feel deeply counterintuitive to clients, requiring careful psychoeducation about the difference between having a thought and acting on it. Response prevention targets not only behavioral compulsions but also mental rituals such as mental reviewing, counting, or praying, and the therapist must be attuned to these subtle forms of avoidance.

A clinically important point for exam preparation: the identification of all relevant rituals — behavioral and mental — is essential to effective ERP. A client who dutifully touches contaminated surfaces in session but silently repeats a "decontamination prayer" in her head is engaging in a covert ritual that undermines the response prevention component. Skilled ERP therapists assess for these mental compulsions at intake and throughout treatment, helping clients recognize and resist them with the same commitment they bring to refraining from overt behaviors.

ERP and Medication:

SSRIs (particularly fluvoxamine, fluoxetine, sertraline, and paroxetine) and clomipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant with strong serotonergic activity) are first-line pharmacological treatments for OCD. The combination of ERP plus an SSRI is often recommended for moderate-to-severe OCD. However, research suggests that ERP alone produces equivalent or superior long-term outcomes compared to medication alone, with lower relapse rates after treatment discontinuation.

Evidence-Based Treatments for PTSD

The delivery of trauma-focused psychotherapy through telehealth has received growing empirical support, with research demonstrating that videoconferencing-based treatment produces outcomes comparable to traditional in-person therapy for PTSD. Studies comparing face-to-face delivery with computer videoconferencing formats — including delivery of evidence-based protocols such as PE and CPT — have found equivalent reductions in PTSD symptom severity across both modalities. Clients receiving treatment via videoconference show similar rates of symptom improvement, treatment completion, and therapeutic alliance quality.

Equally significant, research has demonstrated that treatment fidelity — the degree to which therapists adhere to manualized protocols and deliver interventions competently — is maintained when therapy is conducted through videoconferencing. This finding addresses a common concern that remote delivery might compromise the quality or integrity of complex therapeutic procedures such as imaginal exposure or cognitive processing of trauma-related beliefs.

Example

A veteran living in a rural area 200 miles from the nearest VA medical center receives weekly CPT sessions through a secure videoconferencing platform. The therapist conducts Socratic questioning, reviews cognitive worksheets, and processes stuck points with the same structured protocol used in clinic-based sessions. Outcome data show clinically meaningful PTSD symptom reduction equivalent to what would be expected from in-person treatment, and independent fidelity ratings confirm adherence to the CPT manual. This evidence base has been instrumental in expanding access to trauma-focused care for underserved populations, including rural veterans, individuals with mobility limitations, and those in geographically isolated communities.

Prolonged Exposure (PE)

Prolonged Exposure (Foa et al., 2007) is based on Foa's emotional processing theory, which proposes that PTSD involves a pathological fear structure that is activated by trauma-related cues but maintained by avoidance. The fear structure contains stimulus representations, response representations, and meaning representations — successful processing requires activation of the entire fear structure followed by integration of corrective information.

PE consists of four components: (1) psychoeducation about trauma reactions, (2) breathing retraining, (3) imaginal exposure (repeated detailed revisiting of the traumatic memory in session, usually 45-60 minutes, recorded for homework listening), and (4) in vivo exposure to avoided but objectively safe trauma-related situations. PE is typically delivered in 8-15 weekly 90-minute sessions. The imaginal exposure component involves the client narrating the traumatic event in the present tense with eyes closed, repeatedly processing the memory until emotional distress decreases.

Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)

CPT (Resick et al., 2007) targets maladaptive trauma-related cognitions — "stuck points" — that maintain PTSD symptoms. Common stuck points involve themes of safety, trust, power/control, esteem, and intimacy. Stuck points may be assimilated (the trauma is distorted to fit pre-existing beliefs, e.g., "it was my fault") or over-accommodated (pre-existing beliefs are distorted to fit the trauma, e.g., "no one can be trusted"). CPT uses Socratic questioning and cognitive worksheets (ABC worksheets, Challenging Beliefs Worksheets) to help clients identify and modify distorted beliefs. CPT can be delivered with or without a written trauma account (CPT vs. CPT-C, the cognitive-only version). Both 12-session individual and group formats are well-supported.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing)

EMDR (Shapiro, 1989) proposes that traumatic memories are inadequately processed and stored in a dysfunctional manner. The Adaptive Information Processing (AIP) model holds that bilateral stimulation (typically eye movements, but also tapping or auditory tones) facilitates the processing and integration of traumatic memories.

While EMDR is listed as an effective treatment for PTSD by multiple guidelines (APA, VA/DoD, WHO), debate continues about whether the bilateral stimulation component provides benefit beyond standard imaginal exposure. Component analyses have yielded mixed results, and some researchers argue that the exposure and cognitive restructuring elements, rather than the bilateral stimulation, account for EMDR's efficacy.

Trauma-Focused CBT (TF-CBT)

TF-CBT (Cohen, Mannarino, & Deblinger) is the treatment of choice for children and adolescents (ages 3-18) with PTSD. The components are summarized by the acronym PRACTICE:

  • Psychoeducation — about trauma reactions and the treatment process
  • Parenting skills — teaching caregivers effective behavior management
  • Relaxation — progressive muscle relaxation, focused breathing, mindfulness
  • Affective modulation — identifying and regulating emotions
  • Cognitive coping and processing — connecting thoughts, feelings, and behaviors
  • Trauma narrative — gradual exposure through creating a narrative of the trauma experience
  • In vivo mastery — real-world exposure to trauma reminders that are objectively safe
  • Conjoint sessions — sharing the trauma narrative with the caregiver
  • Enhancing safety — safety planning and healthy relationship skills

TF-CBT includes parallel child and caregiver sessions throughout treatment and culminates in the creation and sharing of the trauma narrative. The caregiver component is considered essential — it addresses caregiver distress, teaches supportive responses, and prepares the caregiver for the conjoint narrative sharing session.

Treatment Selection:

PE, CPT, and EMDR are all recommended as first-line treatments for PTSD by the APA Clinical Practice Guideline (2017) and the VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guideline. PE is particularly strong for avoidance-dominated presentations, while CPT may be preferred when cognitive distortions (guilt, self-blame) are prominent. For children and adolescents, TF-CBT is the first-line recommendation. Written exposure therapy (WET) is an emerging brief intervention (5 sessions) showing promise as an efficient trauma-focused treatment.

Specific Phobia Treatment and Pharmacological Considerations

Functional neuroimaging research has illuminated the neural changes associated with successful psychological treatment of specific phobias. Prior to treatment, individuals with specific phobias typically exhibit heightened activation in threat-processing regions when confronted with phobic stimuli. The amygdala, which serves as a key hub for fear detection and emotional learning, shows exaggerated responses to the feared object or situation. The insula, involved in interoceptive awareness and the anticipation of aversive bodily states, also demonstrates elevated activity, as does the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), a region implicated in conflict monitoring, error detection, and the appraisal of threat salience.

Following effective CBT — particularly exposure-based interventions — neuroimaging studies consistently reveal a pattern of reduced activation across these threat-processing areas. Simultaneously, successful treatment is associated with increased activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), a region critically involved in fear extinction, safety signaling, and top-down regulation of amygdala reactivity. The vmPFC is believed to support the retrieval of inhibitory extinction memories that compete with the original fear association, consistent with the inhibitory learning model of exposure therapy.

Clinical Note

These neuroimaging findings provide a biological framework for understanding why exposure therapy works: treatment does not simply change what patients think about feared stimuli but physically alters the balance between subcortical threat circuits and prefrontal regulatory regions. The shift toward greater prefrontal control over amygdala-driven fear responses mirrors the clinical observation that patients learn to regulate their emotional reactions rather than having the fear memory completely erased.

One-Session Treatment for Specific Phobias

One-session treatment (OST) (Ost, 1989) is a single prolonged session (typically 2-3 hours) of participant modeling and in vivo exposure for specific phobias. The therapist models approach behavior, provides graduated exposure, and uses guided participation. OST has demonstrated large effect sizes for various specific phobias including animal phobias, blood-injection-injury phobia, and claustrophobia. This intensive format achieves comparable outcomes to multi-session exposure therapy.

Blood-Injection-Injury (BII) Phobia:

BII phobia is unique among phobias because it involves a diphasic physiological response — an initial sympathetic activation (increased heart rate) followed by a strong parasympathetic response (vasovagal syncope — sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure leading to fainting). For this reason, standard exposure alone may be insufficient; applied tension (Ost & Sterner) — tensing the large muscles of the body during exposure to maintain blood pressure — is the treatment of choice. This is the only phobia where relaxation training is contraindicated, as relaxation would lower blood pressure further.

Exposure Therapy and Benzodiazepines:

Concurrent benzodiazepine use during exposure therapy is generally discouraged because it may interfere with new learning. State-dependent learning theory suggests that fear extinction learned in a benzodiazepine-modulated state may not transfer to the drug-free state. Research suggests that D-cycloserine (DCS), an NMDA receptor partial agonist, may enhance extinction learning when administered shortly before exposure sessions, though results are mixed and the effect appears strongest in early sessions.
