Overview of Emotion and Stress

Emotion and stress are among the most clinically relevant topics in biological psychology. Understanding the biological mechanisms underlying emotional experience and the stress response is essential for EPPP preparation, as these processes are implicated in numerous psychological disorders, health conditions, and therapeutic interventions.

Emotion: A complex psychological state involving three distinct components: a subjective experience (feeling), a physiological response (autonomic arousal), and a behavioral or expressive response (facial expression, action tendency). Theories differ on the causal ordering of these components.

Theories of Emotion

Imagine you're hiking through the woods and suddenly spot a coiled snake on the path ahead. Your heart pounds, your muscles tense, and you leap backward before you've even had time to think. In the aftermath, you notice you're trembling, your palms are sweaty, and you feel intensely afraid. But here's the question that has fueled over a century of debate in psychology: Did you jump because you were afraid — or are you afraid because you jumped? Did your body react first, with your mind catching up, or did some deeper brain process launch both your fear and your pounding heart at the same instant? The answer matters not just philosophically but clinically, because how we understand the origins of emotion shapes how we intervene when emotional responses go awry — in panic disorder, phobias, PTSD, and beyond.

As we established in the preceding section, emotion involves three interacting components — subjective experience, physiological arousal, and behavioral expression. The theories in this section each propose a different causal ordering of those components, and each carries distinct implications for clinical practice. These theories are among the most frequently tested on the EPPP, and understanding their nuances will serve you well both on the exam and in your work with clients.

James-Lange Theory (1884/1885)

Return to that snake on the hiking trail. Most people assume the sequence is straightforward: you see the snake, feel afraid, and then your body reacts. But in the 1880s, William James and Carl Lange independently turned this intuition on its head. They proposed that emotions result from the perception of bodily changes — that the physiological response comes first, and the feeling of fear emerges only after the brain registers what the body is doing. In James's memorable phrasing, "We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble." The body leads, and consciousness follows.

Consider a clinical example: a client with panic disorder reports that episodes seem to "come out of nowhere." From a James-Lange perspective, this makes intuitive sense — the physiological cascade (racing heart, shortness of breath, dizziness) may begin before any conscious appraisal, and the client's terrifying emotional experience emerges from perceiving those bodily sensations. This is one reason interoceptive exposure — deliberately inducing harmless physical sensations like rapid heartbeat — can be so therapeutically powerful: it helps clients break the link between bodily arousal and the emotional label of "panic."

Sequence: Stimulus --> Physiological arousal/bodily response --> Perception of arousal --> Emotional experience. "We feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble" (James, 1884).

  • Implication: Different emotions should produce distinct patterns of physiological arousal
  • Supporting evidence: Some research shows that different emotions do involve partially distinct autonomic patterns (Levenson, Ekman, & Friesen, 1990)
  • Criticism: Cannon argued that visceral changes are too slow and too undifferentiated to account for the rapid and varied nature of emotional experience

Cannon-Bard Theory (1927)

Walter Cannon was unconvinced by James and Lange. He raised a pointed objection: if emotions depended on perceiving distinct bodily states, then we'd need to tell the difference between the racing heart of anger and the racing heart of excitement — but the visceral nervous system simply isn't precise or fast enough to generate the rich palette of emotions we experience. Cannon, along with Philip Bard, proposed an alternative: emotional experience and physiological arousal occur simultaneously and independently, both triggered by the thalamus in response to an emotion-provoking stimulus. In this model, the brain doesn't wait for the body's feedback. Instead, the thalamus acts like a relay hub, sending one signal to the cortex (producing the conscious feeling) and another to the autonomic nervous system (producing the bodily response) at the same time.

Evidence for this view came from dramatic experiments. Animals whose visceral connections to the brain were surgically severed still displayed emotional behaviors — they snarled, cowered, and showed rage. And consider a more everyday observation: people with high spinal cord injuries who have lost sensation from much of their body still report rich emotional lives. If James and Lange were entirely correct, these individuals should experience significantly diminished emotion, yet most do not. These findings support the idea that the body's feedback, while important, is not the sole engine of emotional experience.

Sequence: Stimulus --> Thalamus sends signals simultaneously to the cortex (emotional experience) and the body (physiological arousal). The two processes are parallel and independent.

  • Key claim: Physiological arousal does not cause emotion; both are products of the same neural event
  • Supporting evidence: Animals with severed connections between the brain and viscera still show emotional behavior; people with spinal cord injuries still experience emotions

Schachter-Singer Two-Factor Theory (1962)

Decades later, Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer proposed an elegant synthesis that borrowed from both previous theories while adding a crucial ingredient: cognition. They agreed with James and Lange that physiological arousal is essential — without it, emotion lacks intensity and urgency. But they also recognized Cannon's point that arousal alone is too vague to specify which emotion you're feeling. Their solution? Emotion requires both physiological arousal AND cognitive interpretation (labeling) of that arousal. The body provides the fuel; the mind provides the label. The same pounding heart can become joy at a surprise party, anxiety before a job interview, or romantic attraction on a first date — depending on how you interpret the context.

This theory gave rise to one of the most fascinating and clinically relevant phenomena in emotion research: the misattribution of arousal. In the famous Dutton and Aron (1974) bridge study, men who had just crossed a terrifying, swaying suspension bridge rated a female interviewer as more attractive than did men who crossed a sturdy, low bridge. The residual physiological arousal from the fear-inducing bridge was cognitively relabeled as romantic attraction. This principle shows up regularly in clinical settings — clients may mistake caffeine-induced arousal for anxiety, or physical symptoms of a medical condition for emotional distress. A clinician aware of two-factor theory knows to explore whether a client's "anxiety" might partly reflect a misattributed physiological state.

Sequence: Stimulus --> Physiological arousal --> Cognitive interpretation of the arousal based on context --> Emotional experience.

  • Classic experiment: Participants injected with epinephrine (causing arousal) who were not informed of the drug's effects labeled their arousal based on social context -- reporting happiness when with a euphoric confederate and anger when with an angry confederate
  • Misattribution of arousal: People may misattribute physiological arousal from one source to another (e.g., Dutton & Aron's [1974] bridge study, where men crossing a fear-inducing suspension bridge were more attracted to a female interviewer)

Other Important Emotion Theories

Beyond the classical theories, several influential models have advanced our understanding of how cognition and neural circuitry interact to produce emotional experience. These theories differ notably on whether cognitive appraisal is necessary for emotion or whether affect can arise independently of conscious thought. This debate — sometimes called the Lazarus-Zajonc debate — has profound implications for clinical practice. If appraisal always comes first, then cognitive restructuring should be effective for all emotional disorders. But if emotion can bypass cognition entirely, we may need interventions that target subcortical circuits directly, such as exposure-based therapies that don't rely on conscious reappraisal.

Lazarus's Cognitive Appraisal Theory (1991)

Richard Lazarus argued that cognitive appraisal is a necessary precondition for emotion. In his model, before you can feel anything, you must first evaluate — even if rapidly and outside full awareness — whether the stimulus is personally relevant (primary appraisal) and whether you have the resources to cope with it (secondary appraisal). A growling dog triggers fear only if you appraise it as dangerous and yourself as unable to escape. The same dog might trigger annoyance in a confident dog trainer and terror in someone with a history of dog bites. This framework maps remarkably well onto cognitive-behavioral therapy, where the therapist helps the client examine and restructure the appraisals that generate distressing emotions. Lazarus's theory essentially provides the theoretical backbone for the CBT principle that it is not events themselves but our interpretation of events that determines our emotional responses.

Zajonc's Affective Primacy Hypothesis (1984)

Robert Zajonc countered Lazarus by arguing that emotional reactions can occur before and independent of cognition. His evidence was compelling in its simplicity: through research on the mere exposure effect, Zajonc demonstrated that people develop preferences for stimuli they have seen before — even when those stimuli are presented so briefly (subliminally) that participants cannot consciously recognize them. If you can like something without knowing you've seen it, then emotion clearly doesn't require the kind of deliberate appraisal Lazarus described. Think of the instant, visceral reaction you might have to a face in a crowd — attraction, unease, warmth — before you've had time to analyze why. Zajonc's work reminds clinicians that not all emotional responses are accessible to conscious cognitive examination, which helps explain why some clients can articulate no "reason" for their anxiety or aversion yet experience it intensely nonetheless.

LeDoux's Dual-Pathway Model (1996)

Joseph LeDoux's neuroscience research provided an elegant resolution to the Lazarus-Zajonc debate by showing that both perspectives have biological validity. LeDoux demonstrated that fear processing involves two neural pathways from the thalamus to the amygdala — and these pathways operate at different speeds and levels of sophistication. Picture yourself in that forest again: you see a curved shape on the ground. Before you can think "it's just a stick," you've already jumped. That's the low road. A moment later, your cortex processes the image in more detail, recognizes it as a harmless branch, and your fear subsides. That's the high road. Both pathways are real, both serve survival functions, and together they explain why emotion can sometimes outrun cognition and why, at other times, careful appraisal shapes what we feel.

  • "Low road" (thalamo-amygdala pathway): Fast, direct, and crude processing. Enables rapid fear responses before conscious awareness.
  • "High road" (thalamo-cortical-amygdala pathway): Slower but more detailed cortical processing. Allows for more nuanced evaluation of threats.

LeDoux's dual-pathway model has important clinical implications. In PTSD, for example, the low road may become hyperactivated — a car backfiring triggers an immediate, overwhelming fear response via the thalamo-amygdala pathway before the cortex can intervene with the reassurance that "it's just a car." Exposure therapies work, in part, by strengthening the high road's ability to regulate the low road's alarm signals. Understanding these two pathways helps clinicians explain to clients why their fear responses can feel so automatic and uncontrollable — and why therapeutic techniques that may seem slow or repetitive are actually retraining the brain's threat-detection circuitry.

Lazarus's model specifies three distinct forms of cognitive evaluation that shape emotional responding. Primary appraisal involves an initial assessment of whether a situation is irrelevant, benign-positive, or stressful (including potential harm, threat, or challenge). Secondary appraisal follows and concerns the individual's evaluation of available coping resources — both personal (e.g., skills, self-efficacy, past experience) and environmental (e.g., social support, financial means, access to information) — and whether these resources are sufficient to manage the demands posed by the stressor. Reappraisal (sometimes called cognitive reappraisal) is the ongoing process of reassessing the situation as new information becomes available or as coping efforts succeed or fail. The interplay among these three appraisal types determines not only which emotion is experienced but also its intensity and duration, making them central targets in cognitive-behavioral and stress-management interventions.

Appraisal Type Core Question Focus
Primary "Is this situation a threat, challenge, or irrelevant to me?" Significance of the event
Secondary "What can I do about it? Do I have the resources to cope?" Personal and environmental coping capacity
Reappraisal "Has the situation or my ability to handle it changed?" Updated evaluation based on new information

Lazarus's model specifies two sequential stages of cognitive evaluation. During primary appraisal, the individual assesses an external event along several dimensions: first, whether the event has any personal relevance at all (irrelevant vs. relevant), and if it is relevant, whether it represents something beneficial or benign versus something threatening or harmful. Events appraised as stressful are further categorized as involving harm/loss (damage already done), threat (anticipated future damage), or challenge (potential for growth or mastery). Secondary appraisal then follows, in which the individual evaluates their available coping resources and options for managing the situation. The specific emotion that emerges — anxiety, anger, guilt, hope — is determined by the interaction of both appraisal stages. This framework explains why two individuals confronting identical circumstances may experience entirely different emotional responses based on how they evaluate both the event and their capacity to handle it.

The Autonomic Nervous System and Emotion

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the division of the peripheral nervous system that controls involuntary bodily functions and mediates the physiological component of emotional responses.

Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS)

The "fight-or-flight" system, activated during stress, danger, or emotional arousal:

  • Increases heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration
  • Dilates pupils and bronchioles
  • Redirects blood from digestive organs to skeletal muscles
  • Stimulates the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine
  • Inhibits digestion and salivation
  • Uses norepinephrine as the primary postganglionic neurotransmitter

Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS)

The "rest-and-digest" system, promoting calm, recovery, and energy conservation:

  • Decreases heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration
  • Constricts pupils and bronchioles
  • Stimulates digestion, salivation, and sexual arousal
  • Promotes energy storage and tissue repair
  • Uses acetylcholine as the primary postganglionic neurotransmitter
  • The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) is the primary parasympathetic pathway

Clinical Significance: Polyvagal theory (Porges, 2011) proposes that the vagus nerve has two branches: the ventral vagal complex (promotes social engagement and calm states) and the dorsal vagal complex (triggers immobilization/shutdown during overwhelming threat). This framework is increasingly used in trauma therapy.

The Stress Response: HPA Axis

In the previous section, we explored how the autonomic nervous system orchestrates rapid, moment-to-moment responses to emotional stimuli — the pounding heart, the sweaty palms, the freeze response mediated by dorsal vagal shutdown. But what happens when stress doesn't resolve in seconds or minutes? What keeps the body mobilized when threat persists — or when the brain merely perceives that it does? The answer lies not in the fast-acting sympathetic nervous system, but in a slower, more sustained neuroendocrine cascade that profoundly shapes both physical health and psychological functioning.

Consider a graduate student preparing for comprehensive exams. Weeks before the test date, she notices she's catching every cold that circulates through her cohort, her sleep is fragmented, and she can't seem to recall material she studied just days ago. Her sympathetic nervous system isn't in overdrive — she's not fleeing a predator. Instead, a different stress system has been grinding away quietly, flooding her body with hormones that, over time, suppress her immune defenses and erode the very hippocampal circuits she needs for memory retrieval. This is the work of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis — the primary neuroendocrine system that mediates the body's sustained response to stress, and one of the most clinically consequential pathways you will encounter on the EPPP.

Where the sympathetic nervous system acts in seconds through direct neural connections, the HPA axis operates over minutes to hours through a hormonal relay — a three-step cascade that begins in the brain and ends at the adrenal glands sitting atop the kidneys. Understanding this sequence is essential, because dysregulation of the HPA axis has been implicated in depression, PTSD, anxiety disorders, metabolic syndrome, and even neurodegenerative processes. Let's trace the pathway step by step.

HPA Axis Activation Sequence

The HPA axis follows an elegant signaling chain in which each structure activates the next, like a series of falling dominoes. When the brain detects a stressor — whether it's a genuine physical threat or the psychological weight of an impending deadline — the hypothalamus initiates a hormonal cascade that ultimately delivers cortisol throughout the body. Critically, the system also contains its own off switch: cortisol itself feeds back to the brain structures that started the cascade, signaling them to stand down. When this negative feedback loop functions properly, the stress response is self-limiting. When it doesn't, chronic stress-related pathology can follow.

  1. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) in response to perceived stress
  2. CRH stimulates the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)
  3. ACTH travels through the bloodstream to the adrenal cortex
  4. The adrenal cortex releases cortisol (the primary stress hormone, a glucocorticoid)
  5. Cortisol acts on multiple organ systems and provides negative feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary to terminate the stress response

A useful mnemonic for remembering this sequence is to think of the acronym "CCA" — CRH triggers the Cascade, ACTH Carries the signal through the blood, and the Adrenal cortex delivers cortisol. Notice that this pathway involves both neural tissue (the hypothalamus) and endocrine glands (the pituitary and adrenals), which is why it is described as a neuroendocrine axis rather than a purely hormonal or purely neural system.

Effects of Cortisol

Cortisol is sometimes called "the stress hormone," but this label, while catchy, can be misleading. Cortisol is not inherently harmful — in fact, it is essential for survival. At moderate, time-limited levels, cortisol mobilizes energy, sharpens certain cognitive functions, and helps the body adapt to challenge. The problems emerge when cortisol levels remain chronically elevated, as happens in prolonged psychological stress, untreated depression, or early-life adversity. The distinction between adaptive short-term cortisol release and destructive chronic elevation is one of the most important concepts in health psychology and behavioral medicine.

  • Metabolic: Increases blood glucose (gluconeogenesis), mobilizes energy stores
  • Immune: Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects at sustained high levels
  • Cognitive: Moderate levels enhance memory consolidation; chronic high levels impair hippocampal function and memory
  • Neural: Chronic elevation causes hippocampal atrophy and dendritic remodeling in the prefrontal cortex

The cognitive and neural effects deserve special clinical attention. Think back to our graduate student: her difficulty recalling previously learned material is not simply a product of anxiety or poor study habits. Chronically elevated cortisol literally remodels the hippocampus — the brain structure most critical for declarative memory consolidation — by causing dendritic retraction and reduced neurogenesis. Research by Robert Sapolsky and others has demonstrated that sustained glucocorticoid exposure can reduce hippocampal volume, a finding that has been replicated in neuroimaging studies of patients with chronic PTSD and recurrent major depression. Meanwhile, cortisol-driven remodeling in the prefrontal cortex impairs executive functioning and emotion regulation, creating a vicious cycle in which the brain becomes progressively less capable of down-regulating its own stress response.

Cortisol: The primary glucocorticoid hormone released by the adrenal cortex in response to ACTH. It follows a diurnal rhythm (highest in the morning, lowest at night) and is a key biomarker of HPA axis function. Chronically elevated cortisol is associated with depression, PTSD, metabolic syndrome, and hippocampal damage.

The diurnal rhythm mentioned in the definition above is clinically significant in its own right. In healthy individuals, cortisol peaks within 30 to 45 minutes of waking — a phenomenon called the cortisol awakening response (CAR) — and then declines steadily throughout the day. Disruptions to this rhythm, such as a flattened diurnal curve or an exaggerated CAR, have been linked to burnout, chronic fatigue, and various psychiatric conditions. Clinicians working in integrated care settings may encounter cortisol assays (typically salivary cortisol) as part of biobehavioral assessments, making familiarity with HPA axis dynamics directly relevant to practice.

Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

Hans Selye (1936, 1956) described the General Adaptation Syndrome, a three-stage model of the body's response to prolonged stress:

Stage 1: Alarm Reaction

The body's initial fight-or-flight response to a stressor. The sympathetic nervous system is activated, releasing catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine). Cortisol levels begin to rise. Resistance to stress temporarily drops below normal.

Stage 2: Resistance (Adaptation)

The body attempts to adapt to the continued presence of the stressor. Cortisol levels remain elevated but the body achieves a new homeostatic balance. Parasympathetic activity partially counterbalances sympathetic arousal. The individual appears to be coping but physiological resources are being depleted.

Stage 3: Exhaustion

Prolonged stress depletes the body's adaptive resources. The immune system becomes compromised, vulnerability to illness increases dramatically, and organ systems may begin to fail. This stage can lead to "diseases of adaptation" including cardiovascular disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and immune dysfunction.

EPPP Application: Selye distinguished between eustress (positive, motivating stress) and distress (negative, harmful stress). He also noted that the GAS response is nonspecific -- the body responds similarly regardless of the type of stressor.

Allostatic Load

Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome showed us what happens when the body mobilizes its defenses against a stressor — the alarm, resistance, and eventual exhaustion that follow a predictable arc. But Selye's model was built around the idea that stress responses are episodic: a threat appears, the body responds, and eventually the system either recovers or collapses. What happens, though, when the threats never really stop? Consider a single mother working two jobs in an under-resourced neighborhood, sleeping five hours a night, worrying constantly about her children's safety, and facing discrimination at work. She isn't encountering a single acute stressor — she is living inside a chronic stress field. Her cortisol doesn't spike and recover; it stays elevated, day after day, month after month. Over time, her blood pressure creeps upward, her inflammatory markers rise, and her metabolic health deteriorates — not because of any single event, but because the cumulative burden on her body has become unsustainable.

This is the phenomenon that Bruce McEwen (1998) captured with the concept of allostatic load — the cumulative physiological wear and tear on the body resulting from chronic exposure to stress hormones and repeated activation of the stress response. Unlike the acute stress that Selye described as adaptive (and even beneficial in the alarm stage), chronic stress overloads the system. The body was never designed to run its emergency machinery indefinitely, and the biological cost of doing so accumulates silently until it manifests as disease.

To understand allostatic load, it helps to first grasp the concept on which it builds: allostasis. Traditional physiology emphasizes homeostasis — the body's effort to maintain fixed internal set points for temperature, blood glucose, pH, and other parameters. Allostasis takes a more dynamic view: it refers to the process by which the body achieves stability through physiological change. Rather than rigidly defending a single set point, the allostatic system flexibly adjusts its parameters in anticipation of environmental demands. When you wake up in the morning, for instance, your cortisol surges before you even get out of bed — your brain is proactively preparing your body for the metabolic demands of the day. That anticipatory flexibility is allostasis at work, and in the short term, it is beautifully adaptive.

The problem arises when this adaptive system is chronically overtaxed. Allostatic overload occurs when cumulative stress exceeds the body's ability to adapt, tipping the system from flexible coping into pathology. Think of it like a bridge engineered to handle normal traffic loads: occasional heavy trucks cause no lasting damage, but years of constant overloading eventually produces cracks in the structural supports. In the human body, allostatic overload leads to cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, immune dysregulation, cognitive decline, and accelerated aging.

  • Allostasis: The process by which the body achieves stability through physiological change (as opposed to homeostasis, which maintains a fixed set point)
  • Allostatic overload: When cumulative stress exceeds the body's ability to adapt, leading to pathology
  • Contributors include chronic psychological stress, sleep deprivation, poor diet, lack of exercise, and social isolation
  • Measurable biomarkers include cortisol levels, blood pressure, waist-to-hip ratio, glycated hemoglobin, and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, IL-6)

One of the most clinically powerful aspects of the allostatic load model is that it is measurable. Researchers and clinicians can assess allostatic load using a composite index of biomarkers — elevated cortisol, high blood pressure, increased waist-to-hip ratio, elevated glycated hemoglobin (a marker of chronic blood sugar dysregulation), and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6. No single biomarker tells the whole story, but taken together, they paint a picture of how much cumulative biological toll a person's life circumstances have exacted on their body. A patient may appear "healthy" by any single measure yet carry a dangerously high allostatic load visible only when these indicators are considered as a pattern.

Clinical Significance: Allostatic load provides a framework for understanding health disparities. Chronic exposure to discrimination, poverty, and adversity produces elevated allostatic load, contributing to poorer health outcomes among marginalized populations. This concept bridges biological psychology and health psychology on the EPPP.

For the EPPP, the allostatic load framework is important because it moves stress research beyond Selye's nonspecific response model and into a more nuanced, cumulative account of how chronic life circumstances get "under the skin." It also sets the stage for the next topic — psychoneuroimmunology — which examines the specific pathways through which psychological stress alters immune function, one of the most consequential downstream effects of sustained allostatic overload.

Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)

Consider a scenario that played out in laboratories across the country during the 1980s and 1990s: medical students sitting for final exams showed measurable drops in their immune function — fewer natural killer cells circulating in their blood, weaker responses to viral challenges, even slower healing of small standardized wounds. These weren't critically ill patients or individuals facing extraordinary trauma. They were healthy young adults experiencing a predictable, time-limited academic stressor. Yet their immune systems responded as though something biologically significant had shifted. Findings like these demanded an explanation that neither psychology nor immunology alone could provide, and they helped crystallize a new interdisciplinary field that has become essential to understanding how the mind shapes physical health.

As we discussed in the preceding section, allostatic overload describes the cumulative physiological toll of chronic stress — the wear-and-tear on organ systems that results when stress-response mechanisms remain activated far beyond their adaptive window. One of the most consequential downstream targets of this overload is the immune system. Psychoneuroimmunology is the interdisciplinary field studying the interactions among psychological processes, the nervous system, and the immune system. Rather than treating these three systems as independent domains, PNI investigates the bidirectional pathways through which thoughts, emotions, and social experiences alter immune function — and, just as importantly, how immune activity feeds back to shape mood, cognition, and behavior. For clinicians, PNI provides a biological foundation for understanding why psychological interventions can improve physical health outcomes and why chronic medical conditions so often co-occur with depression and anxiety.

Key Findings

The PNI literature has grown enormously since its origins in the 1970s, but a handful of landmark findings form the conceptual backbone that EPPP candidates should know well. Each finding illustrates a different facet of the mind-immune connection and carries distinct clinical implications.

  • Stress and immunity: Acute stress can temporarily enhance immune function (mobilizing natural killer cells), while chronic stress suppresses immune function through sustained cortisol elevation
  • Kiecolt-Glaser studies: Medical student exams, caregiver stress, and marital conflict all demonstrated measurable immune suppression (reduced natural killer cell activity, slower wound healing, reduced antibody response to vaccines)
  • Conditioning of immune responses: Ader and Cohen (1975) demonstrated that immune responses can be classically conditioned, providing direct evidence that the nervous system can regulate immune function
  • Cytokines and behavior: Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., interleukin-1, interleukin-6, TNF-alpha) produced by the immune system can induce "sickness behavior" (fatigue, social withdrawal, anhedonia) that overlaps with depressive symptoms

The distinction between acute and chronic stress effects on immunity is worth pausing on because it mirrors a theme we encountered with the general adaptation syndrome and allostatic load: short-term activation of the stress response is adaptive, while prolonged activation becomes destructive. When you face a brief physical threat — say, a near-miss car accident — your sympathetic nervous system surges and redistributes immune cells to the skin, lymph nodes, and other sites where injury is most likely. This is an elegant evolutionary preparation for wounding. But when the stressor is chronic — caregiving for a spouse with dementia, enduring persistent financial hardship, or living in a violent neighborhood — cortisol remains elevated and begins to suppress the very immune processes that acute stress enhances. Natural killer cell activity declines, inflammatory regulation deteriorates, and vulnerability to infection increases.

The research of Janice Kiecolt-Glaser and Ronald Glaser at Ohio State University provided some of the most compelling human evidence for these effects. In one well-known paradigm, they tracked medical students across examination periods and found significant reductions in natural killer cell activity and diminished lymphocyte proliferation during high-stress exam weeks compared to low-stress baseline periods. In caregiver studies, they documented that individuals providing long-term care for relatives with Alzheimer's disease showed suppressed immune function that persisted even years after caregiving ended — a striking demonstration of allostatic load at the immunological level. Perhaps most vividly, their marital conflict studies used standardized wound-healing paradigms: couples who engaged in hostile interactions during a laboratory visit showed significantly slower healing of small biopsy wounds than couples who interacted warmly, illustrating how everyday relational stress translates directly into impaired immune function.

The Ader and Cohen conditioning study deserves special attention because it fundamentally challenged the prevailing assumption that the immune system operated autonomously, beyond the reach of neural influence. In their classic 1975 experiment, rats were given saccharin-flavored water (the conditioned stimulus) paired with cyclophosphamide, an immunosuppressive drug (the unconditioned stimulus). After conditioning, presentation of the saccharin water alone was sufficient to produce immunosuppression — the animals' nervous systems had "learned" to suppress immune function in response to a taste cue. This elegant demonstration provided direct evidence that the brain and immune system communicate bidirectionally, and it essentially gave birth to psychoneuroimmunology as a recognized discipline.

The discovery that cytokines — signaling molecules produced by immune cells — can drive behavioral changes has profound implications for clinical psychology. When the body fights infection or injury, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) are released. These molecules don't just coordinate the immune response locally; they signal the brain and produce a characteristic constellation of symptoms known as sickness behavior: fatigue, reduced appetite, social withdrawal, increased sleep, and anhedonia. Clinicians should recognize that this symptom cluster overlaps substantially with major depressive disorder. Indeed, a growing body of evidence suggests that chronic low-grade inflammation may contribute to depression in a subset of patients, which helps explain why some individuals with elevated inflammatory markers respond preferentially to anti-inflammatory interventions or why patients receiving cytokine-based treatments for hepatitis C or certain cancers frequently develop depressive episodes.

Pathways of Communication

Understanding how psychological experiences reach the immune system — and how immune events reach the brain — requires knowledge of three major communication pathways. These pathways operate simultaneously and interact with one another, creating the integrated network that makes PNI phenomena possible.

  • Neural pathway: The autonomic nervous system innervates lymphoid organs (spleen, thymus, lymph nodes) and can directly modulate immune cell activity
  • Endocrine pathway: Cortisol and other stress hormones regulate immune cell function through glucocorticoid receptors on immune cells
  • Immune-to-brain signaling: Cytokines produced by immune cells signal the brain via the vagus nerve and circumventricular organs, influencing mood and behavior

The neural pathway is perhaps the most intuitive: sympathetic nerve fibers physically innervate the primary and secondary lymphoid organs — the thymus, spleen, bone marrow, and lymph nodes — where immune cells develop, mature, and are activated. Norepinephrine released from these nerve terminals binds to adrenergic receptors on lymphocytes, macrophages, and other immune cells, directly modulating their activity. This hard-wired connection explains why acute psychological stress can produce near-instantaneous changes in immune cell distribution, long before hormonal signals have time to circulate.

The endocrine pathway operates on a somewhat slower timescale through the HPA axis. When cortisol is released from the adrenal cortex during stress, it binds to glucocorticoid receptors found on virtually all immune cells. At normal physiological levels, cortisol helps regulate the immune response — preventing it from overshooting and causing autoimmune damage. But under conditions of chronic stress, sustained cortisol elevation suppresses the proliferation of lymphocytes, reduces the activity of natural killer cells, and shifts the balance of immune signaling away from cellular immunity (which fights viruses and tumors) and toward a less protective profile. This is the endocrine mechanism underlying many of the Kiecolt-Glaser findings described above.

Perhaps most fascinating for psychologists is the immune-to-brain pathway, which completes the bidirectional loop. Cytokines produced peripherally during infection or inflammation can signal the central nervous system through at least two routes: the vagus nerve, which carries afferent signals from the body cavity to the brainstem, and the circumventricular organs — specialized brain regions where the blood-brain barrier is more permeable, allowing cytokines to access neural tissue directly. Once these immune signals reach the brain, they activate microglia (the brain's resident immune cells), alter neurotransmitter metabolism — particularly serotonin and dopamine — and produce the motivational and emotional changes we recognize as sickness behavior. For clinical practice, this pathway underscores a critical insight: depression is not always "all in the head" in the colloquial sense. Sometimes the immune system is driving changes in brain chemistry that manifest as psychological symptoms, a finding that is reshaping how researchers and clinicians conceptualize the biology of mood disorders.

Biological Bases of Specific Emotions

Lateralized Emotional Reactions to Brain Damage

Clinical observations and research on unilateral brain lesions have consistently demonstrated that the side of cerebral damage predicts qualitatively different emotional presentations. Patients with damage to the left (dominant) hemisphere — particularly in frontal and temporal regions — are more likely to display a catastrophic reaction, marked by intense depression, anxiety, tearfulness, and emotional volatility. These patients often become acutely distressed about their functional limitations and may exhibit exaggerated frustration responses even to minor failures.

Conversely, patients with damage to the right (non-dominant) hemisphere frequently display an indifference reaction, characterized by a lack of appropriate emotional concern, flat or blunted affect, and sometimes inappropriate jocularity or minimization of deficits. Some right-hemisphere patients exhibit frank anosognosia — a complete unawareness or denial of their neurological impairments, such as neglecting the left side of their body or denying paralysis of the left limbs.

These lateralized emotional patterns have important implications for both neuropsychological assessment and rehabilitation planning, as the patient's emotional response to injury directly affects motivation, treatment engagement, and functional recovery.

The Papez Circuit

In 1937, James Papez proposed that emotional experience is mediated by a interconnected loop of limbic and cortical structures now known as the Papez circuit. This circuit plays a central role in both the generation of emotional states and the formation of emotionally charged memories.

Papez Circuit

A neural circuit consisting of the hippocampus, fornix, mammillary bodies, anterior thalamic nuclei, cingulate gyrus, and parahippocampal gyrus. Information flows through this loop: the hippocampus projects via the fornix to the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus, which relay to the anterior thalamus, which projects to the cingulate cortex, which feeds back to the parahippocampal region and hippocampus, completing the circuit.

Papez originally theorized that the cingulate cortex was the seat of subjective emotional experience, while the hypothalamus governed the bodily expression of emotion. The hippocampus served as a critical relay connecting cortical processing with hypothalamic output. Although subsequent research — particularly the work of Paul MacLean, who expanded the concept into the broader limbic system — has refined and extended these ideas, the Papez circuit remains foundational to our understanding of how emotion and memory are neuroanatomically integrated. The overlap between structures involved in emotional processing and those critical for declarative memory (especially the hippocampus and mammillary bodies) explains why emotionally significant events are often encoded more robustly and why damage to components of this circuit can simultaneously impair both emotional regulation and memory consolidation.

Example

Korsakoff syndrome, caused by thiamine deficiency associated with chronic alcoholism, produces severe amnesia partly through damage to the mammillary bodies — a key node in the Papez circuit. Patients with this condition show not only profound memory impairment but also emotional blunting and apathy, illustrating the dual role of the circuit in both mnemonic and affective processing.

Research on hemispheric lateralization of emotion has revealed that the left and right cerebral hemispheres make distinct contributions to emotional processing, and that unilateral brain damage produces characteristically different emotional disturbances depending on which hemisphere is affected.

Damage to the left hemisphere — the language-dominant hemisphere in most individuals — frequently produces what is known as a catastrophic reaction. Patients may present with severe depressive symptoms, emotional lability, tearfulness, and intense frustration. This pattern is commonly observed following left-hemisphere strokes, particularly those affecting frontal and anterior regions. The depressive response is thought to reflect both the loss of left-hemispheric positive-affect processing and the patient's preserved awareness (via the intact right hemisphere) of their deficits.

In contrast, damage to the right hemisphere often produces an indifference reaction characterized by emotional flatness, inappropriate unconcern about one's condition, or even euphoria. Patients with right-hemisphere lesions may minimize or deny their impairments (anosognosia) and show a surprising lack of distress despite significant functional losses. This dissociation is consistent with the valence hypothesis, which posits that the left hemisphere is more involved in processing positive emotions and approach behaviors, while the right hemisphere is more specialized for negative emotions and withdrawal behaviors.

Clinical Note

When conducting neuropsychological evaluations of stroke patients, clinicians should be attentive to these lateralized emotional patterns. A patient who appears remarkably unconcerned following a right-hemisphere stroke may not be coping well — they may be exhibiting a neurologically mediated indifference reaction that masks the severity of their condition. Conversely, intense emotional distress after a left-hemisphere stroke may have a neurobiological basis beyond a purely psychological reaction to disability.

Fear

The amygdala is the central structure for fear processing. Fear conditioning involves pairing a neutral stimulus (CS) with an aversive stimulus (US), resulting in the CS eliciting a fear response. The amygdala mediates both the acquisition and expression of conditioned fear, while the prefrontal cortex (particularly the ventromedial PFC) is involved in fear extinction.

Anger and Aggression

The neural circuitry of aggression involves the amygdala, hypothalamus (particularly the ventromedial hypothalamus), and the orbitofrontal cortex (which provides top-down inhibitory control). Low serotonin levels are consistently associated with increased impulsive aggression.

Reward and Pleasure

The mesolimbic dopamine pathway (VTA to nucleus accumbens) is the brain's primary reward circuit. Dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens is associated with reward anticipation and motivation rather than pleasure itself. The opioid system is more directly linked to hedonic "liking."
