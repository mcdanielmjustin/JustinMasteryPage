Overview of Neurological Disorders

Neurological disorders involve structural or functional pathology of the nervous system. For the EPPP, clinicians must understand the neuroanatomical bases, key symptoms, and differential diagnosis of major neurological conditions, as they frequently co-occur with or mimic psychiatric disorders. This chapter covers epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and aphasia syndromes.

Neurological Disorder: A disease or condition caused by dysfunction of the brain, spinal cord, or peripheral nerves. Unlike primary psychiatric disorders, neurological disorders have identifiable neuropathology. However, nearly all neurological disorders produce psychiatric and cognitive symptoms that psychologists must recognize and address.

Not all conditions that produce neurological symptoms originate within the nervous system itself. Metabolic and endocrine disturbances can significantly affect brain function and may mimic or exacerbate neurological and psychiatric disorders. One clinically relevant example involves hypoglycemia — abnormally low blood glucose levels — which can result from excessive insulin secretion by the pancreas. Glucose is the brain's primary energy substrate, and even brief periods of glucose deprivation can produce pronounced neurological and behavioral symptoms.

When the pancreas produces too much insulin — whether due to an insulinoma (insulin-secreting tumor), medication effects in diabetes management, or reactive hypoglycemia — blood glucose drops below the threshold needed for normal neural functioning. Acute symptoms include confusion, difficulty concentrating, irritability, tremor, diaphoresis (sweating), anxiety, and visual disturbances. Severe or prolonged hypoglycemia can cause seizures, loss of consciousness, and permanent brain damage, particularly to the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, which are highly sensitive to glucose deprivation.

Clinical Note

Hypoglycemic episodes can closely resemble panic attacks, acute anxiety states, or even seizure disorders. Psychologists should consider metabolic causes — including blood glucose irregularities — when patients present with episodic confusion, cognitive changes, or anxiety-like symptoms, particularly when episodes are temporally related to fasting or meals. Appropriate medical referral for glucose monitoring and endocrine evaluation is essential before attributing such symptoms to a primary psychiatric condition.

Hypoglycemia

A condition characterized by abnormally low blood glucose, often below 70 mg/dL, that can result from overproduction of insulin by the pancreas or from exogenous insulin administration. The brain's dependence on glucose makes it particularly vulnerable to hypoglycemic states, producing cognitive, behavioral, and neurological symptoms that resolve when glucose levels are restored.

Advances in neuroimaging have greatly enhanced the ability to detect and characterize neurological pathology in vivo. Among the most significant tools for evaluating white matter integrity is diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), a specialized form of magnetic resonance imaging that measures the directional movement of water molecules along axonal tracts. Because water diffuses preferentially along the length of intact myelinated fibers (a property called anisotropy), DTI can reveal disruptions in white matter microstructure that conventional MRI may miss.

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI)

A neuroimaging technique that maps the diffusion of water molecules in brain tissue to assess the structural integrity of white matter pathways. Fractional anisotropy (FA) is a commonly derived metric: higher FA values indicate well-organized, intact fiber tracts, while lower FA values suggest degradation or disorganization of white matter.

DTI has proven valuable across a range of neurological conditions. In traumatic brain injury, DTI can detect diffuse axonal injury that is invisible on standard CT or MRI scans, providing a more sensitive marker of injury severity. In multiple sclerosis, DTI reveals white matter damage beyond the focal lesions visible on conventional imaging, including abnormalities in "normal-appearing" white matter. The technique is also used in research on neurodegenerative diseases such as Huntington's and Alzheimer's disease, where early white matter tract degeneration may precede gray matter atrophy. For clinicians, DTI findings can help explain cognitive and behavioral deficits — particularly involving processing speed, attention, and executive function — that arise from disrupted connectivity between brain regions rather than from damage to the regions themselves.

Clinical Note

When neuropsychological testing reveals deficits in processing speed or executive functioning but structural MRI appears relatively normal, DTI findings of compromised white matter integrity may help account for the clinical presentation. This is especially relevant in mild TBI cases where standard imaging is unremarkable yet patients report persistent cognitive difficulties.

Migraine Headaches

Although not always classified alongside diseases such as epilepsy or multiple sclerosis, migraine headaches represent a significant neurological condition with substantial clinical overlap with psychiatric disorders. Migraines affect approximately 12% of the general population, with higher prevalence in women (approximately 3:1 female-to-male ratio), and are frequently comorbid with depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances.

Migraines are categorized into two primary subtypes. Migraine with aura (sometimes called "classic migraine") begins with a prodromal phase of neurological disturbance — the aura — that typically lasts 20-60 minutes and precedes the headache. Auras most commonly involve visual phenomena such as scintillating scotomata (shimmering zigzag lines), flashing lights, or transient visual field loss, though sensory (tingling, numbness) and language disturbances can also occur. Migraine without aura ("common migraine") lacks this prodromal phase and is the more prevalent subtype.

The headache phase of a migraine is characterized by throbbing or pulsatile pain, often unilateral (though it may become bilateral). The pain typically worsens with routine physical activity. Accompanying symptoms include nausea, vomiting, photophobia (sensitivity to light), and phonophobia (sensitivity to sound), which often lead patients to seek dark, quiet environments. Episodes can last from 4 to 72 hours if untreated.

Clinical Note

For psychologists, migraines are relevant in several contexts: they are commonly triggered or exacerbated by psychological stress, sleep disruption, and hormonal changes; behavioral interventions such as biofeedback, relaxation training, and cognitive-behavioral therapy have demonstrated efficacy in migraine prevention; and the high comorbidity with mood and anxiety disorders necessitates careful differential diagnosis. Migraine aura symptoms can also be mistaken for transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) or focal seizures, making neurological referral appropriate when the presentation is atypical.

Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal, excessive, or hypersynchronous neuronal activity. It affects approximately 1% of the population.

Types of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on their origin and the extent of brain involvement, a distinction that carries significant implications for diagnosis and treatment.

Focal (Partial) Seizures

Originate in a localized area of one hemisphere:

  • Focal aware seizures (formerly "simple partial"): Consciousness is preserved. Symptoms depend on the brain region involved -- motor (jerking), sensory (tingling, visual disturbances), autonomic (nausea, sweating), or psychic (deja vu, fear, hallucinations). An aura is a focal aware seizure that precedes a larger seizure.
  • Focal impaired awareness seizures (formerly "complex partial"): Consciousness is altered. Most commonly originate in the temporal lobe. Typically feature automatisms (repetitive purposeless movements such as lip smacking, picking at clothes, or wandering). Patients are amnestic for the event.
  • Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic: Begins focally and then spreads to both hemispheres (secondarily generalized).

Generalized Seizures

Involve both hemispheres from the onset:

  • Tonic-clonic (formerly "grand mal"): A tonic phase (muscle rigidity, loss of consciousness, possible cry) followed by a clonic phase (rhythmic jerking of all extremities). Postictal confusion and sleepiness follow.
  • Absence (formerly "petit mal"): Brief (5-30 second) episodes of staring and unresponsiveness, often with subtle motor signs (eye blinking, lip movements). EEG shows characteristic 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern. Most common in children and may be mistaken for daydreaming or ADHD.
  • Myoclonic: Brief, shock-like muscle jerks
  • Atonic ("drop attacks"): Sudden loss of muscle tone causing falls
  • Tonic: Sustained muscle stiffening

Temporal Lobe Epilepsy (TLE)

The most common form of focal epilepsy in adults. Frequently involves the hippocampus and amygdala (mesial temporal sclerosis). Associated with:

  • Memory impairment (especially if dominant hemisphere is affected)
  • Interictal personality changes (Geschwind syndrome: hypergraphia, hyperreligiosity, altered sexuality, viscosity)
  • Psychiatric comorbidities: depression (30-50%), anxiety, and psychosis-like symptoms

Clinical Significance: The Wada test (intracarotid amobarbital procedure) is used before epilepsy surgery to determine hemispheric language dominance and memory lateralization. One hemisphere is anesthetized at a time while language and memory are tested. This information guides surgical decisions to avoid causing language or memory deficits.

Parkinson's Disease

Having explored epilepsy — a disorder characterized by abnormal electrical activity in the brain — we now turn to a very different kind of neurological disruption: the slow, progressive loss of neurons that produce one of the brain's most critical neurotransmitters. Imagine a patient who first notices that their hand trembles slightly while resting in their lap, then gradually finds it harder to button a shirt, rise from a chair, or walk without shuffling. Over months and years, movements become slower, muscles stiffer, and balance more precarious. This is the clinical trajectory of Parkinson's disease, a condition that powerfully illustrates how a single neurochemical deficit can cascade into widespread motor and cognitive dysfunction.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, a small but enormously influential structure in the midbrain. These neurons normally project to the striatum (the caudate nucleus and putamen) via the nigrostriatal pathway, providing the dopamine essential for smooth, coordinated voluntary movement. As these neurons degenerate — often with 60–80% already lost before motor symptoms become clinically apparent — dopamine levels in the striatum plummet, disrupting the delicate balance of excitatory and inhibitory signaling within the basal ganglia circuit. The result is the hallmark constellation of motor symptoms that clinicians recognize as parkinsonism.

For the EPPP, understanding Parkinson's disease matters not only because of its motor features, but because of the profound neuropsychological and psychiatric dimensions that accompany it. Psychologists frequently encounter PD patients who are struggling with depression, cognitive decline, or the behavioral side effects of dopaminergic medications. A solid grasp of the underlying neurobiology helps clinicians understand why these complications arise and how they interconnect with the motor disorder.

Cardinal Motor Symptoms (TRAP)

The four cardinal motor features of Parkinson's disease are conveniently remembered with the mnemonic TRAP: Tremor, Rigidity, Akinesia/Bradykinesia, and Postural instability. These symptoms typically have an insidious onset, often beginning unilaterally — a tremor in one hand, stiffness on one side of the body — before gradually becoming bilateral as the disease progresses. Each of these features reflects the disrupted basal ganglia circuitry that results from dopamine depletion.

  • Tremor: Resting tremor (4-6 Hz), often described as "pill-rolling" — as if the patient were rolling a small object between their thumb and fingers. This is a critically important diagnostic distinction: the parkinsonian tremor is most prominent at rest and actually decreases during intentional movement. This is the opposite of the intention tremor seen in cerebellar disorders, which worsens as the hand approaches a target. If an exam question describes a tremor that diminishes when the patient reaches for a cup, think Parkinson's; if it worsens during the reach, think cerebellum.
  • Rigidity: Increased muscle tone throughout the entire range of motion, felt by the examiner as resistance when passively moving the patient's limb. "Cogwheel rigidity" — a distinctive ratchet-like, start-and-stop resistance — is particularly characteristic and results from the combination of rigidity with an underlying tremor. This differs from the spasticity of upper motor neuron lesions, which is velocity-dependent (greater resistance with faster movement).
  • Akinesia/Bradykinesia: Slowness of movement (bradykinesia) or difficulty initiating movement (akinesia). This is often the most disabling feature of PD and manifests in numerous ways: reduced facial expressiveness known as masked facies (the patient's face appears blank or emotionless, which can be mistakenly interpreted as depression or apathy), small and cramped handwriting (micrographia), a characteristic shuffling gait with reduced arm swing, and difficulty with fine motor tasks like buttoning clothes. Clinically, a patient who takes progressively longer to complete daily activities — dressing, eating, walking to the mailbox — is demonstrating bradykinesia.
  • Postural instability: Impaired balance and righting reflexes, leading to increased risk of falls. This is typically a later symptom in the disease course, and its early presence should raise suspicion for atypical parkinsonism (such as progressive supranuclear palsy). Postural instability is tested clinically with the "pull test," in which the examiner stands behind the patient and gives a sudden backward tug on the shoulders; a patient with PD may take several corrective steps backward (retropulsion) or fall without correction.

Non-Motor Symptoms

One of the most important clinical insights about Parkinson's disease — and a frequent focus of EPPP questions — is that PD is far more than a movement disorder. The neurodegeneration extends well beyond the substantia nigra, involving brainstem nuclei, the limbic system, and eventually the cortex. This widespread pathology produces a constellation of non-motor symptoms that often cause as much suffering as the motor deficits, and some of these symptoms may appear years before any tremor or stiffness is noticed.

  • Depression: Affects 40-50% of PD patients and may precede motor symptoms by years. The depression of PD is not simply a psychological reaction to having a chronic illness — it reflects the disruption of dopaminergic, serotonergic, and noradrenergic pathways. Clinicians must be careful not to attribute depressive symptoms solely to the motor disability, as effective treatment of depression significantly improves quality of life and functional outcomes in PD.
  • Cognitive impairment: Executive dysfunction — difficulty with planning, set-shifting, working memory, and abstract reasoning — is the hallmark cognitive profile, reflecting frontal-subcortical circuit disruption. This pattern differs from Alzheimer's disease, where memory encoding deficits predominate early. However, as PD advances, approximately 75-80% of patients develop Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) over the disease course, eventually involving more widespread cognitive domains. The presence of prominent visual hallucinations and fluctuating cognition in a patient with parkinsonism and dementia should also raise consideration of Lewy body dementia, a closely related condition.
  • REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD): Often precedes motor diagnosis by years or even decades. In RBD, the normal muscle atonia during REM sleep is lost, causing patients to physically act out their dreams — sometimes violently. A bed partner who reports being kicked or punched during the night by someone who later develops PD illustrates this prodromal symptom.
  • Autonomic dysfunction: Constipation, orthostatic hypotension (dizziness upon standing), urinary dysfunction, and excessive sweating reflect degeneration of autonomic nervous system pathways.
  • Anosmia: Loss of smell is one of the earliest premotor symptoms of PD, sometimes preceding motor symptoms by a decade. It results from alpha-synuclein pathology in the olfactory bulb — one of the first structures affected in the disease process.
  • Psychosis: Visual hallucinations are particularly common, especially with advancing disease and as a side effect of dopaminergic medications. Patients may initially have benign "passage hallucinations" (seeing a figure briefly pass by in peripheral vision) before developing more formed and persistent visual hallucinations. This creates a difficult treatment dilemma: increasing dopaminergic medication to control motor symptoms may worsen psychosis, while reducing it may worsen motor function.

Treatment

Because Parkinson's disease results fundamentally from dopamine deficiency in the striatum, the treatment logic is straightforward in principle — restore dopamine signaling — but enormously complex in practice. Each major treatment strategy has characteristic benefits and limitations that are important for EPPP preparation, particularly the neuropsychiatric side effects that psychologists are likely to encounter in clinical settings.

  • L-dopa (levodopa): The dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine in the brain. Dopamine itself cannot cross the blood-brain barrier, so administering the precursor molecule is necessary. Levodopa is combined with carbidopa (which prevents peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine, reducing nausea and other peripheral side effects while ensuring more levodopa reaches the brain). Levodopa remains the most effective treatment for PD motor symptoms, but long-term use (typically after 5–10 years) is associated with motor fluctuations — the "wearing off" phenomenon (symptoms return before the next dose is due), the "on-off" phenomenon (unpredictable, sudden switching between mobile "on" states and immobile "off" states), and dyskinesias (involuntary, often writhing movements that occur at peak medication levels). These complications reflect the progressive loss of dopamine neurons and the brain's altered sensitivity to fluctuating dopamine levels.
  • Dopamine agonists: Medications such as pramipexole and ropinirole directly stimulate dopamine receptors, bypassing the need for conversion. They are sometimes used as initial therapy in younger patients to delay levodopa-related complications. A critically important side effect for psychologists to know: dopamine agonists can cause impulse control disorders in approximately 15-20% of patients, including pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping, and binge eating. A patient who has never gambled in their life but suddenly begins making daily trips to the casino after starting pramipexole is showing a classic medication-induced behavioral change — not a personality disorder.
  • MAO-B inhibitors: Selegiline and rasagiline prevent the enzymatic breakdown of dopamine by monoamine oxidase B, thereby prolonging dopamine's action in the synapse. These may be used as early monotherapy for mild symptoms or as adjuncts to levodopa.
  • Deep brain stimulation (DBS): A neurosurgical intervention in which electrodes are implanted in the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus, delivering continuous high-frequency electrical stimulation that modulates abnormal basal ganglia circuit activity. DBS is reserved for patients with medication-refractory motor fluctuations and dyskinesias, and it can dramatically improve motor function and reduce medication requirements. Importantly, DBS does not halt disease progression, and patients with significant cognitive impairment or dementia are generally not candidates for the procedure.

For the EPPP, the key neuropsychological distinction is this: Parkinson's disease produces a subcortical dementia profile — characterized by slowed processing speed, executive dysfunction, and retrieval-based memory deficits (patients benefit from cues and recognition testing) — in contrast to the cortical dementia of Alzheimer's disease, where encoding deficits mean that cues and recognition do not significantly help recall. Understanding this distinction, along with the motor features, psychiatric comorbidities, and medication side effects, provides a comprehensive framework for approaching PD questions on the exam.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has received FDA approval for several movement disorders beyond idiopathic Parkinson's disease. Currently approved indications include essential tremor and dystonia, in addition to PD. DBS has also shown promise in treatment-resistant obsessive-compulsive disorder and is being investigated for major depression and other conditions. A critical distinction for clinical practice is that DBS is not indicated for atypical parkinsonism syndromes, such as progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), multiple system atrophy (MSA), or corticobasal degeneration. These conditions involve more widespread neurodegeneration beyond the nigrostriatal pathway and respond poorly to both dopaminergic medications and DBS. The differential diagnosis between idiopathic PD and atypical parkinsonian syndromes is therefore essential before proceeding with surgical intervention.

Beyond their role in Parkinson's disease management, dopamine agonists such as pramipexole and ropinirole have an additional well-established clinical application: they are first-line pharmacological treatments for restless leg syndrome (RLS), a sensorimotor disorder characterized by an uncomfortable urge to move the legs, typically worsening during rest and in the evening. The efficacy of dopamine agonists in RLS supports the hypothesis that this condition involves dopaminergic dysfunction. Clinicians should be aware that the same impulse control side effects observed in PD patients—compulsive gambling, hypersexuality, and excessive spending—can also emerge when these medications are prescribed for RLS, even at the lower doses typically used.

Huntington's Disease

Huntington's disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion on chromosome 4 (huntingtin gene). Onset is typically in the 30s-40s. Characterized by the triad of:

  • Chorea: Involuntary, irregular, writhing, dance-like movements (the opposite of Parkinson's hypokinesia)
  • Cognitive decline: Subcortical dementia pattern with executive dysfunction, slowed processing speed, and memory retrieval deficits (as opposed to the encoding deficits in Alzheimer's)
  • Psychiatric symptoms: Depression, irritability, apathy, and psychosis; personality and mood changes often precede motor symptoms by years

Neuropathology: Huntington's disease involves progressive atrophy of the caudate nucleus and putamen (striatum), with loss of GABAergic inhibitory neurons. The loss of inhibitory output from the striatum produces excess, uncontrolled movement (chorea). There is no cure; treatment is symptomatic. Genetic testing is available and raises significant ethical considerations for at-risk family members.

Because Huntington's disease follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, only a single copy of the mutated huntingtin gene is sufficient to cause the disorder. When one parent carries the expanded CAG repeat on one copy of chromosome 4 (heterozygous), each biological offspring has a 50% probability of inheriting the affected allele and eventually developing the disease. This risk applies equally regardless of the child's sex, since the gene is located on an autosome rather than a sex chromosome. The combination of complete penetrance (virtually all carriers develop symptoms if they live long enough), delayed onset well into adulthood, and the availability of presymptomatic genetic testing creates profound ethical dilemmas. Individuals at risk must weigh the psychological impact of a positive result—knowing they will develop an incurable, fatal illness—against the potential benefits of informed family planning and early preparation. Genetic counseling is strongly recommended before testing is pursued.

Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system in which the immune system attacks myelin sheaths and the oligodendrocytes that produce them. This results in demyelinating plaques (lesions) scattered throughout the CNS white matter.

  • Demographics: Typically diagnosed between ages 20-40; more common in women (2-3:1 ratio) and in populations farther from the equator
  • Symptoms are variable and depend on the location of plaques: visual disturbances (optic neuritis), motor weakness, sensory changes (numbness, tingling), fatigue, bladder dysfunction, and cognitive impairment (slowed processing, attention and memory deficits)
  • Relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS): The most common form (85%); discrete attacks followed by partial or full recovery
  • Primary progressive MS: Steady worsening from onset without distinct relapses (10-15%)
  • Secondary progressive MS: Initially relapsing-remitting, then transitions to progressive worsening

Clinical Significance: MS lesions are disseminated in space (multiple CNS locations) and time (occurring on different occasions). Charcot's triad (nystagmus, intention tremor, scanning speech) is a classic but not always present presentation. Cognitive impairment affects 40-65% of MS patients and can occur early in the disease course.

Stroke (Cerebrovascular Accident)

Stroke is an acute neurological event caused by disruption of blood supply to the brain, resulting in rapid death of neural tissue. It is a leading cause of disability and the presentation depends on which artery and brain region are affected.

Hypertension (high blood pressure) is one of the most significant modifiable risk factors for stroke, contributing to both ischemic and hemorrhagic events. Understanding the distinction between the two forms of hypertension is clinically relevant for stroke prevention and management.

Primary vs. Secondary Hypertension

Primary (essential) hypertension has no identifiable underlying medical cause — it develops gradually over many years and accounts for approximately 90-95% of all hypertension cases. Contributing factors include genetics, age, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, excessive sodium intake, and chronic stress. Secondary hypertension, by contrast, results from an identifiable and often treatable condition, such as renal artery stenosis, endocrine disorders (e.g., Cushing's syndrome, pheochromocytoma), or medication side effects. The key distinguishing factor between these two forms is whether or not a specific etiology can be identified.

Chronic hypertension damages cerebral blood vessels in multiple ways: it accelerates atherosclerosis (increasing ischemic stroke risk), promotes the formation of small vessel disease and lacunar infarcts in deep brain structures, and weakens arterial walls leading to aneurysm formation and potential rupture (hemorrhagic stroke). Sustained elevated blood pressure can also produce hypertensive encephalopathy, a medical emergency characterized by severe headache, confusion, visual changes, and seizures. For clinicians, recognizing hypertension as a stroke risk factor underscores the importance of behavioral interventions — including diet modification, exercise, stress management, and medication adherence — that psychologists may support in integrated care settings.

Common Stroke Syndromes

  • Middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke: Most common. Contralateral hemiparesis and hemisensory loss (face and arm greater than leg); aphasia if left hemisphere (Broca's, Wernicke's, or global); hemispatial neglect if right hemisphere.
  • Anterior cerebral artery (ACA) stroke: Contralateral leg weakness greater than arm; personality changes; abulia (loss of will/motivation); urinary incontinence.
  • Posterior cerebral artery (PCA) stroke: Contralateral homonymous hemianopsia (visual field loss); memory impairment if medial temporal structures are affected.

When evaluating stroke presentations, it is essential to match the affected vascular territory to the expected symptom profile. The middle cerebral artery (MCA) supplies the lateral surfaces of the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, which means MCA strokes produce contralateral motor and sensory deficits (predominantly in the face and upper extremity), language impairments in the dominant hemisphere, and hemispatial neglect in the non-dominant hemisphere. Critically, the MCA does not supply the occipital cortex or the primary visual areas responsible for bilateral vision. Bilateral vision loss (cortical blindness) results from damage to both occipital lobes, which are perfused by the posterior cerebral arteries (PCAs). A unilateral PCA stroke produces contralateral homonymous hemianopsia, while bilateral PCA involvement can cause complete cortical blindness. Therefore, if a clinical vignette asks which symptom is least likely associated with an MCA stroke, bilateral visual loss is the correct answer.

When a middle cerebral artery stroke involves the dominant hemisphere (typically the left), the clinical presentation extends beyond the motor and sensory deficits seen with non-dominant involvement. The expected syndrome includes contralateral hemiparesis (with face and upper extremity more severely affected than the leg, reflecting the MCA's vascular territory on the motor homunculus), contralateral homonymous hemianopia (loss of the same visual field in both eyes due to involvement of the optic radiations), dysarthria (impaired motor speech production), and — critically — aphasia. The specific aphasia type depends on the precise distribution of the infarct: anterior MCA branches supply Broca's area (producing nonfluent/expressive aphasia), posterior branches supply Wernicke's area (producing fluent/receptive aphasia), and large territorial infarcts involving both regions result in global aphasia. This constellation of motor, visual, speech, and language deficits in dominant-hemisphere MCA stroke contrasts with the non-dominant hemisphere presentation, which characteristically produces hemispatial neglect and anosognosia rather than aphasia.

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

Traumatic brain injury results from an external mechanical force causing brain dysfunction. Severity is classified by Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, duration of loss of consciousness, and duration of post-traumatic amnesia (PTA).

Types of TBI Damage

Selecting the appropriate neuroimaging technique is a critical clinical decision following traumatic brain injury. Computed tomography (CT) is generally regarded as the most useful initial imaging modality for evaluating acute TBI. Several practical advantages make CT the preferred choice in emergency settings. The scan can be completed in seconds to minutes, which is essential when dealing with a potentially deteriorating patient. CT scanners are available in virtually all emergency departments, and the procedure is relatively inexpensive compared to other advanced imaging options. CT is also highly effective at detecting acute intracranial hemorrhage, skull fractures, and mass effect (brain shift due to swelling or bleeding).

By contrast, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while offering superior soft tissue resolution and greater sensitivity to diffuse axonal injury, small contusions, and subacute pathology, has significant limitations in the acute trauma setting. MRI requires the patient to remain motionless inside the scanner for an extended period — often 20 to 45 minutes — which is impractical for agitated, unstable, or unconscious patients who may need continuous monitoring and intervention. MRI is also contraindicated when metallic foreign bodies or certain implanted devices are present, a concern that may not be immediately assessable in trauma patients. For these reasons, CT remains the standard first-line neuroimaging study for acute TBI, with MRI reserved for follow-up evaluation when more detailed characterization of brain injury is needed.

Clinical Note: Neuroimaging in Acute TBI

When a patient presents to the emergency department following a motor vehicle accident or other traumatic injury, the clinician must rapidly determine whether there is intracranial bleeding (hematoma) or a focal area of bruised brain tissue (contusion). In this acute scenario, a CT scan (computed tomography) is the neuroimaging modality of choice. CT is fast, widely accessible in emergency settings, and highly sensitive to acute hemorrhage, which appears as a bright (hyperdense) signal on the scan. For example, a middle-aged man brought to the ER after a car accident with altered consciousness would most likely receive a non-contrast CT of the head as the first imaging study. This allows clinicians to quickly distinguish between epidural hematomas, subdural hematomas, intracerebral hemorrhage, and cortical contusions — each of which may require different urgent interventions, including possible surgical evacuation.

  • Coup injury: Damage at the point of impact
  • Contrecoup injury: Damage at the opposite side of the brain from the impact, caused by the brain bouncing against the skull
  • Diffuse axonal injury (DAI): Widespread shearing of axons due to rotational forces; the most common cause of severe TBI disability
  • Epidural hematoma: Bleeding between skull and dura mater, typically from middle meningeal artery rupture. "Lucid interval" followed by rapid deterioration.
  • Subdural hematoma: Bleeding between dura and arachnoid, from bridging vein rupture. Can be acute or chronic (common in elderly, alcoholics).

Neuropsychological Sequelae

The frontal and temporal lobes are most vulnerable to TBI due to their position against bony skull protrusions:

  • Executive dysfunction (planning, inhibition, cognitive flexibility)
  • Memory impairment (anterograde greater than retrograde)
  • Attention and processing speed deficits
  • Personality and behavioral changes (disinhibition, irritability, apathy)
  • Emotional dysregulation (increased risk of depression, anxiety, PTSD)

Shrinking Retrograde Amnesia

A recovery pattern following head injury in which retrograde amnesia gradually diminishes over time, with older (more remote) memories returning before more recently formed ones. The period of permanent memory loss "shrinks" toward the moment of injury as recovery progresses.

After a traumatic brain injury that produces retrograde amnesia, memory recovery typically follows a predictable temporal gradient consistent with Ribot's Law (sometimes called the "law of regression"). According to this principle, recently acquired memories are more vulnerable to disruption than well-consolidated, remote memories. During recovery, distant memories—such as childhood events or long-held factual knowledge—tend to be restored first, while memories formed closer to the time of the injury are recovered later, if at all. This progressive narrowing of the amnestic gap is termed shrinking retrograde amnesia. The phenomenon is attributed to the fact that older memories have undergone more extensive consolidation and are stored across widely distributed cortical networks, making them more resilient to disruption. Newer memories, which may still depend heavily on hippocampal and medial temporal lobe circuits at the time of injury, are more fragile and susceptible to permanent loss. In many cases, a brief period of amnesia immediately preceding the trauma remains permanent, even after substantial recovery of more remote memories.

Example

A 28-year-old man sustains a moderate TBI in a motor vehicle accident. Initially, he cannot recall events from the past three years. Over the following weeks, he progressively recovers memories—first from college, then from his early career—but he never regains memory of the two days immediately before the accident. This pattern of recovery from the most remote to the most recent exemplifies shrinking retrograde amnesia.

The pattern of memory recovery following TBI typically follows a well-established temporal gradient known as shrinking retrograde amnesia (also called Ribot's gradient of recovery). When retrograde amnesia is present after a closed head injury, memories do not return all at once. Instead, the oldest and most remote memories tend to be recovered first, while events temporally closest to the injury are the last to return — and may never be fully recovered. This pattern reflects the principle that older memories have undergone more extensive consolidation and are stored across widely distributed cortical networks, making them more resistant to disruption. More recent memories, which are still dependent on hippocampal and medial temporal lobe consolidation processes, are most vulnerable to traumatic disruption. For example, a 54-year-old woman who sustains a closed head injury and develops retrograde amnesia spanning several years would be expected to recall events from the distant past (such as a family gathering that occurred years before the injury) before recovering memories of events from the weeks or months immediately preceding the trauma. This progressive "shrinkage" of the amnestic gap — from remote memories inward toward the time of injury — is a hallmark feature of post-traumatic memory recovery that distinguishes it from other amnesia patterns.

Aphasia is an acquired language disorder resulting from brain damage. The major aphasia types are among the most frequently tested neurological topics on the EPPP.
