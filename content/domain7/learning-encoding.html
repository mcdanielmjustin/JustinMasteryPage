How We Learn — Encoding Strategies & Levels of Processing

Fundamental Content
Flashcards Quick Quiz: Fundamental Advanced Learning

Levels of Processing Framework

A psychology intern reads the same textbook chapter three times before her licensing exam, highlighting passages in neon yellow until the pages glow like a nightclub floor. Her study partner reads the chapter once, then closes the book and tries to explain each concept aloud, connecting it to patients she has seen in practicum. When exam day arrives, the second intern dramatically outperforms the first. The explanation for this difference — why the method of encoding matters far more than the number of exposures — lies at the heart of one of the most influential frameworks in memory research.

The levels of processing framework (Craik & Lockhart, 1972) challenged the multi-store model covered in Chapter 4 by arguing that memory durability depends not on which "store" information enters, but on the depth at which it is processed during encoding. The hippocampal consolidation machinery that makes deep encoding possible is detailed in Chapter 5.

Depth of Processing:

Shallow processing involves attending to surface-level features of stimuli, such as the physical appearance of a word (structural encoding) or its sound (phonemic encoding). Deep processing involves attending to meaning and making semantic connections (semantic encoding). Deep processing produces significantly stronger and more durable memory traces than shallow processing.

Levels of Processing: Shallow to Deep
Structural
Physical features — font, shape, appearance
Phonemic
Sound — rhyme, pronunciation
Semantic
Meaning, elaboration, connections — strongest retention

Elaborative vs. Maintenance Rehearsal

Maintenance rehearsal involves simple repetition of information without engaging with its meaning, as when repeating a phone number to keep it in short-term memory. This form of rehearsal produces minimal long-term retention. In contrast, elaborative rehearsal involves connecting new information to existing knowledge, generating examples, and thinking about meaning. Elaborative rehearsal promotes deep processing and robust long-term encoding.

EPPP Example:

A student who reads the definition of "proactive interference" and then generates her own example of how previously learned Spanish vocabulary interferes with learning French vocabulary is engaging in elaborative rehearsal. A student who simply rereads the definition ten times is engaging in maintenance rehearsal. The first strategy produces far superior retention.

Craik, F. I. M., & Lockhart, R. S. (1972). Levels of processing: A framework for memory research. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 11(6), 671-684.

Encoding Specificity & Context Effects

The encoding specificity principle, articulated by Tulving and Thomson (1973), states that memory is most effective when the conditions at retrieval match the conditions present during encoding. This principle has two major applications tested on the EPPP.

  • Godden & Baddeley: divers recalled better in same location
  • Study in varied environments to reduce dependency

Context-Dependent Memory

Context-dependent memory refers to the finding that recall is improved when the external environment at retrieval matches the environment present during encoding. Godden and Baddeley (1975) famously demonstrated this with scuba divers who learned word lists either underwater or on land — recall was significantly better when the learning and testing environments matched.

State-Dependent Memory

State-dependent memory refers to the finding that recall is enhanced when an individual's internal physiological or psychological state at retrieval matches the state during encoding. This has been demonstrated with mood states (mood-dependent memory), pharmacological states, and arousal levels.

Clinical Relevance:

State-dependent memory has important implications for psychotherapy. Traumatic memories encoded during high-arousal states may be difficult to access during the calm conditions of a therapy session. Conversely, therapeutic insights gained in session may be harder to retrieve when the patient is in a distressed state outside of therapy. This principle also explains why substance-related memories may be more accessible under the influence of the same substance.

Tulving, E., & Thomson, D. M. (1973). Encoding specificity and retrieval processes in episodic memory. Psychological Review, 80(5), 352-373.

Self-Reference & Generation Effects

We've just seen how the context surrounding encoding — the physical environment, your emotional state, even the substances in your bloodstream — shapes what you can later retrieve. Now consider an even more powerful encoding context: yourself. What happens when the material you're trying to learn becomes personally meaningful, woven into the narrative of who you are? And what happens when you actively produce information rather than passively consume it? These two phenomena — the self-reference effect and the generation effect — rank among the most potent encoding strategies researchers have identified, and they carry direct implications for how you should prepare for the EPPP.

Imagine a study in which participants view a list of personality trait words — "generous," "anxious," "creative" — and process each word under different instructions. One group judges whether the word is printed in uppercase letters (structural processing). Another group judges whether the word means the same thing as a synonym (semantic processing). A third group answers a simple but deeply personal question: "Does this word describe you?" When recall is tested later, the third group dramatically outperforms the others — even outperforming the deep semantic processing condition that levels-of-processing theory would predict as optimal. This robust finding is the self-reference effect: information encoded in relation to the self is remembered better than information encoded in virtually any other way. The self acts as an extraordinarily rich and well-organized schema — a dense network of personal experiences, goals, emotions, and beliefs — that provides countless hooks on which new information can be hung. When you connect a new concept to your own life ("I've seen this pattern in my clinical practicum"), you are not just processing deeply; you are anchoring the information within the most elaborated cognitive structure you possess.

Clinically, the self-reference effect helps explain why narrative and autobiographical approaches in therapy can be so powerful. When a client reframes a maladaptive belief by connecting it to specific personal experiences, the new understanding becomes deeply encoded. For your own study, this means that pausing to ask "How does this concept relate to my clinical work or my personal experience?" transforms passive reading into a self-referential encoding opportunity that dramatically boosts retention.

Generation Effect:

The generation effect demonstrates that learners remember information far better when they actively produce it than when they passively read it. For example, completing the fragment "Proactive inter_____" produces stronger memory traces than simply reading "Proactive interference." By forcing the learner to search memory and construct the answer, the act of generation engages deeper cognitive processing, creates more distinctive retrieval cues, and strengthens the pathways that will later support recall. This effect provides the empirical foundation for active learning strategies, including the testing effect discussed below.

Think about the difference between reading a definition of "retrograde amnesia" in your notes versus covering the definition and trying to produce it from memory. The second experience feels harder — and that desirable difficulty is precisely what makes it more effective. When you generate an answer, you activate related concepts, resolve ambiguity, and commit cognitive effort that passive reading never demands. A clinical parallel exists in motivational interviewing: when a client generates their own reasons for change rather than hearing the therapist list them, those reasons carry more personal weight and are more readily recalled later. The generation effect operates on the same principle — ownership through production.

The Testing Effect (Retrieval Practice)

The generation effect leads naturally to one of the most robust and practically important findings in all of learning science: the testing effect, also called retrieval practice. Here is the counterintuitive insight — taking a test is not merely a way to assess what you know; it is one of the most powerful ways to learn. Every time you practice retrieving information from memory — through self-testing, flashcards, practice questions, or even simply trying to recall a concept before looking it up — you strengthen the underlying memory trace and make that information more accessible for future retrieval. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) demonstrated this dramatically: students who practiced retrieving material retained it significantly better one week later than students who spent the same total amount of time restudying the material. The restudying group felt more confident immediately afterward, but the testing group showed superior long-term retention — a finding that challenges the intuition that rereading is productive study.

Why does retrieval practice work so well? Each act of retrieval reconstructs and reconsolidates the memory, elaborating the network of cues that can trigger recall in the future. Retrieval also provides feedback — you discover what you know and what you don't — allowing you to target your study more efficiently. Furthermore, the effort involved in retrieval creates a form of desirable difficulty that mirrors the generation effect: the harder you work to pull information from memory, the more durable that memory becomes.

Study Strategy Application:

For EPPP preparation, the testing effect carries a clear and actionable message: repeatedly taking practice tests produces far more durable learning than repeatedly rereading your study materials. Each act of retrieval strengthens the memory trace and builds the retrieval pathways you will need on exam day. Consider closing your notes after studying a section and writing down everything you can remember, then checking what you missed. Use flashcards that require you to generate answers rather than simply recognize them. Combine retrieval practice with the self-reference effect by relating each recalled concept to a clinical case you have encountered. These strategies harness the generation effect and the testing effect together — transforming study time from passive review into active, self-relevant encoding that maximizes long-term retention.

Spacing Effect & Distributed Practice

The spacing effect (Ebbinghaus, 1885; Cepeda et al., 2006) is the finding that distributing study sessions over time produces superior long-term retention compared to massing the same amount of study into a single session (cramming). The spacing effect is one of the most replicable findings in all of experimental psychology.

The optimal inter-study interval depends on the retention interval: for a test one week away, spacing sessions 1-2 days apart is optimal; for a test one month away, spacing sessions one week apart is more effective. This principle interacts with the testing effect — spaced retrieval practice produces the strongest retention of all.

Dual Coding Theory & Mnemonic Strategies

Dual coding theory (Paivio, 1971) proposes that information is stored in two distinct but interconnected coding systems: a verbal system (for linguistic information) and an imaginal system (for visual-spatial information). Information encoded in both systems — such as a word paired with a vivid mental image — creates two retrieval routes and is therefore more memorable than information encoded in only one system.

Mnemonic Strategies

Mnemonic devices leverage dual coding and elaborative processing to enhance memory. Key techniques tested on the EPPP include:

  • Method of loci: Associating items to be remembered with specific locations along a familiar mental route. This ancient technique creates vivid spatial-visual associations and is highly effective for ordered recall.
  • Keyword method: Used for vocabulary learning; the learner creates a concrete image linking the sound of the unfamiliar word to the meaning through an intermediary keyword. Particularly effective for foreign language acquisition.
  • Pegword method: Items are associated with a pre-memorized sequence of rhyming cue words (e.g., "one is a bun, two is a shoe"). Useful for learning numbered lists.
Transfer-Appropriate Processing:

The transfer-appropriate processing principle (Morris, Bransford, & Franks, 1977) states that memory performance depends on the match between the type of processing at encoding and the type of processing required at retrieval. Semantic encoding produces superior performance on meaning-based tests, while phonemic encoding may actually produce better performance on rhyme-recognition tests. This principle refines the levels of processing framework by emphasizing that "deeper" is not always "better" — what matters is the match between encoding and retrieval demands.

Paivio, A. (1971). Imagery and verbal processes. New York: Holt, Rinehart, and Winston. — Paivio's dual coding theory remains influential in understanding how visual imagery enhances verbal learning.

Encoding Failure as a Cause of Forgetting

Having explored the many strategies that enhance encoding — from dual coding and mnemonic devices to elaborative processing and transfer-appropriate retrieval — a natural question arises: what happens when encoding doesn't occur at all? We've spent this chapter discussing how to get information into long-term memory more effectively, but some of the most common everyday "memory failures" aren't really failures of memory at all. They are failures of encoding — moments when information never made it into storage in the first place.

Consider this deceptively simple challenge: without looking, try to draw the front of a U.S. penny from memory. Include the direction Lincoln faces, the location of the date, and the placement of the word "Liberty." Most people — even those who have handled thousands of pennies over their lifetimes — perform remarkably poorly on this task. The classic penny experiment (Nickerson & Adams, 1979) demonstrated exactly this phenomenon, and the explanation is elegantly straightforward. We don't fail to recall the details of a penny because we once knew them and forgot. We fail because we never truly encoded those details in the first place. We process pennies at just the level needed to distinguish them from other coins — their color, size, and general shape — and nothing more. The specific spatial arrangement of features on the coin's face was never attended to with enough depth to enter long-term memory.

This is the essence of encoding failure: information that appears to have been "forgotten" was actually never stored in long-term memory to begin with. Encoding failure typically occurs because the person did not attend to the information sufficiently or did not process it at a depth that would support durable storage. From the perspective of the levels of processing framework we discussed earlier, encoding failure represents the extreme end of shallow processing — processing so minimal that no lasting memory trace is formed at all. It is a reminder that attention is the gateway to memory, and without it, even thousands of exposures may leave no retrievable trace.

Clinical Application:

Encoding failure is particularly relevant to understanding memory complaints in older adults and individuals with attentional disorders. Many reported "memory problems" actually reflect attentional or encoding deficits rather than retrieval failures. For example, an individual with ADHD who reports being unable to remember where they placed their keys may never have encoded the location in the first place due to divided attention at the time of placement. Similarly, an older adult who complains of "losing" names at a dinner party may not have processed those names deeply during introductions — perhaps because they were simultaneously navigating social anxiety or a noisy environment. Distinguishing encoding failures from retrieval failures has important diagnostic implications: if the problem lies in encoding, interventions should target attentional strategies and environmental modifications at the time of learning, rather than retrieval cues after the fact. On the EPPP, recognizing whether a clinical vignette describes an encoding deficit versus a retrieval deficit is a commonly tested distinction.

Encoding failure also helps clarify a broader point about the nature of forgetting itself. Psychologists distinguish among several causes of forgetting — including decay, interference, and retrieval failure — but encoding failure is unique because it challenges the very premise that something was "forgotten." You cannot lose what you never had. This distinction matters clinically, theoretically, and practically, because the intervention for encoding failure is fundamentally different from the intervention for retrieval difficulty. When a student rereads a textbook chapter passively and then performs poorly on an exam, the problem is often not that they "forgot" the material but that they never encoded it meaningfully. Their eyes moved across the page, but their mind was elsewhere.

Understanding encoding processes is essential for the EPPP not only as direct test content, but also for optimizing your own study strategies. The research consistently supports the superiority of active, elaborative, spaced, and retrieval-based approaches over passive rereading and massed practice. Every concept we've covered in this chapter — from levels of processing to dual coding, from the spacing effect to transfer-appropriate processing — ultimately serves the same purpose: ensuring that information is encoded deeply enough and in the right way to be accessible when you need it most.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Levels of processing (Craik & Lockhart): deep/semantic processing produces better memory than shallow/structural processing
  • Elaborative rehearsal (connecting to meaning) produces better retention than maintenance rehearsal (simple repetition)
  • Encoding specificity principle (Tulving & Thomson): retrieval is best when encoding and retrieval conditions match
  • Context-dependent memory: external environment match enhances recall; State-dependent memory: internal state match enhances recall
  • Self-reference effect: relating information to oneself enhances encoding
  • Generation effect: actively producing information is better than passively reading it
  • Testing effect (retrieval practice): self-testing is more effective than restudying for long-term retention
  • Spacing effect: distributed practice produces superior retention compared to massed practice
  • Dual coding theory (Paivio): encoding in both verbal and visual systems creates two retrieval routes
  • Method of loci: mental walk through familiar locations; Keyword method: linking sounds to images; Pegword: rhyming cue words
  • Transfer-appropriate processing: memory depends on the match between encoding type and retrieval demands, not just "depth"
  • Encoding failure: forgetting because information was never adequately encoded in the first place
