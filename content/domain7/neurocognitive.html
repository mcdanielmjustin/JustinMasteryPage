DSM-5 Neurocognitive Disorder Framework

A 74-year-old retired teacher is brought to a neuropsychology clinic by her daughter, who reports that her mother has been repeating the same questions within minutes, misplacing her car keys in bizarre locations (the refrigerator, the mailbox), and getting lost driving home from the grocery store she has visited for thirty years. The daughter asks a deceptively simple question: "Is this just normal aging, or is something wrong?" Answering that question — distinguishing the mild forgetfulness that accompanies healthy aging from the insidious onset of a neurodegenerative disease — is one of the most important clinical skills a psychologist can develop, and the DSM-5 neurocognitive disorder framework provides the diagnostic scaffold for making that distinction with precision.

The DSM-5 replaced the older term "dementia" with neurocognitive disorder (NCD), organizing these conditions along a severity spectrum. This framework emphasizes that cognitive decline occurs on a continuum and recognizes multiple etiological subtypes.

Major vs. Mild Neurocognitive Disorder:

Major NCD involves significant cognitive decline from a previous level of performance in one or more cognitive domains (complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor, or social cognition) that interferes with independence in everyday activities. Mild NCD involves modest cognitive decline that does not interfere with independence in everyday activities, though greater effort, compensatory strategies, or accommodation may be needed. The distinction between major and mild NCD is primarily functional: can the person still manage daily activities independently?

The six cognitive domains assessed in the DSM-5 NCD framework are:

Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of major NCD, accounting for 60-80% of dementia cases. Its pathology targets the hippocampal memory circuits covered in Chapter 5 and the cholinergic neurotransmitter systems from Chapter 7. It is characterized by insidious onset, gradual progression, and a distinctive neuropathological profile.

Neuropathology:

The hallmark pathological features of AD are: (1) Amyloid plaques (extracellular deposits of beta-amyloid protein), (2) Neurofibrillary tangles (intracellular aggregates of hyperphosphorylated tau protein), and (3) Widespread neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction. The pathology typically begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus (explaining the early memory symptoms) and progressively spreads to the temporal, parietal, and eventually frontal cortices. The cholinergic hypothesis proposes that degeneration of cholinergic neurons in the nucleus basalis of Meynert, with resulting acetylcholine deficiency, is a primary contributor to cognitive decline. This is the basis for cholinesterase inhibitor treatment (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine).

Recent neuropathological research has shifted understanding of where Alzheimer's pathology first emerges. While the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex have long been recognized as early sites of clinical symptoms, accumulating evidence points to the locus coeruleus — a small noradrenergic nucleus in the brainstem — as the earliest site of tau pathology. Studies have demonstrated that hyperphosphorylated tau deposits appear in the locus coeruleus decades before cognitive symptoms manifest and before pathological changes are detectable in the medial temporal lobe. This has led some researchers to describe the locus coeruleus as "ground zero" for the disease process.

Clinical Note

The locus coeruleus is the brain's primary source of norepinephrine, a neurotransmitter critical for arousal, attention, and stress responses. Its early degeneration in AD may explain subtle changes in attention, sleep-wake regulation, and anxiety that sometimes precede overt memory decline. The discovery that tau pathology originates in this brainstem structure has broadened the conceptualization of AD beyond a purely cortical disease and has implications for early biomarker development. Noradrenergic dysfunction resulting from locus coeruleus degeneration may also contribute to the neuroinflammatory processes that accelerate disease progression.

The neuropathological changes of Alzheimer's disease do not affect all brain regions equally. The earliest and most severe accumulation of both neuritic plaques (dense-core amyloid deposits surrounded by dystrophic neurites) and neurofibrillary tangles (twisted filaments of hyperphosphorylated tau) occurs within the medial temporal lobe structures — specifically the entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala. The entorhinal cortex serves as the primary gateway between the hippocampus and neocortex, and its early involvement disrupts the encoding of new episodic memories. Hippocampal degeneration further compounds this memory encoding and consolidation failure. Amygdala involvement contributes to the emotional and behavioral changes observed in AD, including apathy, anxiety, and impaired emotional processing. This medial temporal concentration of pathology directly accounts for the clinical hallmark of early AD: profound difficulty forming and retaining new memories while older, well-consolidated memories remain relatively accessible in the initial stages.

Clinical Course

Personality traits have emerged as an area of growing interest in Alzheimer's disease risk research. Among the Big Five personality dimensions, two traits have been most consistently associated with elevated risk for developing AD. Individuals who score high on neuroticism — characterized by chronic negative emotionality, stress reactivity, and psychological distress — show a significantly increased likelihood of receiving an AD diagnosis. Conversely, individuals who score low on conscientiousness — reflecting reduced self-discipline, goal-directed behavior, and organizational capacity — also demonstrate heightened vulnerability. The combination of high neuroticism and low conscientiousness represents the personality profile most reliably linked to AD risk across longitudinal studies.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain these associations. High neuroticism may increase chronic cortisol exposure through sustained stress activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which can damage hippocampal neurons over time. Low conscientiousness may operate through behavioral pathways: individuals lower on this trait are less likely to engage in health-promoting behaviors such as regular exercise, medication adherence, cognitive stimulation, and management of cardiovascular risk factors. Additionally, conscientiousness may reflect underlying frontal lobe integrity, meaning that low scores could represent early, subtle neurodegeneration rather than a purely premorbid trait. These personality-risk associations remain significant even after controlling for education, depression, and other known risk factors.

Clinical Note

When conducting neuropsychological evaluations for suspected AD, clinicians should consider premorbid personality characteristics as part of the risk profile. Collateral informants can help establish whether personality changes (e.g., increased irritability, decreased organization) represent a shift from baseline — potentially signaling early neurodegeneration — or reflect longstanding traits that may have contributed to overall risk. The neuroticism-conscientiousness pattern is distinct from the personality changes seen in frontotemporal dementia, which typically involve disinhibition and loss of empathy rather than anxiety-driven distress.

Emerging research has identified olfactory dysfunction as one of the earliest detectable signs of Alzheimer's disease, often preceding clinically apparent cognitive decline by several years. Specifically, a rapid deterioration in the ability to detect and identify odors has been associated with increased risk of progressing from normal cognition to mild cognitive impairment and subsequently to AD dementia. This olfactory decline reflects early neurodegeneration in structures involved in smell processing — particularly the entorhinal cortex and olfactory bulb — which are among the first regions to accumulate tau pathology and amyloid deposits.

Example

A 68-year-old patient reports to her primary care physician that food has progressively "lost its taste" over the past year and she can no longer smell her morning coffee. Formal olfactory testing reveals significant impairment in odor identification. Although her MMSE score is within normal limits, longitudinal follow-up reveals that she develops episodic memory deficits 18 months later and is eventually diagnosed with probable AD. Her early smell loss, in retrospect, was a prodromal indicator of the neurodegenerative process already underway in medial temporal structures.

Smell testing instruments such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) have been investigated as potential screening tools for preclinical AD. While olfactory decline alone is not diagnostic — it also occurs in Parkinson's disease, normal aging, and other conditions — a rapid decline in smell function, particularly when combined with other biomarkers or risk factors such as APOE-e4 carrier status, may serve as a useful early warning signal warranting further neuropsychological evaluation.

  • Early stage: Episodic memory impairment (forgetting recent events, repeating questions), word-finding difficulty, misplacing items, mild executive dysfunction. Procedural memory and remote memories are relatively preserved. Patients may show anosognosia (lack of awareness of deficits).
  • Middle stage: More pronounced memory loss (may not recognize family members), visuospatial disorientation (getting lost), language deterioration (fluent but empty speech), apraxia (difficulty with learned motor sequences), behavioral symptoms (agitation, wandering, sundowning, delusions of theft, paranoia).
  • Late stage: Severe cognitive decline across all domains, loss of ability to speak, walk, and swallow. Incontinence, bedbound state. Average duration from diagnosis to death is 8-10 years.
Risk Factors & Genetics:

The APOE-e4 allele is the strongest genetic risk factor for late-onset AD: one copy increases risk 3-4 fold; two copies increase risk 12-15 fold. However, APOE-e4 is a risk factor, not a deterministic gene -- not all carriers develop AD, and many AD patients lack the allele. Early-onset AD (before age 65, <5% of cases) has stronger genetic determinism, linked to mutations in APP (amyloid precursor protein, chromosome 21), presenilin 1 (chromosome 14), and presenilin 2 (chromosome 1). The association with chromosome 21 explains the markedly elevated AD risk in Down syndrome (trisomy 21). Other modifiable risk factors include cardiovascular disease, diabetes, low education, social isolation, head injury, and hearing loss.

EPPP Distinction — Cortical vs. Subcortical Dementia:

AD is the prototypical cortical dementia, characterized by amnesia (hippocampal involvement), aphasia, agnosia, and apraxia (the "4 A's"). Subcortical dementias (e.g., Parkinson's disease dementia, Huntington's disease) are characterized by psychomotor slowing, executive dysfunction, personality changes, and movement disorders, with relatively preserved language and recognition memory. Subcortical dementias show retrieval deficits (poor free recall but improved with cues), while cortical dementias show encoding/storage deficits (poor free recall AND cued recall). This distinction is heavily tested on the EPPP.

Vascular Neurocognitive Disorder

Vascular NCD (previously called vascular dementia or multi-infarct dementia) is the second most common cause of dementia, resulting from the cerebrovascular disease mechanisms detailed in Chapter 11 (stroke types and MCA syndromes). Unlike Alzheimer's, vascular NCD classically has a stepwise deterioration rather than gradual decline, with each "step" corresponding to a new vascular event.

  • Multi-infarct dementia: Multiple large-vessel strokes producing cumulative cognitive damage. The cognitive profile depends on stroke locations.
  • Small vessel disease (subcortical vascular dementia): Chronic, progressive white matter ischemia (leukoaraiosis) producing executive dysfunction, psychomotor slowing, and gait abnormality. More gradual than multi-infarct type.
  • Strategic infarct dementia: A single stroke in a critical area (e.g., thalamus, angular gyrus, hippocampus) producing disproportionate cognitive impairment.
Key Distinguishing Features:

Vascular NCD is distinguished from AD by: (1) temporal relationship to cerebrovascular events (onset within 3 months of stroke), (2) stepwise rather than gradual decline, (3) early executive dysfunction and psychomotor slowing (often more prominent than memory impairment), (4) focal neurological signs (hemiparesis, gait abnormality, pseudobulbar affect), (5) evidence of cerebrovascular disease on neuroimaging, and (6) vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, atrial fibrillation). Many patients have mixed dementia (both AD and vascular pathology), which the EPPP may test.

Lewy Body Disease & Frontotemporal NCD

NCD with Lewy Bodies

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is the third most common cause of dementia. Lewy bodies are intracellular inclusions composed of alpha-synuclein protein, found diffusely throughout the cortex and brainstem.

Core Features of DLB:

The four core clinical features are: (1) Fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness (lucid intervals alternating with confused episodes), (2) Recurrent visual hallucinations that are typically well-formed and detailed (e.g., seeing people, children, or animals that are not there), (3) REM sleep behavior disorder (acting out dreams, which may precede cognitive symptoms by years), and (4) Spontaneous parkinsonism (rigidity, bradykinesia, but tremor is less prominent than in Parkinson's disease). A critically important clinical feature: patients with DLB have severe neuroleptic sensitivity -- conventional antipsychotics can cause life-threatening neuroleptic malignant syndrome or severe worsening. This is a high-yield EPPP fact.

The DSM-5 specifies distinct diagnostic criteria for assigning the label of probable versus possible NCD with Lewy bodies. For a diagnosis of probable major or mild NCD with Lewy bodies, the individual must demonstrate at least two of the four core features, or one core feature combined with at least one suggestive feature. If only one core feature is present without any suggestive features, a diagnosis of possible NCD with Lewy bodies is assigned. Critically, the DSM-5 classifies the four core features and suggestive features differently than some clinical research criteria. In the DSM-5 framework, fluctuating cognition, recurrent well-formed visual hallucinations, and spontaneous parkinsonism are designated as core clinical features, while REM sleep behavior disorder is categorized as a suggestive feature rather than a core feature. This classification is an important distinction for the EPPP, as exam questions may specifically test whether REM sleep behavior disorder is a core or suggestive diagnostic indicator.

Feature Type (DSM-5) Features
Core Features Fluctuating cognition/attention, recurrent detailed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism
Suggestive Feature REM sleep behavior disorder
Probable Diagnosis Requires ≥2 core features, OR 1 core + 1 suggestive feature
Possible Diagnosis Requires 1 core feature only, without suggestive features

A clinically and diagnostically critical distinction exists between NCD with Lewy bodies (DLB) and NCD due to Parkinson's disease (PDD). Both conditions share the same underlying pathology — cortical and subcortical accumulation of alpha-synuclein-containing Lewy bodies — and their clinical features overlap substantially, including parkinsonism, cognitive decline, visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder. The primary basis for distinguishing them is the temporal sequence of motor and cognitive symptom onset.

In Parkinson's disease dementia, the hallmark motor symptoms of Parkinson's disease (resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability) are well established for at least one year before significant cognitive decline emerges. The patient has a known history as a movement disorder patient who subsequently develops dementia. In DLB, cognitive symptoms either appear first or emerge concurrently with motor features — there is no extended period of isolated parkinsonism preceding the cognitive deterioration.

The DSM-5 and clinical consensus guidelines operationalize this using the "one-year rule": if parkinsonism precedes cognitive decline by more than one year, the diagnosis is NCD due to Parkinson's disease; if cognitive impairment develops before or within one year of motor symptom onset, the diagnosis is NCD with Lewy bodies.

Clinical Note

On the EPPP, when a vignette describes a patient who first presented with well-established motor parkinsonism and later developed dementia, think Parkinson's disease dementia. When cognitive symptoms and motor features emerge around the same time — or cognition declines first — the correct answer is NCD with Lewy bodies. Both fall under the broader category of alpha-synucleinopathies.

Frontotemporal NCD (Frontotemporal Dementia, FTD)

Frontotemporal NCD encompasses a group of disorders characterized by progressive degeneration of the frontal and/or temporal lobes. FTD typically has a younger onset than AD (often ages 45-65) and accounts for a significant proportion of early-onset dementias.

VariantPrimary LobeCharacteristic Features

FTD vs. Alzheimer's on the EPPP:

The EPPP frequently tests the distinction between FTD and AD. Key differentiators: FTD has earlier onset (often before 65), personality/behavioral changes precede memory loss, memory and visuospatial skills are relatively preserved early on, and social cognition is disproportionately impaired. AD has later onset, memory impairment is the earliest and most prominent symptom, and personality is relatively preserved until later stages. Approximately 40% of FTD cases have a genetic component, with mutations in MAPT (tau), GRN (progranulin), and C9orf72 genes.

When differentiating DLB from FTD on the EPPP, it is essential to recognize which symptoms belong to each disorder. Perseverative behavior — the repetitive, stereotyped continuation of a response or activity despite its no longer being appropriate — is a hallmark of frontotemporal NCD, particularly the behavioral variant. Patients with bvFTD frequently exhibit compulsive rituals, rigid routines, and stereotyped movements or speech patterns, reflecting the degeneration of frontal lobe circuits responsible for cognitive flexibility and behavioral regulation. This perseverative and ritualistic quality is considered a core diagnostic feature of bvFTD. In contrast, DLB is not characterized by perseveration; its defining cognitive feature is fluctuating attention and alertness rather than rigid, repetitive behavioral patterns. If an exam question describes a patient with progressive dementia accompanied by marked perseverative or compulsive-ritualistic behaviors, frontotemporal NCD should be strongly considered over Lewy body disease.

Delirium vs. Dementia

One of the most frequently tested EPPP differential diagnoses is delirium versus dementia (major NCD). Distinguishing these conditions is critical because delirium is a medical emergency that is often reversible when the underlying cause is treated.

Common Causes of Delirium (Mnemonic: I WATCH DEATH):

Infection, Withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), Acute metabolic (electrolytes, hepatic/renal failure), Trauma/pain, CNS pathology (stroke, seizure), Hypoxia, Deficiencies (thiamine/B12), Endocrine (thyroid, adrenal), Acute vascular (stroke, MI), Toxins/drugs (anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines), Heavy metals. Anticholinergic medications are among the most common causes of drug-induced delirium, especially in elderly patients. Pre-existing dementia is itself a major risk factor for developing delirium.

Clinical Note: Pseudodementia vs. True Neurocognitive Disorder

When evaluating an older adult with cognitive complaints, clinicians must differentiate pseudodementia (cognitive impairment secondary to major depressive disorder) from a true neurocognitive disorder. A key differentiating clue is the diurnal pattern of symptoms. In pseudodementia, cognitive difficulties tend to be relatively consistent or may worsen in the morning when depressive symptoms are typically most severe. In contrast, true neurocognitive disorders — particularly delirium but also many dementias — commonly feature sundowning, a worsening of confusion, agitation, and cognitive symptoms during late afternoon and evening hours. Therefore, if a family member reports that a patient's confusion and disorientation become notably worse as evening approaches, this temporal pattern supports a diagnosis of neurocognitive disorder rather than depression-related cognitive dysfunction. Additional features favoring pseudodementia include the patient's tendency to highlight and complain about cognitive failures (rather than minimizing or being unaware of them), a relatively rapid onset that correlates with a depressive episode, "don't know" answers rather than confabulation, and preserved effort on neuropsychological testing despite poor subjective complaints. A prior psychiatric history of mood disorders further increases suspicion for pseudodementia.

Other Neurocognitive Disorders

Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (CJD)

Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a rapidly progressive and fatal prion disease caused by misfolded prion proteins (PrPSc) that convert normal prion proteins to the pathogenic form. CJD produces rapidly progressive dementia (weeks to months, not years), myoclonus (sudden involuntary muscle jerks), and characteristic periodic sharp-wave complexes on EEG. The triad of rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, and periodic EEG changes is classic. Death typically occurs within one year of symptom onset. Variant CJD (vCJD) is linked to bovine spongiform encephalopathy ("mad cow disease") and tends to affect younger individuals.

Although CJD is renowned for its rapid trajectory, the initial presentation is often subtle and insidious. Prodromal symptoms may include fatigue, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and vague cognitive complaints that do not immediately suggest a neurodegenerative process. This insidious onset phase can last weeks to several months before the hallmark rapid cognitive deterioration becomes apparent. Once the disease transitions into its fulminant phase, decline is dramatic — progressing from mild forgetfulness and behavioral changes to profound global dementia, akinetic mutism, and death, typically within 12 months. This biphasic pattern — slow, nonspecific onset followed by precipitous decline — distinguishes CJD from both the gradual linear progression of Alzheimer's and the stepwise deterioration of vascular NCD.

HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorder (HAND)

HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder exists on a spectrum from asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment (ANI) to mild neurocognitive disorder (MND) to HIV-associated dementia (HAD). HIV preferentially targets subcortical structures (basal ganglia, white matter), producing a subcortical dementia profile: psychomotor slowing, executive dysfunction, and retrieval-based memory deficits. With effective antiretroviral therapy (ART), the incidence of HAD has dramatically decreased, though milder forms of HAND remain common.

Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)

Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus:

Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) is one of the few potentially reversible causes of dementia and is characterized by the classic triad of: (1) Gait disturbance (magnetic gait -- wide-based, shuffling, as if feet are stuck to the floor; typically the first and most prominent symptom), (2) Urinary incontinence, and (3) Dementia (subcortical pattern with executive dysfunction and psychomotor slowing). The mnemonic is "wet, wobbly, and wacky." Neuroimaging shows ventriculomegaly (enlarged ventricles) disproportionate to cortical atrophy. Diagnosis is confirmed by improvement after a large-volume lumbar puncture (CSF tap test). Treatment is ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt placement. The EPPP emphasizes NPH as a reversible dementia that must not be missed.

Substance/Medication-Induced NCD

Chronic alcohol use can produce Korsakoff syndrome (thiamine/vitamin B1 deficiency leading to severe anterograde amnesia, confabulation, and apathy, with mammillary body and thalamic damage). The acute, reversible precursor is Wernicke encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) -- this is a medical emergency requiring immediate IV thiamine. Chronic inhalant abuse, chronic sedative use, and other substance use disorders can also produce persistent neurocognitive impairment.
