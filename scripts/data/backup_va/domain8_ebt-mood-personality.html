Fundamental Content

CBT for Depression

A 34-year-old teacher tells her therapist that she has stopped grading papers, avoids her colleagues, and spends most evenings lying on the couch staring at the ceiling. When asked what goes through her mind, she says, "What is the point? I am terrible at my job, nobody really likes me, and nothing is ever going to change." In three sentences, she has articulated the three corners of Aaron Beck's cognitive triad — the theoretical engine that drives his cognitive theory of depression. Beck proposed that depression is maintained by negative cognitive schemas, activated by stressful life events, that generate systematic distortions in how a person thinks about the self ("I'm worthless"), the world ("Everything is hopeless"), and the future ("Things will never get better"). These distorted cognitions fuel withdrawal and inactivity, which in turn reduce positive reinforcement, deepening the depressive cycle.

Core CBT Techniques for Depression

  • Identifying automatic thoughts: Catching the spontaneous, evaluative thoughts that arise in response to situations and are often accepted as true without examination
  • Cognitive restructuring: Examining evidence for and against automatic thoughts, generating alternative interpretations, and testing distorted cognitions through behavioral experiments
  • Behavioral activation: Scheduling and engaging in pleasurable and mastery activities to counteract withdrawal, inactivity, and loss of reinforcement
  • Identifying cognitive distortions: Recognizing systematic thinking errors such as all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, mental filtering, catastrophizing, personalization, and "should" statements
Beck's Cognitive Distortions:

Common distortions include: All-or-nothing thinking (seeing things in black-and-white categories), overgeneralization (viewing a single negative event as a never-ending pattern), mental filter (dwelling on negatives while ignoring positives), magnification/minimization (exaggerating negatives and shrinking positives), emotional reasoning ("I feel it, therefore it must be true"), and personalization (assuming responsibility for negative events outside one's control).

Structure of Beck's CBT for Depression

Beck's CBT is a structured, time-limited (typically 12-20 sessions) treatment that follows a predictable session format: mood check, bridge from previous session, agenda setting, review of homework, discussion of agenda items, assignment of new homework, and summary/feedback. The treatment progresses through three phases:

  • Early sessions (1-4): Establishing the therapeutic relationship, psychoeducation about the cognitive model, introducing activity monitoring and scheduling, identifying automatic thoughts
  • Middle sessions (5-12): Cognitive restructuring using thought records, behavioral experiments to test predictions, identifying and challenging intermediate beliefs (rules, attitudes, assumptions)
  • Late sessions (13-20): Identifying and modifying core beliefs (schemas), relapse prevention, spacing sessions, consolidating gains
Levels of Cognition in CBT:

Beck distinguished three levels of cognition: (1) Automatic thoughts — spontaneous, situation-specific thoughts that are closest to conscious awareness; (2) Intermediate beliefs — conditional rules and attitudes ("If I'm not perfect, I'm a failure"); and (3) Core beliefs/schemas — deep, unconditional beliefs about self, others, and the world ("I am worthless"). CBT for depression addresses all three levels, progressing from automatic thoughts early in treatment to deeper schema-level work in later sessions.

Pharmacological Considerations for Depression

The EPPP expects knowledge of how psychotherapy compares with and combines with pharmacotherapy for depression. Key findings include:

  • For mild to moderate depression, CBT and pharmacotherapy are approximately equally effective. Psychotherapy alone is a reasonable first-line treatment.
  • For severe depression, combined CBT + medication tends to outperform either alone. BA may be as effective as medication for severe depression.
  • Long-term outcomes: CBT has an enduring effect that persists after treatment ends, whereas pharmacotherapy benefits typically disappear when medication is discontinued.
  • Relapse prevention: Both MBCT and maintenance antidepressant medication reduce relapse; MBCT may be equivalent to maintenance medication for patients with 3+ prior episodes.

Behavioral Activation (BA)

Behavioral Activation (Jacobson et al., 1996) emerged from a component analysis (known as the "component analysis study") showing that the behavioral component of CBT was as effective as the full cognitive-behavioral package for depression. This finding was replicated in the landmark NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program and the Dimidjian et al. (2006) trial. BA is based on the theory that depression results from a decrease in response-contingent positive reinforcement — when life circumstances reduce access to reinforcing activities, withdrawal and inactivity follow, which further decreases reinforcement in a self-perpetuating cycle.

Treatment focuses on: (1) monitoring daily activities and mood to identify the activity-mood connection, (2) scheduling and engaging in valued activities (even when motivation is low), (3) reducing avoidance behaviors and withdrawal, (4) addressing rumination as a form of avoidance (TRAP: Trigger-Response-Avoidance Pattern; TRAC: Trigger-Response-Alternative Coping), and (5) problem-solving environmental barriers to activation. BA is simpler to deliver than full CBT and has been shown to be equally effective, even for severe depression.

Activity Scheduling Example:

A depressed client who has stopped exercising, socializing, and pursuing hobbies begins BA by tracking daily activities and rating each for pleasure (0-10) and mastery (0-10). The therapist and client then identify a graded hierarchy of activities to re-engage with, starting with low-effort, high-reward behaviors (e.g., a brief walk) and gradually building toward more demanding activities (e.g., attending a social event). The key principle is that action precedes motivation — the client acts first, and mood improvement follows.

Dimidjian, S., Hollon, S. D., Dobson, K. S., et al. (2006). Randomized trial of behavioral activation, cognitive therapy, and antidepressant medication in the acute treatment of adults with major depression. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 74(4), 658-670.

Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

Having explored how CBT targets the thoughts and behaviors that fuel depression, we now turn to an approach that shifts the lens entirely — from what happens inside the client's mind to what happens between the client and the people in their life. Consider this scenario: A 42-year-old woman presents with a major depressive episode that began shortly after her husband took a demanding new job, leaving her feeling neglected and resentful. She doesn't endorse obvious cognitive distortions; she isn't catastrophizing or overgeneralizing. What she does describe, session after session, is a marriage in which she and her husband cannot agree on how to divide household responsibilities, how to spend weekends, or even how to talk about their frustrations. Her depression lives not in her automatic thoughts but in the space between herself and the person who matters most to her. This is exactly the kind of clinical picture that Interpersonal Therapy was designed to address.

Developed by Klerman, Weissman, and colleagues (1984), IPT is a time-limited treatment — typically 12 to 16 sessions — built on a deceptively simple premise: depression does not occur in a relational vacuum. Even when biological vulnerability is present, depressive episodes are frequently triggered, maintained, and worsened by disruptions in a person's most important relationships. Rather than restructuring cognitions or assigning behavioral homework, IPT zeroes in on the interpersonal context of depression, helping clients understand and resolve the relational problems that are intertwined with their mood. The treatment organizes the full range of interpersonal difficulties into four core problem areas, each with its own therapeutic focus:

Problem AreaDescriptionTreatment Focus
GriefComplicated bereavement or unresolved lossFacilitating mourning, reconnecting with social supports
Role DisputesConflicts with significant other about expectationsIdentifying the dispute, clarifying expectations, negotiation
Role TransitionsLife changes (retirement, divorce, new parenthood)Mourning the old role, embracing the new role, developing supports
Interpersonal DeficitsChronic social isolation or poor social skillsBuilding social skills, expanding social network

Notice how these four areas capture qualitatively different interpersonal challenges. A recently widowed retiree grieving the loss of a spouse presents a very different clinical picture than a new mother struggling to renegotiate her identity and marriage after the birth of her first child (a role transition), or a couple locked in bitter disagreement about whose career takes priority (a role dispute). The fourth category — interpersonal deficits — applies to clients whose depression is embedded in a longstanding pattern of social isolation, limited social skills, or an impoverished relational network. By identifying which problem area is most closely linked to the current depressive episode, the IPT therapist focuses treatment where it will have the greatest impact.

It is important to understand what IPT deliberately does not do. IPT does NOT directly address cognitive distortions or use homework assignments in the CBT sense. Instead, it works on the interpersonal context that triggers and maintains depression. The therapist uses two signature techniques — communication analysis, which involves exploring a recent interpersonal interaction in granular, moment-by-moment detail to identify where communication broke down, and role-playing, in which the therapist and client rehearse more effective ways of handling difficult conversations. A key early task in IPT is the interpersonal inventory — a detailed review of the client's significant relationships, including expectations, satisfactions, and disappointments. Think of this inventory as a relational map: who matters in this person's life, what do they need from those people, and where are the gaps between what they need and what they are getting?

IPT proceeds in three clearly defined phases: (1) the initial phase (sessions 1–3), which involves assessment, formal diagnosis, completion of the interpersonal inventory, assignment of the "sick role" (described below), and identification of the primary problem area that will anchor treatment; (2) the middle phase (sessions 4–13), during which the therapist and client engage in focused, active work on the identified problem area using IPT-specific strategies such as communication analysis, role-playing, and exploration of affect; and (3) the termination phase (sessions 14–16), which involves reviewing progress, consolidating gains, anticipating future challenges, and addressing the feelings that arise around ending treatment itself. This structured, phase-based format gives IPT both its efficiency and its clarity of purpose — therapist and client always know where they are in the process and what they are working toward.

The evidence base for IPT is robust and extends well beyond its original application to major depressive disorder (MDD). IPT has demonstrated efficacy for bulimia nervosa — where interpersonal difficulties often drive binge-eating cycles — as well as for postpartum depression, where the role transition to parenthood is a natural fit for the IPT framework. It is also used as an adjunct to medication in the treatment of bipolar disorder. In practice, remember that IPT is one of the best-supported treatments for depression, but its mechanism of change is fundamentally interpersonal rather than cognitive — a distinction that exam questions frequently test.

The Sick Role in IPT:

Early in treatment, the IPT therapist formally assigns the "sick role" — acknowledging that the client has a legitimate illness (depression) that is not their fault. This concept, borrowed from medical sociology, serves several important therapeutic functions. First, it reduces the guilt and self-blame that so often accompany depression ("I should just snap out of it"). Second, it normalizes the client's difficulties by framing depression as a medical condition rather than a character flaw. Third — and perhaps most importantly — it mobilizes the client to work actively toward recovery, just as they would if diagnosed with diabetes or a broken leg. You would not blame yourself for having pneumonia, the IPT therapist communicates, and you should not blame yourself for having depression. But just as with pneumonia, you do have a responsibility to engage in treatment. This reframing is one of IPT's most distinctive features and often produces visible relief in clients who have been silently judging themselves for being unable to "just feel better."

Klerman, G. L., Weissman, M. M., Rounsaville, B. J., & Chevron, E. S. (1984). Interpersonal psychotherapy of depression. Basic Books.

MBCT and Relapse Prevention for Depression

Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) (Segal, Williams, & Teasdale, 2002) integrates mindfulness meditation practices with elements of CBT specifically for the prevention of depressive relapse in individuals who have recovered from recurrent depression. The theoretical model proposes that after multiple depressive episodes, the association between low mood and negative thinking becomes increasingly automatic — a phenomenon called cognitive reactivity. Eventually, even minor mood fluctuations can trigger a cascade of ruminative negative thoughts that precipitate a full depressive relapse.

MBCT teaches clients to observe their thoughts and feelings with non-judgmental awareness — a skill called decentering or metacognitive awareness — rather than becoming caught up in ruminative cognitive patterns that trigger relapse. The 8-session group program (typically 8-12 participants) includes formal mindfulness practices (body scan, sitting meditation, mindful movement, 3-minute breathing space) along with cognitive therapy elements such as psychoeducation about depression and identifying early warning signs of relapse.

MBCT is recommended for individuals with three or more previous episodes of depression, where it reduces relapse rates by approximately 50% compared to treatment as usual. Notably, MBCT appears most effective for those whose prior episodes were NOT preceded by major adverse life events — suggesting it targets the autonomous, internally triggered relapse process rather than depression precipitated by external stressors. NICE guidelines recommend MBCT as a first-line relapse prevention intervention.

Key EPPP Distinction:

MBCT is primarily a relapse prevention intervention for recovered patients with recurrent depression, not a treatment for current acute depressive episodes. This distinguishes it from CBT (treats acute depression) and from MBSR (Kabat-Zinn's Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, which targets stress and chronic pain in general medical populations). MBCT is most effective for individuals with three or more prior episodes.

MBCT vs. MBSR:

MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction; Kabat-Zinn, 1990) is a general mindfulness program originally developed for chronic pain and stress management. It teaches mindfulness through body scan, sitting meditation, and gentle yoga over 8 weekly sessions plus a day-long retreat. MBCT adapts MBSR's mindfulness practices but adds CBT-specific content (psychoeducation about depression, identifying early warning signs, developing action plans for impending relapse). MBCT is diagnosis-specific (depression relapse prevention); MBSR is transdiagnostic.

Segal, Z. V., Williams, J. M. G., & Teasdale, J. D. (2002). Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for depression: A new approach to preventing relapse. Guilford Press.

DBT for Borderline Personality Disorder

Having explored mindfulness-based approaches to depression relapse prevention, we now turn to a treatment that fundamentally changed how clinicians approach one of the most challenging presentations in mental health. Imagine a client who arrives at your office after her third psychiatric hospitalization this year. She describes intense, rapidly shifting emotions that feel unbearable, a pattern of stormy relationships that cycle between idealization and rage, and a history of self-harm she uses as the only strategy she knows to regulate overwhelming emotional pain. For decades, clinicians felt ill-equipped to help individuals like her — many even avoided treating borderline personality disorder altogether. Then Marsha Linehan developed a treatment specifically designed for this population, and it transformed the landscape of evidence-based practice for personality disorders.

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) (Linehan, 1993) was developed specifically for chronically suicidal individuals with borderline personality disorder (BPD), a population that many clinicians had considered "untreatable." At its theoretical core, DBT rests on the biosocial theory, which explains BPD not as a character flaw or purely learned behavior, but as the product of a transaction between biology and environment. On the biological side, certain individuals are born with a vulnerability to emotional dysregulation — they experience emotions with greater sensitivity, react with greater intensity, and take longer to return to their emotional baseline. On the environmental side, these individuals grow up in what Linehan calls an invalidating environment — a developmental context that pervasively dismisses, punishes, or responds erratically to the child's expression of private emotional experiences. Think of a child who cries and is told "Stop being so dramatic" or "You have nothing to cry about." Over time, the transaction between this biological sensitivity and environmental invalidation produces the hallmark features of BPD: emotional dysregulation, interpersonal chaos, behavioral impulsivity, identity disturbance, and cognitive dysregulation. Understanding this biosocial model is essential because it drives every component of DBT — the treatment simultaneously validates the person's pain (acceptance) while teaching them new ways to manage it (change).

DBT Skills Modules

DBT organizes its skill-building component into four distinct modules, each targeting a specific domain of dysfunction. These modules form the backbone of skills training group and are woven throughout individual therapy as well. Mastering these four areas equips clients with a comprehensive toolkit for managing the emotional, interpersonal, and behavioral challenges that characterize BPD.

  • Mindfulness: Core skill — observing, describing, and participating in experiences with non-judgmental awareness (the "what" skills) and doing so one-mindfully and effectively (the "how" skills)
  • Distress Tolerance: Crisis survival strategies (TIPP, ACCEPTS, IMPROVE) and radical acceptance — tolerating painful emotions without making them worse
  • Emotion Regulation: Understanding and naming emotions, reducing vulnerability to negative emotions (PLEASE skills: treat Physical illness, balanced Eating, avoid mood-Altering substances, balanced Sleep, get Exercise), increasing positive emotional events, and opposite action (acting opposite to the emotion's action urge when the emotion does not fit the facts)
  • Interpersonal Effectiveness: DEAR MAN (Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate) for asking for what one needs; GIVE (be Gentle, act Interested, Validate, use Easy manner) for maintaining relationships; FAST (be Fair, no Apologies, Stick to values, be Truthful) for maintaining self-respect

Notice how these modules address different facets of the same underlying problem. A client who experiences an intense emotional reaction (emotion regulation deficit) may lash out at a partner (interpersonal effectiveness deficit), engage in self-harm to escape the pain (distress tolerance deficit), and do all of this while operating on autopilot without awareness of what triggered the cascade (mindfulness deficit). By building skills across all four domains, DBT creates multiple intervention points where a client can interrupt maladaptive patterns. For example, a client who learns PLEASE skills may sleep and eat more consistently, reducing her baseline emotional vulnerability. When a conflict still arises, she can use mindfulness to notice her emotional reaction early, apply opposite action if her anger does not fit the facts, use DEAR MAN to assert her needs effectively, and — if the situation becomes truly overwhelming — employ distress tolerance strategies like TIPP (Temperature change, Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Progressive muscle relaxation) to survive the crisis without self-harm.

Standard DBT delivers these skills and applies them to clients' lives through four modes of treatment that operate simultaneously:

ModeFrequencyPurpose
Individual therapyWeekly (1 hour)Addresses treatment targets using hierarchy: life-threatening behaviors > therapy-interfering behaviors > quality-of-life-interfering behaviors > skills acquisition
Skills training groupWeekly (2.5 hours)Teaches the four skills modules; structured psychoeducational format; NOT process-oriented group therapy
Phone coachingAs neededHelps generalize skills to everyday life; brief coaching during crises; NOT therapy between sessions
Therapist consultation teamWeeklySupports therapist motivation and competence; prevents burnout; "therapy for the therapist"

The dialectical philosophy stands at the very heart of DBT — it drives the therapist's moment-to-moment clinical decisions by balancing acceptance strategies (validation, mindfulness) with change strategies (behavior analysis, skills training, contingency management) throughout treatment. The fundamental dialectic asks the therapist to hold two seemingly contradictory truths at once: the client is doing the best she can, AND she needs to do better. Picture a session where a client discloses that she cut herself the previous night. The DBT therapist validates the unbearable pain that led to the self-harm (acceptance) while simultaneously conducting a detailed behavioral analysis to identify where the client could have used a skill instead (change). Neither acceptance alone nor change alone suffices — it is the synthesis of both that creates movement.

Two tools make this dialectical work concrete at the session level. Diary cards track daily emotions, urges (e.g., self-harm, substance use), target behaviors, and skills used. Clients complete these every day and bring them to the beginning of each individual session, where the therapist reviews them to set the agenda according to the treatment hierarchy. If the diary card reveals a self-harm episode, that takes priority over everything else — even if the client would rather discuss a fight with her mother. Chain analysis (also called behavioral analysis) then unpacks the problem behavior in minute detail. Together, the therapist and client trace the entire sequence: What was the prompting event? What vulnerability factors were present (poor sleep, skipped meals, an argument the day before)? What were each of the links in the chain — the thoughts, emotions, body sensations, and behaviors that cascaded from one to the next? What were the consequences? And critically, at which links in that chain could the client have intervened with a different skill? Chain analysis transforms a moment of crisis into a learning opportunity, building the client's capacity to interrupt similar chains in the future.

Linehan, M. M. (1993). Cognitive-behavioral treatment of borderline personality disorder. Guilford Press.

While standard DBT delivers treatment through four simultaneous modes — individual therapy, group skills training, phone coaching, and therapist consultation team — family or couples counseling is not a standard component of the treatment package. This distinction appears frequently on the EPPP and catches many test-takers off guard. Although adaptations of DBT exist that incorporate family members (e.g., DBT for adolescents adds a family skills training module, and some programs include multifamily skills groups), the standard adult DBT model developed by Linehan does not include a family or couples therapy mode. When a question asks you to identify which element is least likely part of a DBT treatment plan for an adult with BPD, family or couples counseling is the correct answer. The four required modes function together as an integrated system, each serving a distinct purpose: individual therapy addresses motivation and applies skills to specific problems; group skills training builds capabilities across the four modules; phone coaching promotes real-world generalization of skills; and the consultation team supports the therapist's wellbeing and clinical judgment. Removing any single mode compromises the treatment's integrity — but none of these functions requires involving family members or partners in the standard protocol.

Clinical Note

On the EPPP, distractors for DBT questions often include plausible-sounding therapeutic activities. Remember that standard DBT for adults has exactly four modes. Family therapy, psychodynamic exploration, and process-oriented group therapy are not among them. The skills group in DBT is explicitly psychoeducational and structured—not a traditional process group—and family involvement is not a formal treatment mode. A helpful mnemonic: think "ISPC" — Individual therapy, Skills group, Phone coaching, Consultation team. If an answer choice doesn't fit one of these four, it's likely a distractor.

A central goal across all four modes of standard DBT is the generalization of skills from the treatment setting to the client's daily life. Consider the gap between knowing and doing: a client may learn about opposite action in skills group and even practice it through role-plays, but when her partner criticizes her at 11 PM and rage floods her body, that structured classroom knowledge can feel a world away. Phone coaching exists specifically to bridge this gap — when a client faces a distressing situation between sessions, she contacts her individual therapist for brief, focused guidance on which skill to apply and how. The call is not a therapy session; it is a coaching moment, much like a swimming coach shouting technique reminders from the poolside while the swimmer is in the water. The individual therapist also prioritizes generalization by reviewing diary cards to identify moments where skills could have been used but were not, and by reinforcing successful skill application with genuine enthusiasm. Additionally, the therapist works to repair ruptures in the therapeutic relationship as they arise, modeling effective interpersonal skills and ensuring that therapy-interfering behaviors — from either client or therapist — do not derail treatment. A client who stops returning calls, arrives late consistently, or dissociates during sessions presents therapy-interfering behavior that the DBT therapist addresses directly and non-judgmentally, using the same dialectical balance of validation and problem-solving. This dual emphasis — extending skills beyond the therapy room and maintaining a functional therapeutic alliance — reflects DBT's pragmatic orientation toward building what Linehan describes as "a life experienced as worth living."

Mentalization-Based Therapy (MBT)

While DBT targets emotional dysregulation as the core problem in BPD, other evidence-based treatments conceptualize the disorder through a different lens. MBT (Bateman & Fonagy) focuses on improving the capacity to mentalize — to understand one's own and others' behavior in terms of underlying mental states such as thoughts, feelings, desires, and intentions. Think about what happens when you cut someone off in traffic without realizing it. A person with strong mentalizing capacity might think, "That driver is honking because I startled them — they're probably scared, not angry at me personally." A person whose mentalizing capacity has broken down under stress might instead think, "They're trying to attack me," or simply react with rage without reflecting on mental states at all. MBT conceptualizes BPD as fundamentally a disorder of mentalization: under emotional stress, this reflective capacity collapses, leaving the individual vulnerable to impulsive action, emotional dysregulation, and interpersonal chaos. Rather than teaching discrete behavioral skills, MBT uses the therapeutic relationship itself as the primary vehicle for developing mentalizing capacity. The therapist deliberately adopts a "not-knowing" stance, approaching the client's experience with genuine curiosity and collaborative inquiry rather than authoritative interpretation. Instead of saying "You're projecting your anger onto me," an MBT therapist might say, "I notice something shifted just now — I'm curious what was going through your mind when I said that." This stance models mentalization in real time and invites the client to practice it within the safety of the therapeutic relationship.

Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (TFP)

If MBT asks "What is this person thinking and feeling?", TFP (Kernberg, Yeomans, & Clarkin) asks "How does this person organize their internal world of self and other?" This psychodynamic treatment uses the analysis of transference patterns to restructure pathological object relations — the internalized representations of self and others that shape how a person experiences relationships. TFP assumes that BPD results from the predominance of splitting and other primitive defenses that prevent the integration of positive and negative representations of self and others. For instance, a client may idealize her therapist one week ("You're the only person who truly understands me") and devalue him the next ("You don't care about me at all — you're just like everyone else"). TFP understands these shifts not as manipulation but as the activation of split internal representations — an all-good self-other pair alternating with an all-bad self-other pair. Through systematic analysis of how these split representations manifest in the therapeutic relationship, TFP aims to help the client integrate them into a more nuanced, stable sense of self and others. TFP typically requires twice-weekly individual sessions, reflecting the depth and intensity of the psychodynamic work involved.

Schema Therapy

Where TFP focuses on split object relations and DBT targets behavioral skills deficits, Schema Therapy (Young et al., 2003) zeroes in on deeply entrenched cognitive-emotional patterns that originate in childhood. The approach identifies and modifies early maladaptive schemas (EMS) — broad, pervasive themes regarding oneself and one's relationships that develop during childhood when core emotional needs go unmet and then elaborate and rigidify throughout life. For example, an Abandonment schema might form in a child whose caregivers were unpredictable, leading to an adult who panics at the first sign of distance in a relationship. For BPD specifically, Schema Therapy focuses on four maladaptive schema modes — moment-to-moment emotional states that reflect the activation of different schemas and coping responses: the Detached Protector (emotional numbness and withdrawal to avoid pain), the Punitive Parent (harsh self-criticism internalized from early caregivers), the Abandoned/Abused Child (the vulnerable core experiencing terror, grief, and helplessness), and the Angry Child (rage in response to unmet needs). Treatment aims to strengthen the Healthy Adult mode — the part of the person capable of meeting their own needs, setting limits, and nurturing the vulnerable child within. To accomplish this, Schema Therapy draws on an integrative toolkit: cognitive techniques to challenge schema-driven beliefs, experiential strategies such as empty chair work and imagery rescripting to process early memories emotionally, behavioral pattern-breaking assignments, and a strong relational stance called "limited reparenting" in which the therapist partially meets unmet childhood needs within appropriate therapeutic boundaries.

BPD Treatment Comparison:

All four BPD treatments (DBT, MBT, TFP, Schema Therapy) have RCT support, giving clinicians meaningful choices when tailoring treatment to a client's presentation. DBT has the largest evidence base and is the most widely available, making it the default recommendation in many clinical settings. The APA Practice Guideline for BPD notes that different treatments may be preferred based on the predominant features: DBT when self-harm and emotional dysregulation are primary; MBT when mentalization deficits are prominent; TFP when identity disturbance and splitting are central; and Schema Therapy when rigid patterns of avoidance and early maladaptive schemas dominate the presentation. For EPPP purposes, know that DBT is the treatment most frequently asked about, but be prepared to distinguish among all four approaches based on their theoretical foundations and primary mechanisms of change.

Evidence-Based Treatments for Eating Disorders

Evidence-Based Treatment for Nocturnal Enuresis

The EPPP frequently tests knowledge of the enuresis alarm — known by several names including the night alarm, moisture alarm, and bell-and-pad — as the treatment of choice for children diagnosed with enuresis, nocturnal only under DSM-5. For this diagnosis to be assigned, the child must be at least 5 years of age (or equivalent developmental level), and the bedwetting must not be exclusively attributable to a substance (e.g., a diuretic) or another medical condition.

Research consistently demonstrates that the enuresis alarm yields the best long-term outcomes among available treatments. The mechanism relies on classical conditioning: the alarm sounds when moisture is detected, repeatedly pairing bladder distension with arousal from sleep. Over time, the sensation of a full bladder alone becomes sufficient to wake the child or inhibit urination during sleep. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that approximately 65–75% of children achieve initial dryness with the alarm, and relapse rates are substantially lower than those associated with pharmacological interventions. When relapse does occur, a second course of alarm treatment (sometimes combined with overlearning — having the child drink extra fluids before bed after initial success) is typically effective.

Overlearning Procedure

After the child achieves 14 consecutive dry nights with the alarm, they are asked to drink a measured amount of extra fluid before bedtime while continuing alarm use. This strengthens the conditioned response and significantly reduces post-treatment relapse rates. The alarm is discontinued only after the child again achieves a criterion number of consecutive dry nights under these more challenging conditions.

Example

A 7-year-old boy is referred after wetting the bed 4–5 nights per week. A pediatric evaluation rules out urological abnormalities and diabetes. The psychologist recommends the moisture alarm, explaining to the family that treatment typically requires 8–12 weeks of nightly use. The parents are coached to help their son wake fully when the alarm sounds, change his pajamas, reset the alarm, and return to bed. After 10 weeks, the child achieves 14 consecutive dry nights. The clinician then introduces overlearning before removing the device to consolidate treatment gains.

Enuresis: The Moisture Alarm as First-Line Treatment

Although not an eating disorder, nocturnal enuresis is a condition clinically important within the context of evidence-based treatments for childhood disorders. When a child's bedwetting is not attributable to a general medical condition (e.g., urinary tract infection, diabetes), the moisture alarm (also called the bell-and-pad or urine alarm) is considered the most effective intervention with the strongest long-term outcomes. The device works on classical conditioning principles: a sensor detects moisture as the child begins to urinate, triggering an alarm that wakes the child. Over repeated pairings, bladder fullness becomes a conditioned stimulus that elicits waking before urination occurs.

Compared to pharmacological alternatives such as desmopressin (a synthetic antidiuretic hormone) or imipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant), the moisture alarm produces superior long-term outcomes. While desmopressin and imipramine can reduce bedwetting episodes during active use, relapse rates upon discontinuation are high — often exceeding 50%. The moisture alarm, by contrast, produces sustained dryness in approximately two-thirds of children who complete treatment, and these gains tend to persist after the alarm is removed. Treatment typically requires 8–12 weeks of consistent use before full conditioning is achieved.

Clinical Note

When parents seek treatment for a child's bedwetting, clinicians should first rule out medical causes and confirm that the child is at least 5 years old (the DSM-5 age threshold for an enuresis diagnosis). The moisture alarm requires significant family commitment — parents must help the child wake to the alarm initially — but it remains the gold standard because of its durable effects and absence of pharmacological side effects.

CBT-E for Bulimia and Binge Eating

CBT-Enhanced (CBT-E) (Fairburn, 2008) is the leading evidence-based treatment for bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder. The transdiagnostic model proposes that all eating disorders share a core psychopathology: the overevaluation of eating, shape, and weight and their control. The cognitive-behavioral model of bulimia nervosa identifies a self-perpetuating cycle: low self-esteem and overvaluation of shape/weight lead to extreme dietary restriction, which leads to binge eating (as restriction eventually breaks down), which triggers compensatory behaviors (purging) and intensifies feelings of failure and low self-esteem.

CBT-E has two forms: a focused form (20 sessions) that targets the core eating disorder psychopathology, and a broad form that additionally addresses maintaining processes including clinical perfectionism, core low self-esteem, mood intolerance, and interpersonal difficulties. Key treatment components include:

  • Self-monitoring: Real-time recording of eating, purging, and associated thoughts/feelings
  • Regular eating pattern: Establishing three planned meals and two to three planned snacks per day
  • Cognitive restructuring: Challenging overvaluation of shape/weight and dietary rules
  • Exposure to avoided foods: Gradually reintroducing forbidden foods
  • Relapse prevention: Developing a personalized maintenance plan
CBT-E Outcomes:

CBT-E produces remission rates of approximately 40-50% for bulimia nervosa and higher rates for binge eating disorder. It is superior to pharmacotherapy alone for bulimia nervosa, and gains are well-maintained at follow-up. The transdiagnostic framework allows CBT-E to be applied across eating disorder diagnoses, including atypical presentations.

FBT/Maudsley Approach for Adolescent Anorexia

Family-Based Treatment (FBT), also known as the Maudsley approach (Lock & Le Grange), is the first-line treatment for adolescent anorexia nervosa. FBT proceeds in three phases:

  • Phase 1 (Weight Restoration): Parents take full charge of refeeding their child. The therapist empowers the parents as the experts on feeding their child, while externalizing the eating disorder as separate from the adolescent. A family meal is conducted in session 2, during which the therapist coaches parents to encourage the adolescent to eat one more bite than they feel comfortable with.
  • Phase 2 (Return of Control): Once weight is largely restored and the eating disorder symptoms have decreased, control of eating is gradually returned to the adolescent in an age-appropriate manner.
  • Phase 3 (Adolescent Identity): Focus shifts to establishing a healthy adolescent identity, addressing general developmental issues, and termination of treatment.

FBT is agnostic about the cause of the eating disorder and explicitly exonerates parents from blame. This represents a significant departure from earlier family therapy approaches that implicated family dynamics in the development of anorexia. FBT typically involves 15-20 sessions over 6-12 months.

While FBT was originally developed for adolescent anorexia nervosa, adaptations have been made for adolescent bulimia nervosa (FBT-BN). A critical distinction between the two applications concerns the degree of parental control over eating. In FBT for anorexia, parents assume near-total authority over their child's food intake during Phase 1, reflecting the medical urgency of weight restoration and the ego-syntonic nature of anorexic restriction. In FBT for bulimia, however, the approach is notably more collaborative between parents and the adolescent. Because binge-purge behaviors are typically ego-dystonic — the adolescent recognizes them as problematic and often feels ashamed — the therapist enlists the adolescent as a more active partner from the outset. Parents still play a central role in establishing structured eating patterns and reducing opportunities for binge-purge episodes, but they do so in greater partnership with the teenager rather than through unilateral control. This collaborative stance acknowledges that bulimia involves secretive behaviors that cannot be managed solely through external parental oversight; the adolescent's own motivation and self-monitoring are essential components of recovery.

IPT for Bulimia:

IPT has been shown to be as effective as CBT for bulimia nervosa in the long term, though CBT produces faster symptom improvement. IPT for eating disorders focuses on the same four interpersonal problem areas (grief, role disputes, role transitions, interpersonal deficits) and does not directly target eating behaviors — yet eating symptoms improve as interpersonal functioning improves.

Treatment of Anorexia Nervosa in Adults

Unlike bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder, no single psychotherapy has demonstrated clear superiority for adult anorexia nervosa. Evidence supports CBT-E (adapted for underweight patients), the Maudsley Model of Anorexia Nervosa Treatment for Adults (MANTRA), and Specialist Supportive Clinical Management (SSCM). Medical monitoring and nutritional rehabilitation are essential across all treatment approaches. Inpatient or residential treatment may be necessary when BMI is dangerously low or medical complications are present. The mortality rate for anorexia nervosa is the highest of any psychiatric disorder.

Pharmacotherapy for Eating Disorders:

SSRIs (particularly fluoxetine at 60 mg) are FDA-approved for bulimia nervosa and can reduce binge/purge frequency. For binge eating disorder, lisdexamfetamine (a stimulant) is FDA-approved. Pharmacotherapy has limited efficacy for anorexia nervosa — no medication has been shown to produce weight gain in acutely underweight patients. Olanzapine (an atypical antipsychotic) has shown modest weight gain benefits in some studies. Pharmacotherapy is generally recommended as an adjunct to psychotherapy, not as a standalone treatment for eating disorders.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Beck's cognitive triad: Negative views of self, world, and future. Core of cognitive theory of depression.
  • Behavioral Activation: Jacobson. As effective as full CBT for depression. Based on increasing response-contingent positive reinforcement.
  • IPT problem areas: Grief, role disputes, role transitions, interpersonal deficits. Time-limited (12-16 sessions). Does NOT use cognitive restructuring.
  • MBCT: Relapse prevention for recurrent depression (3+ episodes). NOT for acute depression. Reduces relapse by approximately 50%.
  • DBT: Linehan. Biosocial theory. Four modes (individual, group skills, phone coaching, consultation team). Four skills modules (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness).
  • DBT dialectics: Balance of acceptance and change strategies. Radical acceptance is a key distress tolerance skill.
  • MBT: Bateman & Fonagy. Focuses on mentalization capacity. For BPD.
  • TFP: Kernberg. Object relations approach. Analyzes transference patterns in BPD.
  • CBT-E: Fairburn. Transdiagnostic model. First-line for bulimia and binge eating disorder.
  • FBT/Maudsley: First-line for adolescent anorexia. Parents direct refeeding in Phase 1. Three-phase model.
  • Cognitive distortions: Know all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, mental filter, magnification/minimization, emotional reasoning, personalization, catastrophizing, "should" statements.
  • Schema Therapy: Young. Early maladaptive schemas (EMS). Four schema modes for BPD (Detached Protector, Punitive Parent, Abandoned Child, Angry Child). Strengthen Healthy Adult mode.
  • IPT sick role: Legitimizes depression as an illness, reduces guilt, mobilizes the client for treatment. Unique IPT feature.
  • IPT interpersonal inventory: Detailed review of all significant relationships conducted early in treatment to identify the primary problem area.
  • BA TRAP/TRAC: TRAP = Trigger-Response-Avoidance Pattern. TRAC = Trigger-Response-Alternative Coping. Framework for identifying and breaking avoidance cycles.
  • DBT diary cards: Daily tracking of emotions, urges, behaviors, and skills use. Reviewed at beginning of each individual session.
  • DBT treatment hierarchy: Life-threatening behaviors first, then therapy-interfering behaviors, then quality-of-life-interfering behaviors, then skills acquisition.
  • MBCT mechanism: Teaches decentering — the ability to observe thoughts as mental events rather than reality. This prevents the "depressive interlock" where negative mood activates negative thinking patterns that trigger relapse.
  • Levels of cognition: Automatic thoughts (surface), intermediate beliefs (rules/attitudes), core beliefs/schemas (deep). CBT progresses from surface to deep cognitions.
  • CBT session structure: Mood check, bridge, agenda setting, homework review, agenda items, new homework, summary/feedback. Collaborative empiricism is the working stance.
  • CBT vs. medication for depression: Approximately equal for mild-moderate depression. Combined treatment may be best for severe depression. CBT has enduring effects after treatment ends; medication effects typically disappear when discontinued.
  • Fluoxetine for bulimia: FDA-approved at 60 mg (higher than the typical antidepressant dose). SSRIs reduce binge/purge frequency.
  • Lisdexamfetamine: FDA-approved for binge eating disorder. The only stimulant approved for an eating disorder.
  • No effective pharmacotherapy for anorexia: No medication produces weight gain in acutely underweight AN patients. Nutritional rehabilitation is essential.
  • Anorexia mortality: Highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder. Medical monitoring is essential.
  • Biosocial theory: Linehan's DBT model. Emotional vulnerability (biological) + invalidating environment (social) = BPD features.
  • Invalidating environment: Pervasively dismisses, punishes, or responds inconsistently to emotional expression. Central concept in DBT's developmental model of BPD.
  • Mentalization: The capacity to understand behavior as driven by mental states. When mentalization breaks down under stress, impulsive action replaces reflective thought.
  • Object relations in TFP: Kernberg. BPD involves predominance of splitting — inability to integrate positive and negative representations of self and others. TFP works to integrate split representations through transference analysis.
  • DBT chain analysis: Detailed behavioral analysis of problem behaviors — examining prompting event, vulnerability factors, chain links, and consequences. Used to identify intervention points.
  • MBSR vs. MBCT: MBSR = Kabat-Zinn, general stress/pain, transdiagnostic. MBCT = Segal/Williams/Teasdale, depression relapse prevention, includes CBT-specific content.
  • Cognitive reactivity: After multiple depressive episodes, the association between low mood and negative thinking becomes automatic. MBCT targets this process.
  • Opposite action: DBT emotion regulation skill. When an emotion does not fit the facts of the situation, act opposite to the emotion's action urge (e.g., approach instead of avoid when afraid; be kind when unjustifiably angry).
  • IPT for postpartum depression: IPT has specific adaptations for postpartum depression, addressing the role transition to motherhood and interpersonal changes during the perinatal period.
  • CBT-E transdiagnostic model: Fairburn. All eating disorders share core psychopathology — overevaluation of shape, weight, and eating control. Focused (20 sessions) vs. broad form.
