Attachment Theory

Attachment—the deep emotional bond between an infant and a caregiver—is a key topics in practice within the developmental domain. The theoretical foundations laid by John Bowlby and the empirical classification system developed by Mary Ainsworth remain cornerstones of clinical and developmental psychology.

Bowlby's Ethological Attachment Theory

Bowlby (1969/1982) proposed that attachment is an evolved behavioral system designed to maintain proximity between infants and caregivers, thereby promoting survival. He described four phases of attachment development:

  1. Pre-attachment (birth–6 weeks): Indiscriminate social responsiveness. Infants produce signals (crying, grasping) that attract caregivers but do not yet show specific attachment.
  2. Attachment-in-the-making (6 weeks–6–8 months): Infants begin to show differential responding to familiar caregivers, smiling and vocalizing more with known figures.
  3. Clear-cut attachment (6–8 months–18 months): The infant displays separation anxiety and stranger anxiety, using the caregiver as a secure base for exploration.
  4. Reciprocal relationship (18 months–2+ years): With growing language and cognitive abilities, the child begins to understand the caregiver's goals and can negotiate separations.
Internal Working Model

Bowlby proposed that early attachment experiences create mental representations—internal working models—of the self and others that guide expectations about relationships throughout the lifespan. A securely attached child develops a model of the self as worthy of care and of others as reliable and responsive.

Ainsworth's Strange Situation

Mary Ainsworth developed the Strange Situation paradigm to assess the quality of infant-caregiver attachment in a laboratory setting. The procedure involves a series of separations and reunions between the infant (typically 12–18 months) and the caregiver, observed through a one-way mirror.

Exam Example

In the Strange Situation, a 14-month-old becomes very upset when her mother leaves the room. When the mother returns, the child reaches out to be picked up but then immediately pushes away and cries harder, alternating between clinging and angry resistance. This pattern is most consistent with insecure-resistant (ambivalent) attachment (Type C).

Clinical Application

Disorganized attachment has been identified as a significant risk factor for the development of dissociative symptoms, borderline personality features, and externalizing behavior problems in later childhood and adolescence. Attachment-based interventions such as Circle of Security and Child-Parent Psychotherapy target the caregiver-child relationship to promote security.

Bowlby, J. (1982). Attachment and loss: Vol. 1. Attachment (2nd ed.). Basic Books.

Ainsworth, M. D. S., Blehar, M. C., Waters, E., & Wall, S. (1978). Patterns of attachment. Erlbaum.

Temperament

Having explored attachment theory — how infants form emotional bonds with their caregivers and how the quality of those bonds shapes development — we now turn to a complementary question: What does the infant bring to the relationship? Any parent with more than one child will tell you that babies arrive with distinct personalities. One newborn sleeps through the night by six weeks, coos contentedly through diaper changes, and smiles at strangers in the grocery store. Another infant in the same family, raised by the same parents, wakes every two hours, screams during baths, and recoils from unfamiliar faces. These differences are not products of parenting style or attachment history alone — they reflect something intrinsic to the child from the very beginning.

Temperament refers to these individual differences in emotional reactivity and self-regulation that appear early in life, show moderate stability over time, and are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Think of temperament as the biological substrate of personality — the infant's characteristic style of responding to the world before experience has had much time to shape behavior. Understanding temperament is clinically essential because it helps explain why two children raised in similar environments can follow strikingly different developmental trajectories, and it reminds clinicians never to assume that a child's behavior is solely a reflection of parenting quality.

The most influential classification system for infant temperament was developed by Thomas and Chess (1977) as part of the New York Longitudinal Study (NYLS), a landmark project that followed 133 children from infancy into adulthood. By interviewing parents about their infants' behavior across a range of everyday situations — feeding, sleeping, bathing, encountering new people — Thomas and Chess identified nine dimensions of temperament (including activity level, rhythmicity, approach/withdrawal, adaptability, and intensity of reaction) and distilled these into three broad temperamental profiles.

Thomas and Chess's Three Temperament Types

Easy child (~40%): Positive mood, regular biological rhythms, adaptable, moderate intensity of reaction.
Difficult child (~10%): Negative mood, irregular rhythms, slow to adapt, intense reactions.
Slow-to-warm-up child (~15%): Initially negative, low activity level, slow adaptation, mildly intense reactions. The remaining ~35% showed mixed patterns.

Notice the percentages: only about 10% of infants fall into the "difficult" category, yet these children tend to generate outsized parental concern and clinical referrals. Meanwhile, the largest single group — the roughly 35% who showed mixed patterns and did not fit neatly into any category — reminds us that classification systems are useful heuristics, not rigid boxes. In clinical work, you will encounter many children whose temperamental profiles blend features from more than one type.

Perhaps the most clinically powerful concept to emerge from the NYLS was not the temperament types themselves but the idea Thomas and Chess called goodness of fit — the match between a child's temperament and the demands and expectations of the caregiving environment. This concept shifts the clinical lens away from "What's wrong with this child?" and toward "How well does this child's natural style mesh with the world around them?" A child with a difficult temperament — irregular rhythms, intense reactions, slow adaptability — may thrive in a patient, flexible household where parents can tolerate unpredictability and respond calmly to emotional outbursts. That same child placed in a rigid, punitive environment, where schedules are strictly enforced and intense emotions are met with harsh discipline, may develop significant behavioral problems. The temperament itself is not pathological; the mismatch is what creates risk.

This principle has profound implications for parent consultation. When a frustrated mother says, "I must be doing something wrong — my first child was so easy," a clinician grounded in temperament theory can normalize the experience, explain that the child's behavioral style has a strong constitutional component, and help the parent develop strategies that honor the child's temperament rather than fighting against it. Goodness of fit also has implications for classroom settings, peer relationships, and even therapeutic approaches — a slow-to-warm-up child, for example, may need a therapist who builds rapport gradually and avoids overwhelming the child with novelty too quickly.

Exam Example

A parent describes their 6-month-old as fussy, with unpredictable eating and sleeping schedules, and strong emotional reactions to new situations. Using Thomas and Chess's classification, this infant would be described as having a "difficult" temperament. A clinician would focus on goodness of fit between the child's temperament and parenting approach.

Kagan's Behavioral Inhibition

While Thomas and Chess mapped the broad landscape of temperament, Jerome Kagan zoomed in on one particular temperamental dimension with striking clinical relevance. Imagine two four-month-old infants presented with an unfamiliar mobile dangling above their cribs. One baby stares at it with curiosity, reaches out, and coos. The other baby arches her back, flails her limbs, and begins to cry. Kagan discovered that these early reactions — which he could reliably measure in the laboratory — were remarkably predictive of a child's social and emotional profile years later.

Kagan identified this temperamental dimension as behavioral inhibition — the tendency to react with wariness, physiological arousal, and withdrawal when confronted with novel or unfamiliar stimuli. Approximately 15–20% of infants are classified as behaviorally inhibited, and Kagan's longitudinal research demonstrated that these infants tend to remain cautious, quiet, and shy well into childhood and even adolescence. Physiologically, behaviorally inhibited children show a lower threshold for activation in the amygdala, producing heightened autonomic arousal — higher heart rates, elevated cortisol levels, and greater muscle tension — in response to novelty. Kagan's work is especially important in clinical practice because of its direct connection to psychopathology: behaviorally inhibited children show a heightened risk for developing anxiety disorders, particularly social anxiety disorder. However, this trajectory is not inevitable — warm, supportive parenting and gentle, gradual exposure to new situations can buffer inhibited children against developing clinical anxiety, another powerful illustration of the goodness-of-fit principle in action.

Thomas, A., & Chess, S. (1977). Temperament and development. Brunner/Mazel.

Kagan, J. (1994). Galen's prophecy: Temperament in human nature. Basic Books.

Attachment Across the Lifespan

The preceding sections explored how attachment bonds form in infancy and how temperament shapes the child's contribution to those bonds. But the story of attachment does not end in the nursery. One of Bowlby's most consequential insights was that the internal working models forged in early caregiving relationships function as templates for close relationships throughout life -- influencing romantic partnerships, friendships, parenting behavior, and even the therapeutic alliance. Understanding this continuity is essential for clinicians, because the adult sitting across from you in therapy is carrying forward patterns that were laid down decades earlier, in interactions they cannot consciously remember.

Bowlby proposed that internal working models operate largely outside conscious awareness, organizing expectations about whether others will be available and responsive, whether the self is worthy of care, and how to regulate distress in close relationships. A securely attached infant develops a working model of the self as lovable and of others as dependable -- expectations that translate into comfort with intimacy, effective emotion regulation, and the capacity to seek support when stressed. An insecurely attached infant, by contrast, may develop models that others are unreliable (avoidant), unpredictable (ambivalent/resistant), or frightening (disorganized), shaping adult relationship patterns in correspondingly troubled ways.

Adult Attachment: The AAI and Beyond

Mary Main and colleagues developed the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI), a semi-structured interview that classifies adults' current state of mind regarding attachment based on how they discuss their childhood attachment experiences. Critically, the AAI assesses narrative coherence -- how the person tells the story -- rather than the content of what happened. An adult who experienced difficult early relationships but can discuss them reflectively and coherently is classified as "earned secure," while one who had objectively positive early experiences but cannot discuss them coherently may be classified as insecure.

Research has consistently demonstrated intergenerational transmission of attachment: a parent's AAI classification predicts their infant's Strange Situation classification with approximately 75% accuracy. This means that how a mother or father processes their own attachment history shapes the caregiving behaviors that, in turn, shape their child's attachment security. This finding has profound clinical implications -- it suggests that one of the most effective ways to promote secure attachment in children is to help parents develop more coherent, reflective narratives about their own early experiences.

Clinical Application: Reactive Attachment Disorder

When early attachment is not merely insecure but severely disrupted, the consequences can be devastating. Reactive Attachment Disorder (RAD) is a DSM-5 diagnosis characterized by a consistent pattern of emotionally withdrawn behavior toward caregivers, with the child rarely or minimally seeking comfort when distressed and rarely or minimally responding to comfort when offered. RAD requires evidence of insufficient care (social neglect, repeated changes of primary caregivers, or rearing in institutional settings that limit attachment formation). Critically, RAD is distinguished from autism spectrum disorder by its social-emotional profile: children with RAD can form selective attachments when placed in adequate caregiving environments, while the social-communication deficits of ASD are not driven by caregiving history. The related diagnosis, Disinhibited Social Engagement Disorder (DSED), involves indiscriminate social approach behavior with unfamiliar adults, and unlike RAD, it can persist even after the child forms a selective attachment. Both conditions underscore that the quality of early caregiving is not merely important for emotional comfort -- it is a biological necessity for healthy social development.

Exam Example: Intergenerational Transmission

A psychologist administers the Adult Attachment Interview to a pregnant woman and classifies her as "dismissing." She describes her childhood as "perfectly normal" but cannot recall specific episodes and brushes aside questions about emotional closeness with "I don't really remember -- it was fine." After the birth, her infant is assessed in the Strange Situation at 14 months and classified as insecure-avoidant (Type A). This pattern of concordance between maternal AAI classification and infant attachment security illustrates the intergenerational transmission of attachment, likely mediated by the mother's tendency to minimize emotional cues and provide less sensitive, less responsive caregiving.

Main, M., Kaplan, N., & Cassidy, J. (1985). Security in infancy, childhood, and adulthood: A move to the level of representation. Monographs of the Society for Research in Child Development, 50(1–2), 66–104.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Ainsworth's four attachment types: Know the reunion behaviors, caregiver patterns, and relative prevalence of secure (B), avoidant (A), resistant/ambivalent (C), and disorganized (D) attachment. Disorganized carries the highest psychopathology risk.
  • Bowlby's phases: Pre-attachment, attachment-in-the-making, clear-cut attachment, and reciprocal relationship. Internal working models shape later relationship expectations across the lifespan.
  • Thomas and Chess: Easy (~40%), difficult (~10%), and slow-to-warm-up (~15%) temperaments. The key clinical concept is goodness of fit between temperament and environment.
  • Kagan's behavioral inhibition: A biologically based tendency toward wariness of novelty; associated with amygdala hyperreactivity and later social anxiety. Warm parenting can buffer this risk.
  • Goodness of fit: The match between a child's temperament and the demands of the caregiving environment. A difficult temperament is not pathological in itself; mismatch creates risk.
  • Internal working models: Mental representations of self and others formed through early attachment experiences. Operate largely outside conscious awareness and shape relationship patterns across the lifespan.
  • Adult Attachment Interview (AAI): Classifies adults by narrative coherence, not childhood events. Autonomous, Dismissing, Preoccupied, and Unresolved classifications parallel the four infant types.
  • Intergenerational transmission: Parental AAI classification predicts infant Strange Situation classification with ~75% accuracy.
  • Reactive Attachment Disorder: Requires evidence of pathogenic care (neglect, institutional rearing). Emotionally withdrawn pattern toward caregivers. Differentiate from ASD (RAD is caregiving-driven, not neurodevelopmental).
  • Disorganized attachment: The strongest predictor of later psychopathology, including dissociation and borderline features. Often associated with frightening or frightened caregiver behavior.
