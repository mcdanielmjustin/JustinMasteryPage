Overview of Standard 9: Assessment

A forensic psychologist is asked to evaluate a Spanish-speaking defendant's competency to stand trial. The only cognitive measure available in the assessment battery was normed exclusively on English-speaking populations, and the interpreter provided by the court has no training in psychological testing protocols. If the psychologist proceeds under these conditions, the resulting report could mischaracterize the defendant's cognitive abilities — with consequences ranging from wrongful commitment to an unjust trial outcome. Scenarios like this underscore why assessment ethics are not procedural formalities but clinical imperatives with real-world stakes.

Standard 9 of the APA Ethics Code governs the ethical conduct of psychological assessment, a key standards in practice because assessment is a core professional activity that directly affects clients' lives — from clinical diagnoses to forensic evaluations, educational placements, and employment decisions. The standard establishes requirements for the proper use, administration, interpretation, and security of psychological tests and assessment instruments, requiring that assessments be scientifically grounded, culturally appropriate, and conducted with appropriate informed consent.

Assessment vs. Testing

Psychological assessment is a comprehensive process that integrates information from multiple sources (tests, interviews, observations, records) to answer referral questions. Psychological testing refers specifically to the administration and scoring of standardized instruments. Assessment is the broader, clinical process; testing is one component of assessment.

Standard 9.01: Bases for Assessments

Psychologists base the opinions contained in their recommendations, reports, and diagnostic or evaluative statements, including forensic testimony, on information and techniques sufficient to substantiate their findings.

9.01(a) -- Sufficient Information

Psychologists provide opinions only after they have conducted an examination of the individual adequate to support their statements or conclusions. When a personal examination is not practical, psychologists document the efforts they made and the results of those efforts, clarify the probable impact of their limited information on the reliability and validity of their opinions, and appropriately limit the nature and extent of their conclusions or recommendations.

EPPP Tip

A frequent EPPP scenario: A psychologist is asked to provide a diagnosis or custody recommendation based solely on record review (without examining the individual). Standard 9.01 does not prohibit this, but requires the psychologist to clearly state the limitations and restrict conclusions accordingly. When a personal examination is not practical, the psychologist must document this and qualify their opinions.

9.01(b) -- Limitations When Personal Examination Is Not Possible

Except as noted in 9.01(c), psychologists provide opinions of the psychological characteristics of individuals only after they have conducted an examination adequate to support their statements. When, despite reasonable efforts, such an examination is not practical, psychologists document their efforts and the impact on conclusions.

9.01(c) -- Record Review Without Examination

When psychologists conduct a record review or provide consultation or supervision and an individual examination is not warranted or necessary, they explain this and the sources of information on which they based their conclusions and recommendations.

Standard 9.02: Use of Assessments

Psychologists administer, adapt, score, interpret, or use assessment techniques, interviews, tests, or instruments in a manner and for purposes that are appropriate in light of the research on or evidence of the usefulness and proper application of the techniques.

9.02(a) -- Appropriate Use

Psychologists use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for use with members of the population tested. When such validity or reliability has not been established, psychologists describe the strengths and limitations of test results and interpretation.

9.02(b) -- Outdated Norms and Cultural Factors

Psychologists use assessment instruments whose validity and reliability have been established for the specific purpose and population. When assessing individuals from populations not represented in the normative sample, psychologists must consider cultural, linguistic, and individual factors that may affect interpretation and clearly note these limitations in reports.

Example

A psychologist administers an intelligence test normed on English-speaking Americans to a recent immigrant whose first language is Spanish. The psychologist must note the cultural and linguistic limitations of the assessment, consider using instruments validated for Spanish speakers, and avoid drawing definitive conclusions about intellectual ability based on the potentially biased results.

Standard 9.03: Informed Consent in Assessments

Imagine you're a psychologist about to begin a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation with a new client. Before you open the first test booklet, there's a critical step that must occur — one that shapes the entire ethical foundation of the assessment process. The client needs to understand what's about to happen, why it's happening, who will see the results, and what the limits of privacy are. This is the essence of informed consent in assessments, and it's far more than a form to be signed. It represents the client's right to make a knowing, voluntary decision about participating in a process that could profoundly affect their life — from diagnostic labels to custody decisions to disability determinations.

Having just discussed the ethical use of assessments under Standard 9.02 — including the obligation to use instruments appropriate for the individual being tested — we now turn to what must happen before any assessment begins. Standard 9.03 establishes the requirement that psychologists obtain informed consent for assessments, evaluations, or diagnostic services. However, the Ethics Code recognizes that certain contexts make traditional informed consent impractical or unnecessary. Psychologists are not required to obtain informed consent in the following circumstances:

  1. Testing is mandated by law or governmental regulations
  2. Informed consent is implied because testing is conducted as a routine educational, institutional, or organizational activity (e.g., admission testing)
  3. The purpose of the testing is to evaluate decisional capacity

The third exception is particularly worth noting: if a psychologist has been asked to determine whether a person can make competent decisions — for example, an elderly patient whose family is concerned about dementia — requiring that person to provide truly informed consent before the evaluation would create a logical paradox. You'd be asking someone to demonstrate the very capacity you've been asked to assess. Similarly, when a court orders a forensic evaluation or when students routinely take placement tests at school, the traditional consent process is either legally superseded or contextually implied. Even so, as you'll see below, notification and explanation are never entirely waived.

9.03(a) -- Explaining the Purpose

When informed consent is required, it must be more than a vague assurance that "we'll be doing some testing today." Consider a parent bringing their child in for a psychoeducational evaluation. Ethical practice demands that the psychologist explain, in language the parent can readily understand, what types of tests will be administered, what they're designed to measure, how long the process will take, and what will happen with the results. Informed consent includes an explanation of the nature and purpose of the assessment, fees, involvement of third parties, and limits of confidentiality in a language reasonably understandable to the person being assessed or their legal representative. For instance, if the evaluation has been requested by the child's school district, the parent needs to know that a report will be shared with school personnel — this is the kind of third-party involvement that must be disclosed upfront. The key phrase here is "reasonably understandable": a psychologist working with a client who has limited education shouldn't rely on jargon-heavy consent forms but should instead use clear, plain language and check for genuine comprehension.

9.03(b) -- Individuals Lacking Capacity

Some of the most ethically delicate assessment situations arise when the person being evaluated cannot provide meaningful consent on their own — a young child, an adult with significant intellectual disability, or a patient with advanced Alzheimer's disease. In these cases, the psychologist's obligations don't simply vanish; they become layered. For persons who lack the capacity to consent (e.g., due to cognitive impairment), psychologists provide appropriate explanation at a level the person can understand, seek the person's assent even when formal consent isn't possible, consider the person's preferences and best interests, and obtain consent from a legally authorized person such as a guardian or parent. This standard reflects a deeply humanistic principle: even when someone cannot legally consent, their dignity and autonomy still matter. A psychologist evaluating a teenager with moderate intellectual disability should still explain, in simple terms, what the testing involves and why it's being done — and should pay attention to whether the teen seems willing or reluctant to participate. Obtaining a parent's signature on a consent form does not absolve the psychologist from engaging respectfully with the individual being assessed.

9.03(c) -- Court-Ordered or Mandated Assessment

Forensic and legal contexts create a unique tension in the consent process. When a judge orders a defendant to undergo a psychological evaluation, that individual cannot simply decline. Yet the absence of choice doesn't eliminate the psychologist's ethical responsibilities. When services are court ordered or otherwise mandated, psychologists inform the individual of the nature of the anticipated services, including whether the services are court ordered or mandated and any limits of confidentiality, before proceeding. Picture a forensic psychologist meeting with a parent involved in a custody dispute. Even though this parent must submit to the evaluation, the psychologist should clearly explain: "This evaluation was ordered by the court. I'll be assessing your parenting capacity and psychological functioning. My report will be submitted to the judge and will be available to both attorneys. I am not acting as your therapist, and what you tell me is not confidential in the way therapy would be." This kind of transparent notification protects the individual from the mistaken belief that they're in a therapeutic relationship and ensures they understand the evaluative — not supportive — nature of the interaction.

EPPP Tip

Court-ordered assessments still require notification -- the psychologist must tell the individual about the nature of services and limits of confidentiality, even though the person cannot refuse the assessment. Informed consent is modified but not eliminated in mandated evaluations. On the EPPP, watch for answer choices that suggest mandated evaluations require no communication with the examinee at all — that's incorrect. The obligation shifts from obtaining consent to providing clear, upfront notification. Think of it this way: the person loses the right to say no, but they never lose the right to know what's happening and why.

Standard 9.04: Release of Test Data

Having just explored informed consent in assessments — including the special considerations for court-ordered evaluations — we now turn to one of the most important and practically confusing areas of the APA Ethics Code: what happens after the assessment is complete and someone wants to see the results. Imagine you've just finished a comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation. The client's attorney calls requesting "everything from the testing." Do you send the scored protocols? The raw data sheets? The test booklet itself? Your clinical notes? The answer depends on a critical distinction that every practicing psychologist must master — and one the EPPP tests repeatedly.

The key lies in understanding what qualifies as test data versus what constitutes test materials. These two categories look similar on the surface, but the ethical obligations surrounding each are quite different. Test data refers to raw and scaled scores, client responses to test questions or stimuli, and psychologists' notes and recordings concerning client statements and behavior during an examination. Think of test data as everything that is uniquely generated by or about the client during the assessment process. Test materials, by contrast, are the instruments themselves — manuals, protocols, test questions or stimuli, and scoring keys. These belong to the test, not to the client, and their release is governed by separate ethical obligations related to test security (covered under Standard 9.11). When that attorney calls asking for "everything," you need to know which pile each document falls into.

9.04(a) -- Client Request

When a client (or their legal representative) signs a release authorizing the disclosure of test data, the general rule is straightforward: psychologists provide the requested test data to the client or to other persons identified in the release. This aligns with the broader ethical principle of respecting client autonomy — after all, the data were generated about them and in many ways belong to their clinical record. However, the standard wisely builds in an important exception. Psychologists may refrain from releasing test data to protect a client or others from substantial harm or misuse or misrepresentation of the data or the test, recognizing that in many instances release of confidential information under these circumstances is regulated by law.

Consider a clinical example: you've conducted a custody evaluation, and one parent demands the raw data, stating they intend to post the other parent's responses on social media. Here, releasing the data could cause substantial harm — both to the other parent and potentially to the integrity of the assessment process. In such a scenario, you could ethically withhold the data despite having a signed release. But notice the careful language of the standard: this is a "may" provision, not a "shall." It gives you discretion, not a mandate. And critically, you must remain aware that applicable laws — such as HIPAA, which generally treats test data as part of the medical record — may override your ethical judgment and require release regardless. This intersection of ethics and law is a favorite EPPP testing point.

9.04(b) -- Absent Client Release

What happens when there is no signed client release? Perhaps the client refuses to authorize disclosure, or perhaps the request comes from a third party with no release on file. In the absence of a client release, psychologists provide test data only as required by law or court order. This is a firm boundary: without legal compulsion, you do not release test data to anyone the client hasn't authorized. A requesting attorney, an insurance company, or even another psychologist has no right to the data without either client consent or a legal mandate. This provision underscores the fundamental principle that test data, because they contain intimate details of a client's psychological functioning, deserve robust confidentiality protections.

For example, if a forensic psychologist receives a subpoena (not a court order) for test data and the client has not signed a release, the psychologist should not automatically comply. A subpoena is issued by an attorney, not a judge, and does not carry the same legal force as a court order. The appropriate response is typically to notify the client, seek legal guidance, and — if necessary — assert the privilege on the client's behalf until a judge rules on the matter. Understanding the difference between a subpoena and a court order in the context of releasing test data is essential both for ethical practice and for EPPP success.

Test Data vs. Test Materials

Test data (9.04) = the client's responses, scores, and behavioral observations -- generally released to the client upon request. Test materials (9.11) = the test itself (questions, stimuli, manuals, scoring keys) -- protected from release to maintain test security. A helpful mnemonic: test data are about the person; test materials are about the test. If a document would exist regardless of which client sat down — such as a test manual or a blank answer sheet — it is a test material. If it was created because this particular client participated — such as their scores, their specific answers, or your behavioral observations — it is test data. This distinction is clinically important.

Standard 9.05: Test Construction

Having explored the critical distinction between test data and test materials — and the responsibilities psychologists carry when releasing assessment information — we now turn to where every assessment instrument begins: its construction. Before a test can generate data worth protecting, before scores can be interpreted or released, someone must build the test itself. And Standard 9.05 establishes that this process is far from casual — it demands rigorous scientific discipline at every stage.

Consider a psychologist developing a new screening measure for postpartum depression tailored to a specific cultural community. She cannot simply compile a list of questions that seem relevant, administer them, and call the result a validated instrument. Instead, she must carefully evaluate whether her items capture the construct she intends to measure, whether the instrument produces consistent scores across administrations, whether it performs equitably across demographic groups, and whether adequate norms exist to guide interpretation. The stakes are enormous: a poorly constructed screening tool might miss mothers in genuine crisis or, conversely, pathologize normal cultural expressions of distress.

Standard 9.05 addresses exactly this responsibility: psychologists who develop tests and other assessment techniques use appropriate psychometric procedures and current scientific or professional knowledge for test design, standardization, validation, reduction or elimination of bias, and recommendations for use. In other words, the Ethics Code holds test developers to the same empirical rigor that the field demands in any scientific endeavor — because an assessment instrument is, at its core, a measurement tool, and measurement tools must be precise, fair, and well-calibrated.

This standard applies specifically to psychologists who create new assessment instruments, and it requires that such instruments meet accepted standards for reliability, validity, and fairness. Reliability means the instrument yields consistent, reproducible results; validity means it actually measures what it claims to measure; and fairness means the instrument does not systematically disadvantage particular groups due to cultural, linguistic, or demographic bias. A test that is reliable but biased — or valid for one population but untested in another — falls short of the standard's requirements.

In practice, remember that Standard 9.05 is specifically about the construction side of assessment. It complements the standards that govern test use, interpretation, and data release by ensuring that the foundation — the instrument itself — is psychometrically sound. Think of it this way: Standards 9.02 through 9.04 govern how clinicians wield assessment tools responsibly, while 9.05 governs how developers forge those tools in the first place. A brilliant clinician cannot compensate for a fundamentally flawed instrument, which is why the Ethics Code addresses both sides of the equation.

Standard 9.06: Interpreting Assessment Results

When interpreting assessment results, including automated interpretations, psychologists take into account the purpose of the assessment as well as the various test factors, test-taking abilities, and other characteristics of the person being assessed, such as situational, personal, linguistic, and cultural differences, that might affect psychologists' judgments or reduce the accuracy of their interpretations. They indicate any significant limitations of their interpretations.

Example

A psychologist administering a neuropsychological battery to a client who recently suffered a concussion notes that the client was experiencing headaches and fatigue during testing. The psychologist must note these factors in the report as they may have attenuated test performance, potentially leading to an overestimation of cognitive impairment.

Standard 9.07: Assessment by Unqualified Persons

Psychologists do not promote the use of psychological assessment techniques by unqualified persons, except when such use is conducted for training purposes with appropriate supervision. When psychologists delegate scoring and interpretation to others (e.g., technicians, computer scoring services), they retain responsibility for the appropriate application, interpretation, and use of assessment instruments.

EPPP Tip

A psychometrist or testing technician may administer and score tests under supervision, but the psychologist retains full responsibility for interpretation and for ensuring the delegate is properly trained. This connects to Standard 2.05 (Delegation of Work to Others).

Standard 9.08: Obsolete Tests and Outdated Test Results

9.08(a) -- Obsolete Tests

Psychologists do not base their assessment or intervention decisions or recommendations on data or test results that are outdated for the current purpose.

9.08(b) -- Outdated Instruments

Psychologists do not base decisions or recommendations on tests and measures that are obsolete and not useful for the current purpose.

Example

A psychologist continuing to use the WISC-III (published 1991) for a cognitive evaluation when the WISC-V (published 2014) is available would potentially violate Standard 9.08, as the older version has outdated norms that may not accurately represent current population characteristics. There may be exceptions (e.g., research comparing results across versions), but for clinical purposes the most current edition should be used.

Standard 9.09: Test Scoring and Interpretation Services

Psychologists who offer assessment or scoring services to other professionals accurately describe the purpose, norms, validity, reliability, and applications of the procedures and any special qualifications applicable to their use. Psychologists select scoring and interpretation services (including automated services) on the basis of evidence of the validity of the program and procedures as well as on other appropriate considerations.

Computer-Based Test Interpretation (CBTI)

Psychologists who use computer-based test interpretation services must understand the limitations of such services. Automated interpretations are probabilistic statements based on group data and may not apply to a specific individual. The psychologist, not the computer program, is responsible for the final clinical interpretation.

Standard 9.10: Explaining Assessment Results

Imagine a client who has just completed a lengthy neuropsychological battery — three hours of testing that felt exhausting and, at times, anxiety-provoking. The testing is finished, the scores are computed, and now the client sits across from you and asks the most natural question in the world: "So, what did you find?" How you handle that moment — whether you provide a clear, compassionate explanation of what the results mean — is not merely good clinical practice. It is an ethical obligation codified in Standard 9.10 of the APA Ethics Code.

Having just explored the responsibilities psychologists carry when using scoring and interpretation services (Standard 9.09), we now turn to what happens after the numbers are generated: communicating those results to the people they belong to. No matter how sophisticated our instruments or how accurate our computer-generated interpretations, the assessment process remains incomplete until the person who was assessed understands what the findings mean for them.

Standard 9.10 establishes a clear default expectation: psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure that explanations of results are provided to the individual being assessed or to a designated representative. This obligation holds regardless of who actually scored and interpreted the test — whether the psychologist did it personally, delegated it to a trained employee or assistant, or used an automated or outside scoring service. The psychologist retains responsibility for ensuring that a meaningful explanation reaches the client.

Consider a school psychologist who administers a cognitive and achievement battery to a 9-year-old. The child's parents are the designated representatives, and Standard 9.10 requires the psychologist to take reasonable steps to explain the results to them in language they can understand — not simply mail home a printout of standard scores. Similarly, when a clinical psychologist administers a personality assessment to an adult therapy client, the psychologist should provide feedback that helps the client understand the findings and how they connect to treatment planning. This feedback session is often one of the most therapeutically powerful moments in the entire assessment process.

However, the standard recognizes that certain professional contexts create legitimate exceptions to this default rule. In some organizational consulting arrangements, preemployment or security screenings, and forensic evaluations, the nature of the professional relationship may preclude sharing results directly with the person assessed. A forensic psychologist conducting a competency-to-stand-trial evaluation for the court, for example, may not be in a position to provide a detailed explanation of findings to the defendant. A psychologist screening applicants for a law enforcement agency may report results only to the hiring authority. These are legitimate exceptions — but they come with a critical caveat: the person being assessed must be clearly informed in advance that they will not receive an explanation of results. This advance notification transforms what might otherwise feel like a baffling or unfair withholding of information into a transparent, ethically grounded boundary.

EPPP Tip

The default rule is that assessment results should be explained to the individual. Exceptions exist for organizational, forensic, and security contexts — but only when the person was informed in advance that results would not be shared with them. The EPPP tests whether you understand both the rule and its exceptions. When facing exam questions on this standard, look for answer choices that emphasize the two-part structure: (1) the general obligation to explain results, and (2) the requirement for prior notification when that obligation will not apply. A wrong answer on the exam often presents a scenario where the psychologist withholds results without having provided advance notice — that is the ethical violation the standard is designed to prevent.

Standard 9.11: Maintaining Test Security

Imagine a scenario: a client's attorney demands a complete copy of a neuropsychological test battery — not just the scores and responses, but the actual test booklets, stimulus cards, and scoring keys. If you hand everything over, the test is effectively compromised for future use — any future examinee who gains access to those materials could prepare responses in advance, rendering the instrument useless. But if you refuse entirely, you may appear to be withholding information the client has a right to see. This tension — between protecting the instruments that make valid assessment possible and honoring the rights of the people we assess — is exactly what Standard 9.11 addresses.

The term test materials refers to manuals, instruments, protocols, and test questions or stimuli — essentially, the proprietary components that make a test function as a standardized measurement tool. These materials often represent decades of research, careful norming, and significant financial investment by test publishers. Once compromised, a test may lose its validity entirely, which harms not just the publisher but every future client who might have benefited from an accurate assessment. Under Standard 9.11, psychologists are required to make reasonable efforts to maintain the integrity and security of test materials and other assessment techniques, doing so in a manner consistent with law, contractual obligations, and the broader Ethics Code.

Note the language carefully: the standard says "reasonable efforts," not absolute protection at all costs. This phrasing acknowledges that psychologists operate within legal systems that may, at times, compel disclosure. A court order, for instance, might override a psychologist's preference to keep materials secure. But even in such cases, the psychologist should advocate for protective measures — such as having materials reviewed only by a qualified professional rather than entered into open court records — to minimize unnecessary exposure.

Balancing Security and Client Rights

This is where the It is important to understand to test your understanding of a distinction introduced earlier in the Ethics Code (Standard 9.04): the difference between test data and test materials. These two categories may seem straightforward in theory, but in clinical practice they overlap in ways that demand careful professional judgment. Test security concerns must be balanced against clients' legitimate rights to access their own data, and getting this balance wrong — in either direction — can create serious ethical and legal problems.

Think of it this way: a client who completed an IQ assessment has a right to know their scores, to understand how they performed, and to obtain their responses if they request them. That information belongs in the domain of test data — it is about the client. But the test manual that explains how to administer and score the WAIS, the scoring algorithms, and the actual test questions themselves? Those are test materials — they belong to the instrument, not to the individual examinee. Releasing them would jeopardize the test's utility for everyone. Here is how the distinction plays out in practice:

  • A client may receive their scores and responses (test data)
  • A client should not receive the test manual, scoring key, or stimulus materials (test materials)
  • When test data are intertwined with test materials (e.g., a response booklet that includes test questions), the psychologist must use professional judgment to balance client rights with test security

That third bullet point is the one that generates the most exam questions — and the most real-world headaches. Consider a projective test response form where the client's answers are written directly on pages that also contain the test stimuli. You cannot release one without the other. In such cases, the psychologist might redact the stimulus portions, provide a summary of responses rather than the raw form, or release materials only to another qualified professional who can interpret them appropriately. The key principle the EPPP expects you to grasp is that neither test security nor client access is an absolute trump card; rather, psychologists must navigate the tension thoughtfully, document their reasoning, and aim for solutions that honor both values to the greatest extent possible.

Key Takeaways in clinical practice

  • Psychologists base assessment opinions on sufficient information and appropriate techniques (9.01). When personal examination is not possible, they must document limitations and qualify their conclusions.
  • Assessment instruments must be valid and reliable for the specific population and purpose (9.02). Cultural, linguistic, and individual factors must be considered.
  • Informed consent is required for assessments except when mandated by law, implied by context, or evaluating decisional capacity (9.03).
  • Test data (scores, responses, observations) can be released to clients upon request; test materials (manuals, questions, stimuli) are protected for test security (9.04 vs. 9.11).
  • Psychologists do not use obsolete tests or outdated test results for current purposes (9.08).
  • Computer-based interpretations are tools, not substitutes for clinical judgment; the psychologist retains responsibility for the final interpretation (9.09).
  • Assessment results should be explained to the individual unless the person was informed in advance that results would not be shared (9.10).
  • Psychologists retain full responsibility for assessment even when administration or scoring is delegated to others (9.07).

Overview of Standard 10: Therapy

Imagine a client who walks into your office for the first time. She is anxious, unsure of what to expect, and placing an extraordinary amount of trust in you — a person she has never met — to help her navigate some of the most painful experiences of her life. She may not fully understand what therapy involves, how long it might take, what her rights are, or what boundaries will govern your relationship. This moment, and every moment that follows in the therapeutic relationship, is precisely what Standard 10 of the APA Ethics Code is designed to protect.

The therapy relationship is unlike virtually any other professional relationship. Clients share their deepest fears, most shameful secrets, and most vulnerable emotions with their therapists. This level of intimate disclosure, combined with the inherent power differential between therapist and client, creates a context in which the potential for harm — whether through exploitation, negligence, or simple carelessness — is significant. It is no accident that the Ethics Code devotes an entire standard to the conduct of therapy relationships, and it is no accident that this standard appears so frequently on the EPPP.

Standard 10 of the APA Ethics Code governs the ethical conduct of psychotherapy and counseling relationships. This standard covers informed consent to therapy, specialized treatment contexts (couples, families, groups), restrictions on providing therapy to those already receiving services elsewhere, sexual boundaries, and the appropriate interruption and termination of therapy. Think of Standard 10 as the ethical architecture that supports every phase of the therapeutic relationship — from the very first session, when a client needs to understand what she is agreeing to, all the way through the final session, when the therapist must ensure that the ending serves the client's welfare rather than the therapist's convenience.

Standard 10 is a key areas in practice because therapy relationships involve significant power differentials and intimate disclosures that create particular vulnerability for clients. Exam questions in this domain often present nuanced clinical scenarios — a couple in therapy where one partner wants to share session content with an attorney, a group therapy member who encounters another member at a social event, a therapist who must relocate and needs to transfer or terminate with dozens of clients — and ask you to identify the most ethically appropriate course of action. Understanding the principles that undergird each subsection of Standard 10 will help you reason through these scenarios, even when the specific situation is one you have never encountered before.

As we move through the subsections of Standard 10, notice a recurring theme: the psychologist's obligation to prioritize client welfare at every decision point, while balancing the practical realities of clinical work. We begin with the foundation of all ethical therapy — informed consent — which sets the stage for a relationship built on transparency, respect, and collaboration.

Standard 10.01: Informed Consent to Therapy

10.01(a) -- General Requirements

When obtaining informed consent to therapy, psychologists inform clients as early as is feasible in the therapeutic relationship about:

  • The nature and anticipated course of therapy
  • Fees, involvement of third parties, and billing practices
  • Limits of confidentiality
  • Potential risks and benefits of treatment
  • Available alternatives to the proposed treatment
  • The client's right to refuse or withdraw from treatment at any time

10.01(b) -- Scientifically Unvalidated Treatments

When the therapist's technique or approach is substantially unvalidated or still developing, the psychologist must inform the client of the developing nature of the treatment, the potential risks involved, and available alternative treatments that may be effective.

10.01(c) -- Individuals Lacking Capacity

When the person receiving therapy is legally incapable of giving informed consent (e.g., a minor or an individual adjudicated incompetent), the psychologist provides an appropriate explanation, seeks the individual's assent, considers the person's preferences and best interests, and obtains appropriate permission from a legally authorized person.

EPPP Tip

Informed consent is not a one-time event but an ongoing process. As therapy progresses and circumstances change (e.g., switching from individual to couples therapy, adding a new treatment modality), informed consent should be revisited.

Standard 10.02: Therapy Involving Couples or Families

When a psychologist agrees to provide services to several persons who have a relationship (such as spouses, significant others, or parents and children), the psychologist takes reasonable steps to clarify at the outset:

  1. Which of the individuals are clients/patients
  2. The relationship the psychologist will have with each person
  3. The role of the psychologist (therapist, mediator, etc.)
  4. The probable uses of the services and information obtained

If it becomes apparent that the psychologist may be called on to perform potentially conflicting roles (such as family therapist and then witness for one party in divorce proceedings), the psychologist takes reasonable steps to clarify and modify, or withdraw from, roles as appropriate.

Example

A psychologist providing couples therapy learns that one partner wants to file for divorce and asks the psychologist to testify about the other partner's mental health. The psychologist should clarify that their role as couples therapist is incompatible with serving as a forensic evaluator or witness for one party. The psychologist should decline the forensic role and may need to discuss whether continuing couples therapy is appropriate.

Standard 10.03: Group Therapy

When psychologists provide services to several persons in a group setting, they describe at the outset the roles and responsibilities of all parties and the limits of confidentiality. The psychologist must explain that while the psychologist is bound by confidentiality, other group members are not and the psychologist cannot guarantee that group members will maintain each other's confidentiality.

Key Point

In group therapy, the therapist's ethical obligation to confidentiality applies to the therapist, but the therapist cannot compel other group members to keep information confidential. This limitation must be disclosed to all group members at the outset.

Standard 10.04: Providing Therapy to Those Served by Others

In deciding whether to offer or provide services to those already receiving mental health services elsewhere, psychologists carefully consider the treatment issues and the potential client's welfare. The psychologist discusses these issues with the client, or another legally authorized person on behalf of the client, to minimize the risk of confusion and conflict, consults with the other service providers when appropriate, and proceeds with caution and sensitivity to the therapeutic issues.

EPPP Tip

This standard does not prohibit providing services to someone already in treatment elsewhere -- it requires careful consideration. If a client is seeing another therapist, the new psychologist should discuss potential issues (e.g., conflicting treatment approaches), consider consulting with the other provider (with appropriate consent), and proceed carefully.

Standard 10.05: Sexual Intimacies with Current Therapy Clients/Patients

If the previous standard asked psychologists to exercise careful judgment when a client is already receiving services elsewhere, the current standard leaves no room for judgment at all — because none is needed. Few ethical principles in psychology are stated as categorically as this one, and for good reason. The therapeutic relationship creates a profound power differential: clients disclose their deepest vulnerabilities, transfer powerful emotions onto the therapist, and trust that the professional space will remain safe. When a therapist violates that trust through sexual contact, the harm to the client is often devastating — comparable in its psychological impact to childhood sexual abuse, according to decades of research on therapist-client sexual exploitation. This is why the prohibition against sexual contact with current clients is treated as the "bright line" rule of psychotherapy ethics: it admits no ambiguity and no exceptions.

Psychologists do not engage in sexual intimacies with current therapy clients/patients. This prohibition is absolute — there are no exceptions, regardless of the client's age, apparent consent, the client's level of functioning, or the nature of the therapeutic relationship. A client who says "I want this" does not transform the contact into something ethically permissible; the power imbalance inherent in the therapeutic relationship means that genuine informed consent in this context is considered impossible. Whether the therapy is long-term psychodynamic work or a brief, solution-focused intervention for a circumscribed problem, the rule is the same.

Sexual intimacies include sexual intercourse, sexual contact, kissing of a romantic or sexual nature, and other behaviors that are sexual in character. Importantly, this standard applies regardless of who initiates the sexual contact. A therapist cannot defend against an ethics charge by arguing that the client pursued the relationship. Consider a scenario in which a client with a history of using seductive behavior as a coping strategy makes explicit advances toward her therapist. Even if the therapist believes the client is acting autonomously, responding to those advances constitutes an ethical violation — full stop. The appropriate clinical response is to address the behavior therapeutically, seek consultation or supervision, and if necessary, refer the client to another provider.

Why is this standard so absolute when much of ethics involves contextual judgment? The answer lies in what research consistently shows: sexual contact between therapists and clients causes significant psychological harm, including increased depression, suicidality, difficulty trusting future therapists, confusion about boundaries, and symptoms resembling post-traumatic stress. The therapeutic frame — the predictable, boundaried space where healing occurs — is shattered beyond repair. Licensing boards and ethics committees treat violations of this standard with corresponding seriousness; sexual contact with a current client is among the most common reasons psychologists lose their licenses.

In practice, remember that this standard is one of the clearest in the entire Ethics Code: there is no clinical context, no client characteristic, and no therapist rationale that creates a valid exception. When you encounter exam questions about sexual intimacies with current clients, the answer will always reflect this absolute prohibition — regardless of how the question is framed or what mitigating circumstances are presented.

Standard 10.06: Sexual Intimacies with Relatives or Significant Others of Current Clients

Having established that sexual intimacies with current therapy clients are always prohibited — regardless of who initiates contact — we now turn to a closely related ethical boundary that extends the protective circle beyond the client themselves. Consider this scenario: a psychologist is providing individual therapy to a woman working through severe anxiety and relationship difficulties. Over several months, the psychologist meets the client's husband during a couples session and begins to feel a strong mutual attraction. The husband is not the psychologist's client, so would a sexual relationship with him be permissible? The answer, as Standard 10.06 makes unequivocally clear, is no.

Psychologists do not engage in sexual intimacies with individuals they know to be close relatives, guardians, or significant others of current clients/patients. This prohibition exists because the therapeutic relationship does not operate in a vacuum — it is embedded in the client's relational world. A sexual involvement with someone in the client's intimate circle would almost inevitably compromise the therapist's objectivity, create devastating conflicts of interest, and introduce a profound betrayal into the client's support system. Imagine the woman in our scenario discovering that her therapist — the person she trusted with her deepest vulnerabilities — had become sexually involved with her husband. The damage would extend far beyond the therapy room, shattering both the therapeutic alliance and the client's personal relationships simultaneously.

This standard reflects an important principle that runs throughout the Ethics Code: the psychologist's ethical obligations extend beyond the narrow dyad of the therapy relationship to encompass those who could be harmed by the psychologist's conduct. Even if the relative, guardian, or significant other is not a client, the psychologist's duty to protect the welfare of the person who is in treatment creates a clear boundary. The language "individuals they know to be" is worth noting — the standard applies when the psychologist has knowledge of the relationship. A psychologist who genuinely had no way of knowing that a person was connected to a current client would not be in violation, though such situations are rare in practice and clinicians are expected to exercise reasonable diligence.

Clinically, this boundary also protects the integrity of the therapeutic process itself. Therapy often involves exploring family dynamics, attachment patterns, and relational conflicts. If the therapist were sexually involved with the client's partner, parent, or sibling, the therapist's ability to help the client navigate those very relationships would be hopelessly compromised. The therapist could not maintain neutrality, could not offer honest clinical guidance, and could not serve the client's best interests. For these reasons, Standard 10.06 draws a firm ethical line that shields not only the client but also the broader therapeutic endeavor from the destructive effects of such dual relationships.

Standard 10.07: Therapy with Former Sexual Partners

Psychologists do not accept as therapy clients/patients persons with whom they have engaged in sexual intimacies. This prohibition is absolute and permanent -- there is no time limit or exception. If a psychologist has had a sexual relationship with someone, that person may never become the psychologist's therapy client.

Key Distinction

Standard 10.07 addresses the direction from sexual partner to therapy client (always prohibited). Standard 10.08 addresses the direction from therapy client to sexual partner (prohibited for at least two years, and rarely appropriate even after). Both reflect the permanent power imbalance created by the therapeutic relationship.

Standard 10.08: Sexual Intimacies with Former Therapy Clients

Consider a scenario that surfaces with surprising regularity in ethics consultations: A psychologist terminated therapy with a client eighteen months ago. The former client reaches out — not for a session, but for a date. They explain that they've moved on, feel completely resolved, and want to explore a personal connection. The psychologist, genuinely attracted and convinced no harm would come of it, wonders whether enough time has passed. This is precisely the situation Standard 10.08 was designed to address, and the answer — at eighteen months — is unequivocal.

In the previous section, we examined Standard 10.07's absolute prohibition on providing therapy to someone with whom the psychologist has had a sexual relationship. That standard addresses movement in one direction — from sexual partner to therapy client — and the answer is always "no." Standard 10.08 now addresses movement in the opposite direction: from therapy client to sexual partner. Here the Ethics Code adopts a nuanced but deeply cautious stance, reflecting the reality that the power dynamics forged in therapy do not simply vanish the moment a termination letter is signed. Transference, emotional vulnerability, and the lingering authority of the therapist role can persist for years — sometimes indefinitely.

Psychologists do not engage in sexual intimacies with former clients/patients for at least two years after cessation or termination of therapy. This two-year minimum represents an absolute floor: no combination of circumstances, mutual desire, or apparent psychological health on the client's part can justify a sexual relationship within that window. The clock begins ticking at the point of termination — not at the start of therapy, not at some midpoint, but at the final session or formal termination date.

Even after two years, psychologists do not engage in sexual intimacies with former clients except in the most unusual circumstances. The psychologist who engages in such activity bears the burden of demonstrating that there has been no exploitation, in light of all relevant factors, including:

  1. The amount of time that has passed since therapy terminated
  2. The nature, duration, and intensity of the therapy
  3. The circumstances of termination
  4. The client's personal history
  5. The client's current mental status
  6. The likelihood of adverse impact on the client
  7. Any statements or actions made by the therapist during therapy suggesting or inviting a posttermination sexual or romantic relationship

Notice how heavily this standard is weighted against the psychologist. The phrase "most unusual circumstances" sets an extraordinarily high bar — it signals that the Ethics Code views post-therapy sexual relationships as almost never appropriate, even beyond the two-year mark. The burden of proof falls entirely on the psychologist, not the client. Imagine a licensing board hearing in which a psychologist must demonstrate, across all seven factors listed above, that no exploitation occurred. A client who was seen for three sessions of career coaching five years ago presents a very different profile than a client who engaged in intensive psychodynamic therapy exploring attachment trauma over the course of two years. The deeper, longer, and more emotionally charged the therapy, the stronger the presumption against any subsequent sexual relationship.

Factor seven deserves particular attention because it reaches back into the therapy itself. If a psychologist made any statement during treatment — even subtle, even ambiguous — that hinted at future romantic possibilities, the entire post-termination relationship becomes ethically indefensible. This factor effectively prohibits therapists from "grooming" clients during therapy with the intention of pursuing a relationship after the two-year waiting period expires. Ethics committees and licensing boards take an especially dim view of cases where termination appears to have been orchestrated to start the two-year clock running.

Most ethics scholars and professional organizations go further than the code itself, advising that sexual relationships with former therapy clients should simply be avoided altogether. The Canadian Psychological Association, for instance, maintains an absolute lifetime ban. Many seasoned practitioners adopt this stricter stance as personal policy, recognizing that the potential for harm — to the former client, to public trust in the profession, and to the psychologist's own career — almost always outweighs any claimed benefit of the relationship.

EPPP Tip

The two-year rule (10.08) is one of the most tested facts on the EPPP. Key points: (1) The two-year clock starts at termination, not at the beginning of therapy. (2) Even after two years, sexual contact is permitted only in the "most unusual circumstances." (3) The burden of proof falls entirely on the psychologist. (4) Most ethics scholars advise that sexual relationships with former clients should always be avoided. When answering exam questions, remember that the two-year period is a minimum, not a green light — and watch for distractors that place the start of the clock at the wrong point or that shift the burden of proof onto the former client.

Standard 10.09: Interruption of Therapy

Having examined the ethical boundaries that protect clients after therapy ends — particularly the stringent rules around sexual intimacies with former clients — we now turn to a different but equally important concern: what happens when therapy is unexpectedly disrupted while it is still ongoing. Imagine a psychologist in a community mental health center who learns on a Friday afternoon that the agency is closing its doors permanently at the end of the month. She has forty active clients, several of whom are in the midst of processing significant trauma. What obligations does she have? Or consider a psychologist in private practice who receives a sudden medical diagnosis requiring immediate surgery and an extended recovery. His clients — some of whom have histories of abandonment and attachment disruption — will experience his absence not merely as an inconvenience but potentially as a reactivation of old wounds. These are the real-world scenarios that Standard 10.09 was designed to address, and they underscore a fundamental ethical truth: the therapeutic relationship carries responsibilities that extend beyond the therapy hour itself.

At its core, Standard 10.09 requires that when psychologists enter into employment or contractual relationships, they make reasonable efforts to provide for orderly and appropriate resolution of responsibility for client/patient care in the event that the employment or contractual relationship ends, with paramount consideration given to the welfare of the client/patient. Notice the language carefully — this standard is forward-looking and preventive. It does not merely tell psychologists what to do when a disruption occurs; it tells them to plan for potential disruptions before they happen, ideally at the very point when they are entering a new professional arrangement. Think of it as the ethical equivalent of an emergency preparedness plan: you hope you never need it, but you are professionally obligated to have one in place.

The practical implications of this standard are significant. Psychologists should anticipate a range of potential disruptions in service — illness, disability, relocation, organizational restructuring, loss of funding, or even death — and ensure that mechanisms exist for clients to continue receiving appropriate care. This might involve identifying backup clinicians who can step in on short notice, maintaining organized and accessible clinical records so that a covering professional can quickly understand a client's treatment history, or negotiating contractual provisions with employers that guarantee adequate transition time when a position ends. For psychologists working in institutional settings such as hospitals, universities, or government agencies, this planning is especially critical because the psychologist may have limited control over employment decisions made by the organization.

Consider the clinical weight of this standard through the lens of the therapeutic alliance. Research consistently demonstrates that the quality of the therapeutic relationship is one of the strongest predictors of positive treatment outcomes. An abrupt, unplanned interruption in therapy — particularly one that leaves the client without a clear path to continued care — can damage that alliance, erode trust in the mental health system more broadly, and in severe cases contribute to clinical deterioration. For clients with borderline personality features, trauma histories, or chronic suicidality, an unplanned disruption without adequate transition planning could constitute a genuine risk to safety. Standard 10.09 recognizes that the welfare of the client must be the paramount consideration in all decisions about how service interruptions are managed, elevating client well-being above administrative convenience, financial considerations, or the psychologist's personal circumstances.

For EPPP preparation, remember that this standard emphasizes proactive planning rather than reactive crisis management. The ethical psychologist does not wait until disruption is imminent to consider what will happen to their clients — they build continuity-of-care provisions into their professional arrangements from the outset. When disruptions do occur, the psychologist's obligation is to ensure that the transition is as orderly and clinically appropriate as possible, minimizing harm and maintaining the client's access to needed services. This principle connects naturally to the next standard we will examine — Standard 10.10 on terminating therapy — which addresses the psychologist's responsibilities when the therapeutic relationship reaches its planned or necessary conclusion.

Standard 10.10: Terminating Therapy

10.10(a) -- When to Terminate

Psychologists terminate therapy when it becomes reasonably clear that the client/patient no longer needs the service, is not likely to benefit, or is being harmed by continued service.

10.10(b) -- Pretermination Counseling

Except where precluded by the actions of clients/patients or third-party payors, psychologists provide pretermination counseling and suggest alternative service providers as appropriate prior to termination.

10.10(c) -- Threats and Relationships

Psychologists may terminate therapy when threatened or otherwise endangered by the client/patient or another person with whom the client/patient has a relationship.

Example

A psychologist's client has met their treatment goals and has been stable for several months. The psychologist discusses the client's progress, proposes termination, and works with the client to develop a plan for maintaining gains. The psychologist provides referral information in case the client needs services in the future. This is an example of appropriate, planned termination.

EPPP Tip

Termination is appropriate when: (1) the client has met treatment goals, (2) the client is no longer benefiting, (3) continued treatment could cause harm, or (4) the therapist is endangered. Abandonment -- abruptly discontinuing services without appropriate notice and referral -- is an ethical violation. Even when a client refuses to pay, the psychologist should make reasonable efforts to provide pretermination counseling and referrals.

Key Takeaways in clinical practice

  • Informed consent to therapy must include information about the nature and course of therapy, fees, confidentiality limits, risks and benefits, alternatives, and the right to withdraw (10.01).
  • In couples/family therapy, the psychologist must clarify roles, identify who the client(s) are, and address potential conflicts at the outset (10.02).
  • In group therapy, the psychologist cannot guarantee other members' confidentiality and must disclose this limitation (10.03).
  • Providing therapy to someone already seen by another provider requires careful consideration and consultation -- it is not prohibited but must be handled thoughtfully (10.04).
  • Sexual intimacies with current clients and their relatives/significant others are always prohibited (10.05, 10.06).
  • Psychologists may never accept former sexual partners as therapy clients (10.07).
  • Sexual intimacies with former clients are prohibited for at least two years after termination, and even then only in the most unusual circumstances (10.08).
  • Termination should occur when the client has met goals, is not benefiting, or is being harmed (10.10). Pretermination counseling and referrals should be provided.
  • Abandonment -- abruptly ending treatment without notice or referral -- is always an ethical violation.
