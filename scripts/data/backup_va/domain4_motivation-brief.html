Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)

Most of the therapies covered in this domain share an assumption so fundamental that it often goes unexamined: to help a client, you must first understand their problem. Psychoanalysts trace symptoms to unconscious conflicts. Cognitive therapists map distorted thinking patterns. Behaviorists conduct functional assessments. But what if a therapist could help a client improve without ever fully understanding the problem at all? What if the solution and the problem are not necessarily related? This counterintuitive proposition, developed by Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, represents one of the most radical departures in the history of psychotherapy. Rather than exploring the origins or maintenance of problems, SFBT directs attention toward constructing solutions by identifying what clients already do well and amplifying those successes.

Core Philosophy

SFBT operates on several key assumptions: (1) if it isn't broken, don't fix it; (2) if something works, do more of it; (3) if something doesn't work, do something different; (4) small steps can lead to large changes; and (5) the solution is not necessarily related to the problem.

The Miracle Question

The miracle question is the hallmark technique of SFBT. The therapist asks: "Suppose tonight, while you are sleeping, a miracle happens and the problem that brought you here is solved. When you wake up tomorrow morning, what will be different that will tell you a miracle has happened?" This question serves multiple functions:

  • Bypasses the client's sense of hopelessness by creating a vivid vision of a preferred future
  • Identifies concrete, behavioral goals for therapy in the client's own language
  • Shifts focus from problem-saturated narratives to solution-oriented thinking
  • Reveals what the client truly values and wants, rather than simply what they want to eliminate

Scaling Questions

Scaling questions ask clients to rate their current experience on a scale of 1 to 10. For example: "On a scale of 1 to 10, where 10 means you are handling your anxiety as well as you ever could, where would you say you are today?" Follow-up questions explore what has helped the client reach their current number and what a one-point improvement would look like.

Clinical Example

Therapist: "You said you're at a 4 today. What are you doing that has you at a 4 rather than a 1?"

Client: "Well, I have been going on walks and calling my sister more."

Therapist: "That's impressive. What would be different at a 5?"

This questioning identifies existing strengths and generates incremental, achievable goals.

Exception Questions and Coping Questions

Exception questions explore times when the problem could have occurred but did not: "Tell me about a time recently when you expected the panic to happen but it didn't." These questions highlight client competencies and suggest that solutions are already partially in place.

Coping questions are used when clients feel overwhelmed and cannot identify exceptions: "Given how difficult things have been, how have you managed to keep going?" This validates the client's struggle while simultaneously identifying resilience.

SFBT Session Structure

A typical SFBT session includes: (1) establishing what the client wants from the session, (2) exploring exceptions and competencies, (3) using the miracle question or scaling, (4) a consultation break where the therapist reflects, and (5) delivering feedback that includes compliments and a therapeutic task. Sessions are typically limited to 5-8 meetings.

Motivational Interviewing (MI)

Having explored how Solution-Focused Brief Therapy harnesses clients' existing strengths and preferred futures in just a handful of sessions, we turn now to a challenge that every clinician faces sooner or later: what do you do when a client simultaneously wants to change and doesn't want to change? Picture a man sitting in your office after his third DUI. He knows his drinking is destroying his marriage, his health, and now his driving record. Yet he opens the session by saying, "I don't really have a problem — I just got unlucky." He is not lying, and he is not in denial in the psychoanalytic sense. He is genuinely of two minds. That tug-of-war — the experience of wanting something and fearing it at the same time — is ambivalence, and it is one of the most common obstacles to behavior change. Motivational Interviewing was built specifically to help people navigate that ambivalence and find their own reasons for moving forward.

William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick developed Motivational Interviewing as a directive yet client-centered counseling style designed to elicit behavior change by helping clients explore and resolve their ambivalence from within. Miller first articulated the approach in 1983 while working with problem drinkers, noticing that empathic, non-confrontational conversations about drinking produced better outcomes than aggressive confrontation — a finding that ran counter to the prevailing treatment culture of the time. Together, Miller and Rollnick refined MI into a systematic approach that honors client autonomy while gently guiding the conversation toward change. Although originally developed for substance use treatment, MI has since demonstrated effectiveness across a wide range of health behaviors, from medication adherence and diet modification to gambling cessation and chronic disease management.

The Spirit of MI

MI is characterized by four interrelated elements that together define its underlying philosophy — what practitioners call its "spirit." These elements are: (1) Partnership — MI is done "with" and "for" the client, not "to" or "on" the client; the therapist and client collaborate as equals exploring the client's life; (2) Acceptance — which encompasses absolute worth (prizing the client's inherent value), autonomy support (honoring the client's right to choose), accurate empathy (striving to understand the client's internal frame of reference), and affirmation (recognizing strengths and efforts); (3) Compassion — actively promoting the client's welfare and prioritizing their needs above the clinician's agenda; and (4) Evocation — drawing out the client's own motivations, values, and resources rather than installing them from the outside. When clinicians internalize this spirit, MI techniques flow naturally; without it, the techniques become hollow manipulation.

MI draws on several foundational theoretical traditions, weaving them into a distinctive clinical approach. From Carl Rogers's person-centered therapy, MI inherits its emphasis on accurate empathy, unconditional positive regard, and a non-judgmental therapeutic stance — the conviction that clients possess an inherent capacity for growth when the right relational conditions are present. Rogers demonstrated that when therapists listen deeply and reflect without judgment, clients begin to hear themselves more clearly. MI harnesses that same principle: by reflecting a client's own words with precision, the therapist creates a conversational mirror in which the client can examine their values and behaviors with fresh eyes.

From Albert Bandura's self-efficacy theory, MI incorporates the recognition that a person's confidence in their ability to perform a specific behavior is a critical determinant of whether change actually occurs. A client may desperately want to quit smoking but feel certain she will fail — and that belief alone can keep her reaching for another cigarette. Accordingly, MI therapists actively support and strengthen the client's belief that change is achievable, often by highlighting past successes, however small, and affirming the client's resourcefulness.

Finally, MI is closely linked to the transtheoretical model of behavior change, which provides a framework for understanding where a client falls along the continuum of readiness and helps the clinician match interventions to the client's current stage — an idea we will explore in detail shortly. This integration of humanistic relational principles, social-cognitive theory, and a stage-based model of change gives MI its distinctive character as both directive and deeply respectful of client autonomy.

OARS: Core Skills of MI

If the spirit of MI describes the heart of the approach, the core micro-skills describe its hands — the moment-to-moment conversational moves that bring MI to life. Practitioners capture these skills with the acronym OARS, a fitting image for steering a conversation through the sometimes choppy waters of ambivalence. Think of a therapist sitting with a college student who has been binge drinking every weekend but insists everything is fine. The therapist doesn't lecture or list consequences. Instead, she asks, "What role does drinking play in your weekends?" (an open-ended question), responds to his candor with, "You've really thought about this honestly, which isn't easy" (an affirmation), reflects his mixed feelings by saying, "So part of you loves the social connection, and part of you is scared of where it's heading" (a reflection), and periodically ties his statements together into a coherent narrative (a summary). Each of these moves is deliberately designed to draw out the client's own voice rather than impose the therapist's agenda.

Change Talk and Sustain Talk

One of MI's most important contributions to clinical practice is the recognition that the language clients use during sessions actually predicts their behavior after sessions. When clients voice their own reasons, desires, and plans for change — rather than hearing those arguments from the therapist — they become significantly more likely to follow through. MI practitioners call this self-motivating language change talk, and they organize its varieties with the mnemonic DARN-CAT:

  • Desire -- "I want to..." / Ability -- "I could..." / Reasons -- "Because..." / Need -- "I have to..." (Preparatory)
  • Commitment -- "I will..." / Activation -- "I'm ready to..." / Taking steps -- "I actually did..." (Mobilizing)

Notice the distinction between the first group — preparatory change talk — and the second — mobilizing change talk. A client who says "I want to cut back on drinking" (desire) is in a very different place than one who says "I will stop drinking on weekdays starting tonight" (commitment). Preparatory change talk signals that the client is warming up to the idea of change; mobilizing change talk signals readiness to act. Skilled MI therapists listen for the shift from DARN to CAT as a sign that the client is moving toward action.

On the other side of the ambivalence equation sits sustain talk — any client speech that favors maintaining the status quo. "I don't think it's really that bad" or "I've tried quitting before and it never works" are classic examples. A critical MI principle is that the therapist never argues against sustain talk. Arguing pushes the client into a defensive posture, which only intensifies their commitment to the status quo. Instead, the MI therapist responds with empathic reflections and gently explores the ambivalence, trusting that when both sides of the dilemma are laid out honestly, the client's own values will tip the balance toward change.

Developing Discrepancy

One of the most powerful strategic principles in MI is developing discrepancy — the process of helping clients perceive a gap between where they currently are and where they want to be. Specifically, the therapist guides the client to recognize how their present behavior conflicts with their deeply held values, personal goals, or sense of identity. For example, a client who values being a responsible parent may come to see how continued heavy drinking undermines that self-concept. The resulting cognitive and emotional tension — the awareness that "what I'm doing doesn't match who I want to be" — becomes a natural catalyst for change motivation. This is not a trick or a technique imposed from outside; it is a genuine moment of self-recognition that carries enormous motivational power precisely because it originates within the client.

Crucially, in MI the therapist does not create this discrepancy through confrontation or argumentation. Instead, discrepancy emerges organically through skilled use of open-ended questions, reflections, and summaries that juxtapose the client's stated values against their current actions. The client voices the contradiction, which proves far more persuasive than having the clinician point it out. Consider the difference between a therapist saying, "Don't you see that your drinking is hurting your kids?" — which triggers defensiveness — and a therapist reflecting, "You've described yourself as someone who would do anything for your children, and you've also noticed that the drinking has been taking you away from them in the evenings." The second approach places two truths side by side and invites the client to sit with the tension. This principle aligns with the broader MI philosophy that sustainable motivation must come from within the client rather than being imposed externally.

Example

A client states, "Being healthy enough to watch my grandchildren grow up is the most important thing to me." Later in the conversation, the client describes smoking two packs a day. The MI therapist might offer a reflective summary: "On one hand, seeing your grandchildren thrive matters more to you than anything else. On the other hand, you're noticing what the smoking is doing to your health." By placing these statements side by side without judgment, the therapist allows the client to sit with the discrepancy and draw their own conclusions about the need for change.

The Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)

MI is closely associated with Prochaska and DiClemente's Transtheoretical Model (TTM), and understanding this model is essential for knowing when and how to apply MI strategies effectively. The TTM proposes that behavior change does not happen in a single moment of decision — it unfolds through a series of predictable stages. A clinician who mistakes a contemplator for someone ready for action may push too hard and provoke resistance; one who treats an action-stage client like a contemplator may waste valuable momentum. Recognizing the client's current stage allows the therapist to meet them where they are, which is one of MI's guiding principles.

  1. Precontemplation -- Not yet considering change; unaware or in denial of the problem
  2. Contemplation -- Aware of the problem but ambivalent; weighing pros and cons
  3. Preparation -- Intending to take action soon; making small preliminary steps
  4. Action -- Actively modifying behavior; requires considerable commitment of time and energy
  5. Maintenance -- Sustaining the change over time; working to prevent relapse

The transtheoretical model specifies that different processes of change are optimally matched to different stages — a principle that has profound clinical implications. Prochaska and DiClemente identified ten processes, broadly divided into two families. The experiential (cognitive-affective) processes — consciousness raising, dramatic relief, environmental reevaluation, self-reevaluation, and social liberation — involve shifts in awareness, emotion, and perspective. The behavioral processes — self-liberation, counterconditioning, stimulus control, reinforcement management, and helping relationships — involve concrete actions and environmental modifications. Experiential processes tend to be most relevant during the earlier stages (precontemplation and contemplation), where the primary task is building awareness and motivation. Behavioral processes become more important during the action and maintenance stages, where the individual is actively modifying habits and managing their environment. Matching the right process to the right stage is what separates effective, stage-sensitive intervention from well-intentioned but poorly timed clinical advice.

Self-Liberation

Self-liberation involves making a firm commitment to change and believing in one's capacity to follow through. It corresponds to the act of choosing to act — a willful decision that one will change. Think of it as the moment when a person moves from "I might quit smoking" to "I am quitting smoking." This process is most useful during the preparation and action stages, when the individual has resolved their ambivalence and is ready to commit. It represents an internal declaration of intent that mobilizes the person's resources toward a specific behavioral goal.

For clients still in the contemplation stage, self-liberation is generally considered the least appropriate intervention — and this is a distinction worth highlighting in clinical practice. Contemplators are still ambivalent and have not yet decided to change; asking them to make a firm behavioral commitment is premature and may provoke the very resistance MI seeks to avoid. Instead, contemplators benefit most from experiential processes — particularly self-reevaluation (examining how the problem behavior conflicts with personal values and self-image) and consciousness raising (gaining new information about the behavior and its consequences). Attempting to mobilize commitment before ambivalence is adequately explored violates a core principle of stage-matched intervention and often backfires, pushing the client further from change rather than closer to it.

Example

A client who drinks heavily is aware of health risks but remains uncertain about quitting. A therapist using a stage-matched approach would avoid pushing for a firm pledge of sobriety (self-liberation). Instead, the therapist might encourage the client to reflect on how drinking fits — or doesn't fit — with their identity as a parent (self-reevaluation), or explore new information about long-term health consequences (consciousness raising). Premature commitment demands can backfire, increasing defensiveness and sustain talk.

EPPP Clinical Application

A key principle of MI — and one that is clinically important — involves the righting reflex, which is the therapist's natural, well-intentioned tendency to try to fix the client's problem or persuade them to change. Most clinicians enter the field because they want to help, and when they see someone engaging in self-destructive behavior, every instinct screams, "Let me tell you what you need to do." But MI teaches that this impulse, however compassionate, often backfires. When clinicians argue for change, clients instinctively argue against it — generating more sustain talk and digging deeper into their current position. MI posits that the client, not the therapist, should be the one voicing the arguments for change. Rolling with resistance, rather than confronting it head-on, remains a core MI strategy. On exam questions, look for answer choices that have the therapist reflecting ambivalence and evoking change talk rather than lecturing, advising, or warning — those reflective responses align with MI principles.

Prochaska, J. O., & DiClemente, C. C. (1983). Stages and processes of self-change of smoking: Toward an integrative model of change. Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology, 51(3), 390-395.

The contemplation stage is characterized by growing awareness that a problem exists, coupled with significant ambivalence about whether to address it. Individuals in this stage are actively weighing the advantages and disadvantages of modifying their behavior. They are not yet committed to action but generally believe they will be ready to begin making changes within approximately six months. This internal cost-benefit analysis can persist for extended periods — some individuals remain "chronic contemplators" for years, cycling through ambivalence without progressing. The contemplation stage is where techniques like the decisional balance exercise are most naturally aligned, as the person is already engaged in evaluating the trade-offs of change versus the status quo. Clinicians working with contemplators should resist the urge to push toward premature action and instead help the client fully explore both sides of their ambivalence.

Interpersonal Therapy (IPT)

Interpersonal Therapy was developed by Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman in the 1970s, originally as a time-limited treatment for major depression. IPT focuses on resolving interpersonal problems and improving interpersonal functioning, based on the premise that psychiatric symptoms arise in an interpersonal context and that improving relationships will alleviate symptoms.

Four Interpersonal Problem Areas

IPT organizes treatment around one or two of four defined problem areas:

Problem AreaDescriptionTherapeutic Focus
GriefComplicated bereavement following the death of a significant otherFacilitate mourning process, help establish new relationships and interests
Role DisputesConflicts with a significant other due to differing expectationsIdentify the dispute, determine the stage (renegotiation, impasse, dissolution), plan strategies
Role TransitionsDifficulty coping with life changes (e.g., divorce, retirement, diagnosis)Mourn the loss of the old role, develop a positive view of the new role, build mastery
Interpersonal DeficitsImpoverished social networks, chronic loneliness, or poor social skillsReduce social isolation, enhance social skills, form new relationships

Phases of IPT

IPT is typically conducted in 12-16 weekly sessions organized into three phases:

  1. Initial Phase (Sessions 1-3): Assess symptoms, conduct an interpersonal inventory (review of significant relationships), identify the primary problem area, assign the "sick role" (legitimizing the patient's need for help), and establish the treatment contract
  2. Middle Phase (Sessions 4-12): Work on the identified problem area using specific strategies; therapist uses techniques such as communication analysis, role playing, and exploring parallels between relationships
  3. Termination Phase (Sessions 13-16): Consolidate gains, anticipate future difficulties, establish the patient's sense of competence, and address feelings about ending therapy

Evidence Base for IPT

IPT is one of the most well-researched psychotherapies. It has demonstrated efficacy for major depression (comparable to CBT and medication), bulimia nervosa, and is recommended in treatment guidelines by the American Psychiatric Association. The NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program found IPT and CBT were both effective for mild to moderate depression, with IPT showing particular advantages for patients with severe interpersonal difficulties.

Additional Brief Therapy Concepts

MI Processes: The Four-Process Model

Miller and Rollnick (2013) organized MI into four overlapping processes that guide the flow of a session:

  1. Engaging -- Establishing a working relationship and mutual trust; the foundation for everything that follows
  2. Focusing -- Developing and maintaining a specific direction for the conversation about change
  3. Evoking -- Eliciting the client's own motivations for change (change talk); the heart of MI
  4. Planning -- Developing commitment to change and formulating a specific plan of action when the client is ready

SFBT: Compliments and First-Session Tasks

At the end of each SFBT session, the therapist delivers carefully constructed compliments that validate the client's existing strengths and coping strategies. Following compliments, the therapist may assign a first-session task -- most famously the formula first-session task: "Between now and next time we meet, I'd like you to observe what happens in your life that you want to continue to have happen." This observation task shifts attention from problems to strengths.

IPT and the Medical Model

IPT is unique among psychotherapies in its deliberate use of a medical model. Depression is conceptualized as a medical illness, and the therapist explicitly assigns the patient to a sick role during the initial phase. This serves to: (1) reduce guilt and self-blame; (2) legitimize the need for help; and (3) provide temporary relief from overwhelming role expectations. However, the sick role is time-limited -- a key therapeutic goal is helping the patient gradually resume normal role functioning.

Decisional Balance in MI

The decisional balance exercise is a technique in which the client explores four quadrants: (1) benefits of the status quo, (2) costs of the status quo, (3) benefits of change, and (4) costs of change. This structured exploration of ambivalence helps the client articulate their own reasons for change without the therapist arguing for it. The decisional balance is particularly useful in the contemplation stage, where the person is actively weighing pros and cons.

Brief Therapy: Common Principles

Having compared the major brief therapy approaches side by side, a natural question emerges: what do all of these models share? Despite their very different theoretical lineages — from psychodynamic conflict theory to solution-focused pragmatism to the spirit of motivational interviewing — brief therapies converge on a surprisingly consistent set of operating principles. Understanding these shared features is essential not only in clinical practice but for clinical practice, because they reveal what makes brevity therapeutic rather than merely abbreviated.

Consider two therapists working in a community mental health center. One sees a client for 52 weekly sessions of open-ended exploration; the other sees a different client with a similar presenting problem for just 12 sessions. The second therapist isn't simply doing "less therapy." She is doing a fundamentally different kind of therapy — one shaped by urgency, precision, and a deliberate harnessing of time pressure as a catalyst for change. The principles below capture what makes that possible.

  • Time limitation -- A defined number of sessions creates urgency and focuses the work; the time limit itself is considered therapeutic. Knowing that session ten of twelve is approaching mobilizes both client and therapist in ways that an open-ended frame does not.
  • Focus -- A specific, circumscribed problem or theme is identified early and maintained throughout treatment. When a client raises a new concern in session six, the brief therapist gently redirects back to the agreed-upon focal issue rather than following every emerging thread.
  • Therapist activity -- Brief therapy clinicians are generally more active and directive than in long-term approaches. Rather than sitting with long silences or waiting for material to unfold organically, the therapist steers, summarizes, and intervenes with purpose.
  • Rapid alliance formation -- The therapeutic relationship must be established quickly, as there is limited time. Research consistently shows that a strong early alliance is among the best predictors of outcome in brief treatment — there simply isn't time to let trust develop gradually over months.
  • Goal orientation -- Clear, measurable goals are established collaboratively in early sessions. Both therapist and client know what success looks like, which keeps the treatment anchored and allows for meaningful evaluation of progress.
  • Emphasis on client strengths -- Identifying and building on existing resources rather than exploring pathology in depth. A brief therapist asks, "What has worked for you before?" as readily as "What brought you in today?"

Together, these principles create a treatment frame that is inherently action-oriented and efficient. They also help explain why brief therapy is not appropriate for every client or every problem — its power depends on the ability to focus, form a rapid alliance, and work within a boundaried time frame. With these shared principles in mind, let us turn to one of the most clinically rich brief therapy traditions: the psychodynamic models.

Brief Psychodynamic Therapies

Imagine a 32-year-old professional who seeks therapy after a painful breakup — her third in five years. Each relationship followed the same arc: initial idealization, growing anxiety about abandonment, escalating demands for reassurance, and eventual rejection by a partner who felt suffocated. She is bright, motivated, and able to articulate what keeps happening, yet she cannot seem to stop the pattern. A long-term psychoanalyst might settle in for years of exploration. But what if a skilled clinician could zero in on the core conflict driving this cycle and use the intensity of a time-limited relationship — including the feelings the client develops toward the therapist — to produce meaningful change in 20 sessions?

Brief psychodynamic therapy (BPT) adapts psychoanalytic principles to a time-limited format, typically ranging from 12 to 40 sessions. Several models fall under this umbrella, including the work of Davanloo, Malan, Sifneos, and Strupp. Although these approaches differ in specific technique — Sifneos, for example, emphasizes anxiety-provoking confrontation while Strupp focuses on cyclical maladaptive patterns — they share a number of core features that distinguish them from open-ended psychodynamic treatment while preserving its theoretical foundation.

Common characteristics of brief psychodynamic approaches include: (1) early identification of a central or focal conflict that organizes the treatment — in our example above, the client's unconscious expectation that attachment inevitably leads to abandonment; (2) active use of interpretation, particularly linking current relational patterns to early developmental experiences (perhaps the client's emotionally unavailable mother); (3) explicit attention to the therapeutic relationship, including the analysis of transference as a productive vehicle for understanding the client's relational world; and (4) the therapist adopting a more active and directive stance than in traditional long-term psychoanalysis. Notice how these features align with the general brief therapy principles we just reviewed — focus, therapist activity, goal orientation — while adding distinctly psychodynamic content.

A critical point for examination purposes concerns the role of transference. Unlike some characterizations that might suggest transference disrupts therapeutic progress, brief psychodynamic therapists view transference reactions as therapeutically valuable material. Transference is not regarded as an obstacle that must be minimized or avoided. On the contrary, it is actively explored and interpreted as a window into the client's recurring interpersonal patterns. When our hypothetical client begins to worry that her therapist will "give up on her" after session eight, the BPT clinician does not simply reassure — she uses this moment to illuminate the very abandonment schema that sabotages the client's romantic relationships. The time-limited frame can actually intensify transference reactions, and skilled BPT clinicians leverage this intensity to accelerate insight and emotional processing. The approaching termination date itself becomes a laboratory for exploring the client's responses to loss and separation.

Clinical Note

When encountering EPPP items about brief psychodynamic therapy, remember that all major BPT models treat transference as a core therapeutic tool — not as a barrier to treatment. The assumption that transference impedes therapy is inconsistent with psychodynamic theory in general and with BPT in particular. This is a important distinction: if an answer choice suggests that transference should be avoided or minimized in brief psychodynamic work, it is almost certainly wrong. Distinguishing BPT from other brief approaches (such as SFBT or MI) often hinges on this active engagement with transference and unconscious conflict within a focused, time-limited structure.

The selection criteria row in the table above deserves special attention, because it connects directly to one of the most important concepts related to brief therapy: client suitability. Not every person who walks through the clinic door is a good candidate for a 12-session psychodynamic treatment. The question of who benefits most from brief therapy — and who may need a longer-term approach — has practical and ethical dimensions that clinicians must weigh carefully.

Who Is Suitable for Brief Therapy?

Brief therapy tends to be most appropriate for clients who present with: (1) a circumscribed, clearly defined problem — something specific enough to serve as a focal point for treatment; (2) at least one prior experience of a meaningful relationship, suggesting the capacity to form a therapeutic alliance quickly; (3) adequate ego strength, meaning the ability to tolerate the emotional intensity of focused work without decompensating; (4) motivation for change, which fuels engagement in a treatment that moves fast; and (5) the ability to engage quickly with the therapist, since there is no luxury of a long warm-up period. Clients with chronic personality disorders, severe trauma histories, or active psychosis may require longer-term treatment, though brief interventions can be useful as part of a stepped-care model. In practice, remember that "capacity for alliance" and "circumscribed problem" are the two selection criteria most frequently highlighted across brief therapy models.

Focus: Key Concepts

  • The miracle question is strongly associated with SFBT and de Shazer/Berg -- know this pairing
  • MI's OARS (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries) and the concept of the righting reflex are clinically important
  • Know the five stages of change: Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance
  • IPT focuses on four problem areas: grief, role disputes, role transitions, interpersonal deficits
  • Distinguish SFBT's exception questions (exploring when the problem is absent) from MI's change talk (evoking the client's own arguments for change)
  • Scaling questions are used in SFBT to quantify progress and identify next steps
  • IPT assigns the sick role in the initial phase to legitimize the patient's need for treatment and reduce self-blame
  • MI was originally developed for substance use disorders but is now applied broadly
