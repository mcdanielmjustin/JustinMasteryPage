Death, Dying, and Grief

In the previous chapter, we explored how older adults navigate cognitive aging, construct wisdom, and deploy strategies of selection, optimization, and compensation to maintain well-being in the face of decline. But there is one reality that no strategy can ultimately compensate for: the inevitability of death. Every human life ends, and every person who lives long enough will confront the deaths of people they love. Understanding the psychology of death and dying is therefore not a peripheral clinical topic -- it is essential for clinicians who work with patients facing terminal illness, their families, and bereaved individuals of all ages. The questions this chapter addresses are among the most profound in all of psychology: How do people come to terms with their own mortality? How does grief unfold, and when does it become pathological? And how does a child's understanding of death differ from an adult's?

Kübler-Ross's Five Stages

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (1969) conducted pioneering interviews with terminally ill patients and proposed five emotional responses that individuals commonly experience when confronting their own death. Her work in On Death and Dying broke the silence around a topic that medicine and psychology had largely avoided, and for that reason alone it remains historically significant.

  1. Denial: "This can't be happening to me." An initial shock response that serves as a psychological buffer, giving the person time to absorb overwhelming news.
  2. Anger: "Why me? This isn't fair!" As denial fades, frustration and rage may be directed at doctors, family members, God, or the universe. The anger often reflects a deep sense of injustice.
  3. Bargaining: "If I could just have more time, I'll..." The person attempts to negotiate with a higher power, fate, or medical providers, often making promises in exchange for more time or a cure.
  4. Depression: Mourning impending losses -- loss of health, independence, future plans, and relationships. Kübler-Ross distinguished between reactive depression (response to specific losses already experienced) and preparatory depression (anticipatory grief over impending death).
  5. Acceptance: Coming to terms with the reality of death; not necessarily happy, but at peace. The person may become quiet, reflective, and focused on saying goodbye.
Important Clinical Caveat

Kübler-Ross's model, while widely known and clinically important, has been substantially criticized on empirical grounds. Contemporary research shows that not all individuals experience all stages, the stages do not necessarily occur in the described order, and people may move back and forth between stages or experience several simultaneously. Some dying persons never reach acceptance; others move directly from denial to acceptance without passing through the intermediate stages. The model is best understood as a description of common emotional reactions to dying rather than a prescriptive or universal progression. Clinicians should avoid rigidly applying this framework -- telling a patient that they "need to move past anger to reach acceptance" imposes a template on an experience that is deeply individual. Instead, attend to the person's unique emotional trajectory and provide support wherever they are.

The Dual Process Model of Bereavement

While Kübler-Ross focused on the dying person, Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut (1999) developed a more nuanced and empirically supported model for understanding how the bereaved cope with loss. Their Dual Process Model (DPM) proposes that healthy grieving involves a dynamic oscillation between two fundamentally different orientations:

Dual Process Model of Grief (Stroebe & Schut, 1999)

Loss-oriented coping involves confronting and processing the emotional pain of the loss -- crying, yearning for the deceased, reviewing memories, working through the meaning of the death. Restoration-oriented coping involves attending to the life changes that result from bereavement -- learning new skills the deceased used to handle (finances, cooking, household repairs), developing a new identity (from "wife" to "widow"), and engaging with the world in new ways. The key insight of the DPM is that healthy grief requires both orientations, and bereaved individuals naturally oscillate between them. A person who is exclusively loss-oriented -- unable to attend to daily life because they are consumed by grief -- may become overwhelmed. A person who is exclusively restoration-oriented -- throwing themselves into activity to avoid confronting the pain -- may be engaging in denial. The dynamic back-and-forth between the two is what constitutes adaptive coping.

Exam Example: Dual Process Model

Six months after her husband's death, a 68-year-old woman spends some mornings weeping over old photographs and letters (loss-oriented coping), but in the afternoon she takes a computer class to learn how to manage the household finances her husband always handled (restoration-oriented coping). According to Stroebe and Schut's Dual Process Model, this oscillation between confronting the emotional reality of loss and rebuilding practical life skills represents healthy, adaptive grieving. A clinician who understands this model would not pathologize either the tears or the practical activity but would recognize both as essential components of the grief process.

Prolonged Grief Disorder

While grief is a normal, universal human experience, a subset of bereaved individuals develop a pattern of grief that persists with debilitating intensity well beyond what is culturally expected. The recognition of this condition as a distinct clinical entity has been one of the most significant developments in bereavement research over the past two decades.

Prolonged Grief Disorder (DSM-5-TR)

Prolonged grief disorder, included as a new diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR (2022), is characterized by persistent, pervasive grief following the death of someone close, lasting at least 12 months in adults (6 months in children). Core features include intense yearning or longing for the deceased, preoccupation with the deceased or circumstances of the death, and marked difficulty accepting the loss. Additional symptoms may include emotional numbness, a sense that life is meaningless without the deceased, identity confusion ("a part of me died"), difficulty engaging in ongoing life, and avoidance of reminders of the loss. The condition affects approximately 7–10% of bereaved individuals and is distinct from both normal grief and major depressive disorder -- though comorbidity with MDD is common. Risk factors include sudden or violent death, loss of a child, insecure attachment style, and limited social support.

Clinical Note: Differential Diagnosis

Distinguishing prolonged grief disorder from major depressive disorder is a frequent EPPP topic. In prolonged grief, the dominant emotion is yearning for the deceased and the cognitive content centers on the lost person, while in MDD, the dominant emotions are pervasive sadness and anhedonia with broader cognitive themes of worthlessness, guilt, and hopelessness that extend beyond the loss. A bereaved person who says "I miss her every moment of every day and I cannot imagine going on without her" is expressing grief; one who says "I am worthless, nothing matters, and I would be better off dead regardless of the loss" is likely experiencing depression. The distinction matters because the treatments differ: complicated grief therapy (CGT) targets grief-specific processes, while MDD responds to standard antidepressant and psychotherapy approaches.

Developmental Understanding of Death

A child who loses a parent at age four, an adolescent whose friend dies in a car accident, and a middle-aged adult who watches a parent decline with cancer are all experiencing grief -- but they are not experiencing it in the same way. One of the most clinically important topics in death and dying psychology is the developmental progression in how humans understand what death means, because a person's cognitive capacity shapes how they process loss and what kind of support they need.

Children's Understanding of Death

Researchers have identified several sub-concepts of death that children must grasp before they achieve a mature understanding: irreversibility (the dead person cannot come back to life), universality (all living things eventually die), non-functionality (all bodily functions cease at death), and causality (understanding the biological mechanisms that cause death). These concepts are acquired gradually and largely follow Piagetian cognitive stages:

  • Preschool (ages 2–5): Children in the preoperational stage often view death as temporary and reversible -- similar to sleep or a long trip. They may expect the dead person to return and may ask concrete questions ("Does grandpa need a blanket in his coffin?"). Magical thinking may lead to guilt ("Did grandpa die because I was naughty?").
  • Early school age (ages 5–7): Children begin to understand irreversibility but may personify death as a monster, skeleton, or ghost. They may believe that death is something that happens to others (especially old people) but not to themselves or their parents.
  • Middle childhood (ages 7–10): With the acquisition of concrete operational thought, children grasp universality, irreversibility, and non-functionality. Their understanding becomes more realistic and biological. They can comprehend that death has physical causes and that all living things will eventually die, including themselves.
  • Adolescence: Formal operational thinking allows abstract reflection on the meaning of death, existential questions about mortality, and the capacity for anticipatory grief. However, the personal fable may lead adolescents to intellectually understand death while feeling subjectively invulnerable ("I know people die, but it won't happen to me").
Clinical Application: Talking to Children About Death

Clinicians who work with grieving children must calibrate their language and interventions to the child's developmental level. Euphemisms like "passed away," "gone to sleep," or "lost" can confuse young children and create anxiety (if grandma "went to sleep" and didn't wake up, what happens when I go to sleep?). Concrete, honest, age-appropriate language -- "Grandma's body stopped working and she died" -- is recommended. Young children may need to hear the explanation repeatedly, as their limited cognitive capacity means they may not fully absorb it the first time. Providing opportunities for expression through drawing, play, and storytelling can help children process grief that they cannot yet articulate verbally.

Cultural Variations in Death and Mourning

The psychology of death cannot be understood apart from its cultural context. Mourning practices vary dramatically across cultures, and what constitutes "normal" grief in one cultural framework may look pathological in another. In some cultures, emotional restraint during bereavement is valued and expected; in others, loud, public expressions of anguish are normative and even encouraged. Some traditions prescribe specific mourning periods (the Jewish practice of shiva, for example, involves seven days of intensive mourning followed by structured periods of gradually resuming normal activities), while others involve ongoing relationships with the deceased through ancestor veneration, shrine maintenance, or spiritual communication.

Clinicians must be culturally humble when assessing grief. A widow from a West African tradition who reports talking to her deceased husband and feeling his presence in the home is engaging in a culturally normative practice, not experiencing psychotic symptoms. An East Asian client who shows little outward emotion during a bereavement assessment may be displaying culturally expected emotional restraint, not pathological avoidance. The DSM-5-TR explicitly acknowledges cultural variation in grief expression, and the EPPP expects candidates to recognize that cultural context modifies both the expression and the assessment of bereavement.

Hospice Psychology and End-of-Life Care

The hospice movement, which Kübler-Ross helped inspire, has transformed end-of-life care by emphasizing comfort, dignity, and quality of life over curative treatment. Psychologists working in hospice settings address a range of concerns: helping patients cope with anticipatory grief and existential distress, facilitating communication between patients and their families about end-of-life wishes, managing pain and symptom-related anxiety, supporting family caregivers, and providing bereavement counseling after the death. The hospice model recognizes dying as a process that can be supported psychologically, not merely managed medically.

Exam Example: Developmental Understanding

A 4-year-old whose mother has died keeps asking when mommy is coming home, despite being told several times that mommy has died. This behavior does not indicate denial or a psychological disorder -- it reflects the preoperational child's cognitive limitation in grasping the irreversibility of death. The child may need concrete, repeated explanations and the opportunity to express confusion through play, drawing, or storytelling.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Kübler-Ross stages: Denial, Anger, Bargaining, Depression, Acceptance. Know the stages but also know the critique: not a fixed sequence, not universal, not all stages experienced by all individuals. Best understood as common reactions, not prescriptive stages.
  • Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut): Bereaved individuals oscillate between loss-oriented coping (processing grief) and restoration-oriented coping (rebuilding daily life). Healthy grieving requires both orientations; exclusive focus on either is maladaptive.
  • Prolonged Grief Disorder (DSM-5-TR): New diagnosis. Duration >12 months in adults, >6 months in children. Core features: intense yearning, preoccupation with deceased, difficulty accepting loss. Distinct from MDD (yearning vs. pervasive anhedonia/worthlessness). Affects ~7–10% of bereaved.
  • PGD vs. MDD: In prolonged grief, the dominant emotion is yearning centered on the deceased. In MDD, the dominant emotions are sadness and anhedonia with broader cognitive themes extending beyond the loss. Comorbidity is common but treatments differ.
  • Children's understanding of death: Develops through sub-concepts: irreversibility, universality, non-functionality, causality. Preschoolers view death as reversible; by ages 7–10, understanding is mature and biological. Avoid euphemisms with young children.
  • Adolescent understanding: Formal operational thought allows abstract reflection on mortality, but the personal fable may create a sense of subjective invulnerability.
  • Cultural variation: Mourning practices vary dramatically across cultures. Clinicians must assess grief within cultural context and avoid pathologizing culturally normative expressions of bereavement.
  • Hospice: Focuses on comfort, dignity, and quality of life. Psychologists address anticipatory grief, existential distress, family communication, and bereavement support.
