Introduction to Reliability

The previous chapter examined threats to research validity — internal, external, construct, and statistical conclusion validity — all of which concern the trustworthiness of research findings. Now we shift from evaluating studies to evaluating the measurement instruments themselves. Imagine a psychologist administering an intelligence test to a 10-year-old child on a Monday morning. The child scores 112. The following week, under virtually identical conditions — same room, same time of day, same level of alertness — the psychologist administers the test again. This time, the child scores 87. Should the psychologist trust either result? The 25-point swing raises a fundamental question that sits at the heart of all psychological measurement: How consistent are our test scores, and how much confidence can we place in any single measurement?

This question matters enormously in clinical practice. When a neuropsychologist uses test results to determine whether a patient has experienced cognitive decline, or when a forensic psychologist's assessment influences a custody decision, the stakes of inconsistent measurement are profound. A test that yields wildly different scores from one administration to the next is like a bathroom scale that reads 150 pounds one morning and 180 the next — it may occasionally land on the right number, but you'd never trust it to track meaningful change. This is why reliability — the consistency, stability, and dependability of test scores — is one of the most essential concepts in psychometrics, and a foundational topic on the EPPP.

A reliable test produces similar results under consistent conditions. If an examinee takes the same test twice under identical circumstances, a reliable test should yield similar scores. Critically, reliability is a prerequisite for validity: a test cannot measure a construct accurately if it produces inconsistent measurements (Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). Think of it this way — if your thermometer gives you a different reading every time you measure the temperature of boiling water, it doesn't matter whether it's "really" measuring temperature or air pressure or nothing at all. You first need consistency before you can even ask whether the instrument is measuring what it claims to measure.

Key Terminology

  • Reliability: The degree to which test scores are consistent, stable, and free from measurement error
  • Reliability Coefficient: A correlation coefficient indicating the proportion of score variance that is systematic (true score variance)
  • Measurement Error: Random fluctuations in scores due to factors unrelated to the construct being measured
  • True Score: A hypothetical score representing an examinee's actual level on the measured attribute

These four terms work together in an elegant framework. Every time someone takes a test, their observed score — the number actually recorded — is understood to be a combination of two components: a true score reflecting the person's actual standing on the attribute, and measurement error reflecting random noise from irrelevant factors. Perhaps the examinee was distracted by construction noise outside the window, misread a test item, or simply guessed correctly on a question they didn't truly know. These random fluctuations inflate or deflate the observed score, pulling it away from the true score in unpredictable directions.

The reliability coefficient captures this relationship quantitatively. A reliability coefficient of 1.00 would mean that every bit of variability in test scores across examinees reflects genuine differences in the trait being measured — a perfect, error-free test. A coefficient of 0.00 would mean that scores are pure noise, with no systematic signal at all. In practice, well-constructed psychological tests typically yield reliability coefficients between .80 and .95, meaning that the large majority of score differences among examinees reflect real differences in the attribute, with a smaller portion attributable to random error.

In Classical Test Theory (CTT), reliability is conceptualized as the proportion of observed score variance attributable to true score variance. Mathematically, the reliability coefficient (rxx) represents the ratio of true score variance to observed score variance (Lord & Novick, 1968). This deceptively simple ratio — true variance divided by total variance — forms the backbone of how psychologists evaluate measurement quality, and it gives rise to the several different methods of estimating reliability that we will explore throughout this chapter. But before we can fully appreciate those methods, we need to understand the formal assumptions of Classical Test Theory itself.

Classical Test Theory

In the preceding section, we introduced reliability as the proportion of observed score variance that reflects true score variance — a deceptively simple ratio that underpins how psychologists evaluate the quality of their measurements. But where does this ratio come from? What theoretical framework gives rise to the idea that every test score contains a "true" component and an "error" component? The answer lies in Classical Test Theory (CTT), a framework so foundational that virtually every reliability estimate you will encounter on the EPPP — test-retest, internal consistency, inter-rater — traces its logic back to this single model.

Consider a clinical psychologist who administers the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) to a client on a Monday morning. The client scores a 24, placing her in the "moderate depression" range. But imagine that same client, with the exact same level of depression, took the test under slightly different conditions — perhaps she had slept poorly the night before, misread one item, or guessed on a question she found ambiguous. Her score might shift to a 22 or a 26. The underlying depression hasn't changed, but the observed number on the page has. Classical Test Theory offers an elegant way to think about this phenomenon: every observed score is a composite of what the person truly possesses (the signal) and random noise introduced by the imperfections of measurement (the static).

More formally, Classical Test Theory provides the theoretical foundation for understanding reliability. The core assumption is that any observed score (X) consists of two components: a true score (T), representing the hypothetical score a person would obtain if measurement were perfect, and error (E), representing the random fluctuations that push the observed score above or below that true value. This partitioning of scores is not merely an abstract exercise — it is the engine that drives every reliability estimate in psychometrics.

Classical Test Theory Formula

X = T + E

Where:

  • X = Observed score (what we measure)
  • T = True score (the person's actual ability)
  • E = Error (random measurement error)

This formula is worth committing to memory not only for its simplicity but for the profound implications it carries. Because every observed score is "contaminated" by error, no single test administration can perfectly reveal a person's true score. The true score itself is a theoretical construct — defined as the average score a person would receive if they could take the same test an infinite number of times under identical conditions, with no memory of previous administrations. We never directly observe T; we only ever observe X. The goal of reliability analysis, then, is to estimate how much of the variability in observed scores across people reflects genuine differences in their true scores versus random noise.

Assumptions of Classical Test Theory

Like any mathematical model, CTT rests on a set of assumptions that make its equations work. These assumptions are idealized — they may not hold perfectly in practice — but they provide the logical scaffolding for deriving reliability coefficients and standard errors of measurement. Understanding these assumptions is critical because violations can distort reliability estimates and mislead clinical decision-making.

  • The mean error of measurement equals zero across repeated testing
  • True scores and error scores are uncorrelated
  • Error scores on one test are uncorrelated with error scores on another test
  • Error scores are uncorrelated with true scores on other tests

The first assumption — that errors average to zero over many administrations — is what makes the true score interpretable as a long-run average. Sometimes error pushes a score up; sometimes it pushes it down. Over an infinite number of testings, these random fluctuations cancel out, leaving the true score. The second assumption states that error is truly random with respect to the trait being measured: a person with a higher true score on intelligence does not systematically experience more (or less) measurement error than a person with a lower true score. The third and fourth assumptions extend this logic across tests, ensuring that the error on one measure is not systematically related to the error — or the true scores — on another measure. Together, these assumptions ensure that error behaves as pure noise, uncorrelated with anything meaningful, which is precisely what allows us to isolate the true score signal from the error static.

Reliability as True Score Variance

With the CTT framework in place, we can now express reliability in its most precise form. When we administer a test to a group of people, we observe a distribution of scores. Some of the variability in that distribution reflects genuine differences in what people truly possess (true score variance), and some reflects the random noise of measurement (error variance). The reliability coefficient captures the proportion of total observed variability that is attributable to true differences among individuals, rather than to measurement error.

rxx = σ²T / σ²X = 1 - (σ²E / σ²X)

This dual expression is worth studying carefully. The left side of the equation says: reliability equals the ratio of true score variance to observed score variance. The right side provides the complementary perspective: reliability equals one minus the proportion of variance due to error. Both expressions yield the same number, but they offer different intuitive handles on the concept. A reliability coefficient of .80, for example, means that 80% of the observed score variance reflects true differences among test-takers, while the remaining 20% is attributable to error variance. A test with a reliability of .95 is transmitting mostly signal; a test with a reliability of .50 is transmitting as much noise as signal — a sobering thought when that test is being used to make diagnostic or placement decisions.

Notice what this means in practice: reliability is not a fixed property of a test itself but rather a property of the scores obtained from a particular sample under particular conditions. The same test can yield highly reliable scores in one population (where true score variance is large) and much less reliable scores in another (where the range of true scores is restricted). This insight — that reliability is bound to sample and context — is one of the most important takeaways from Classical Test Theory and one that the EPPP frequently tests. As we move to the next section on Item Response Theory, we will see how a different framework addresses some of CTT's limitations by modeling reliability at the level of individual items and individual trait levels rather than relying on a single summary coefficient for the entire test.

Item Response Theory (IRT)

While Classical Test Theory operates at the test level, Item Response Theory (IRT) operates at the item level, modeling the probability that a person with a given ability level will respond correctly to a specific item. IRT addresses several limitations of CTT by providing item-level analysis and more flexible measurement properties (Embretson & Reise, 2000).

CTT vs. IRT: Key Contrasts

  • Unit of analysis: CTT analyzes total test scores; IRT analyzes individual items
  • Sample dependence: CTT item statistics (difficulty, discrimination) depend on the sample tested; IRT item parameters are sample-independent (invariant) once the model fits
  • Test dependence: CTT ability estimates depend on which test was given; IRT ability estimates are test-independent within a calibrated item bank
  • Measurement precision: CTT assumes equal SEM for all examinees; IRT provides a unique standard error for each ability level

Item Parameters

IRT models characterize items using up to three parameters:

  • Difficulty (b): The ability level at which an examinee has a 50% probability of answering correctly. Higher b values indicate more difficult items. On the ability (theta) scale, b typically ranges from -3 to +3.
  • Discrimination (a): How well the item differentiates between examinees of different ability levels. Higher a values indicate that the item sharply distinguishes between those just above and just below the difficulty level. Graphically, this is the steepness of the item characteristic curve at the inflection point.
  • Guessing (c): The probability that an examinee with very low ability will answer correctly (also called the pseudo-guessing parameter). For a four-option multiple-choice item, c is often around .25 but may be lower if distractors are effective.

In addition to the IRT-based discrimination parameter (a), Classical Test Theory offers the item discrimination index (D), a straightforward measure of how effectively an item separates high-performing examinees from low-performing ones. To compute D, the total sample is typically divided into upper and lower groups based on overall test scores (often the top 27% and bottom 27%). The proportion of correct responses in the lower group is then subtracted from the proportion correct in the upper group.

Item Discrimination Index (D)

D = Pupper − Plower
Where Pupper is the proportion of high scorers answering correctly and Plower is the proportion of low scorers answering correctly. D ranges from −1.0 to +1.0.

A positive D value indicates that high-ability examinees are more likely to answer the item correctly than low-ability examinees, which is the desired outcome. Values of D ≥ .40 are generally considered excellent discriminators, values between .20 and .39 are acceptable, and values below .20 suggest the item does poorly at distinguishing between ability levels. A D value near zero indicates the item fails to differentiate between high and low scorers at all. Negative D values are problematic—they indicate that low-scoring examinees outperform high-scoring examinees on that item, which may signal a flawed or ambiguous item that should be revised or removed.

Example

On a 100-item exam, 90% of the top-scoring group answers Item 12 correctly, while only 30% of the bottom-scoring group does so. The discrimination index is D = .90 − .30 = .60, indicating strong discrimination. By contrast, if 50% of each group answers Item 23 correctly, D = .50 − .50 = .00, meaning the item provides no useful differentiation between high and low performers.

Item Characteristic Curves (ICCs)

The Item Characteristic Curve (ICC) is an S-shaped (logistic) curve that plots the probability of a correct response (y-axis) as a function of examinee ability, denoted theta (x-axis). Each item has its own ICC. The curve's position along the theta axis reflects difficulty (b), its steepness reflects discrimination (a), and its lower asymptote reflects guessing (c).

Reading an ICC

An item with b = 0.0 is of average difficulty (50% correct at average ability). If a = 1.5, the curve is moderately steep, meaning the item differentiates well. If c = 0.20, even very low-ability examinees have a 20% chance of guessing correctly, so the curve never drops below 0.20 on the y-axis.

IRT Models

Model Parameters Key Features
1PL (Rasch Model) Difficulty (b) only All items assumed equally discriminating; no guessing. Simplest model; emphasizes measurement properties and specific objectivity
2PL Difficulty (b) + Discrimination (a) Allows items to vary in discrimination. ICCs can differ in steepness. More realistic for many tests
3PL Difficulty (b) + Discrimination (a) + Guessing (c) Accounts for correct guessing on multiple-choice items. Lower asymptote of ICC is above zero. Most commonly used for standardized multiple-choice tests

Advantages of IRT

  • Item parameter invariance: Item parameters remain stable across different examinee samples (provided the model fits), enabling items to be calibrated once and used across contexts
  • Ability estimate invariance: Examinee ability estimates are comparable regardless of which specific items were administered, as long as items come from a calibrated bank
  • Conditional standard error: IRT provides unique precision estimates at each ability level, rather than a single SEM for all examinees
  • Flexible test design: Items can be selected to maximize information (precision) at specific ability levels

Applications of IRT

Key Applications

  • Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT): IRT enables tests that dynamically select items based on the examinee's estimated ability. After each response, the ability estimate is updated and the next item is chosen to provide maximum information at that ability level. CATs are typically shorter yet more precise than fixed-length tests.
  • Test equating: IRT allows scores from different test forms to be placed on a common scale, ensuring fairness when different examinees take different versions of a test
  • Differential Item Functioning (DIF): IRT can identify items that function differently across demographic groups (e.g., gender, ethnicity) after controlling for ability, which is essential for detecting item-level bias
  • Item banking: Calibrated items can be stored in a bank and assembled into multiple test forms with known psychometric properties

IRT and the EPPP

The EPPP itself uses IRT-based scoring. Items are calibrated using IRT models, and examinee scores are estimated on a theta scale. This means the EPPP can draw from a large item bank while maintaining score comparability across administrations. The pass/fail decision is based on a theta cutoff rather than a raw percentage correct, which accounts for differences in item difficulty across test forms.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • The CTT formula X = T + E states that every observed score equals the true score plus random error — commit this to memory
  • Reliability equals the proportion of observed score variance attributable to true score variance: rxx = sigma-squared-T / sigma-squared-X
  • Reliability is a property of scores from a specific sample, not a fixed property of the test itself — the same test can have different reliabilities in different populations
  • Reliability is necessary but not sufficient for validity — a test can be reliable without being valid, but it cannot be valid without being reliable
  • IRT vs. CTT: CTT provides a single SEM for all examinees; IRT provides a unique standard error at each ability level (conditional standard error)
  • Item parameter invariance is a key advantage of IRT — item difficulty and discrimination remain stable across different samples when the model fits
  • The 3PL IRT model includes difficulty (b), discrimination (a), and guessing (c) parameters — it is most commonly used for standardized multiple-choice tests
  • The item discrimination index (D) in CTT is computed as P-upper minus P-lower; values of .40+ indicate excellent discrimination
  • Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) is enabled by IRT — items are dynamically selected to maximize information at the examinee's estimated ability level
  • The EPPP itself uses IRT-based scoring — pass/fail decisions use a theta cutoff, not a raw percentage correct
