Parkinson's Disease

A 63-year-old retired engineer notices that his right hand trembles slightly when resting on his lap during dinner conversations, though the tremor vanishes the moment he reaches for his water glass. His wife has observed that he walks with shorter, shuffling steps and that his face has become oddly expressionless — "like a mask," she says — even when he reports feeling happy or amused. Over the next year, buttoning his shirt becomes a frustrating ordeal, and he finds himself frozen at doorways, unable to initiate the first step. These motor symptoms — the resting tremor that disappears with voluntary movement, the rigidity, the progressive slowness, and the freezing episodes — form the clinical signature of the most common neurodegenerative movement disorder, and understanding its neurobiological basis connects directly to some of the most important material in practice.

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which projects to the striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen) via the nigrostriatal pathway. The result is a dramatic reduction in striatal dopamine, producing the characteristic motor symptoms.

Cardinal Motor Features (TRAP):

Tremor (resting tremor, "pill-rolling," 4-6 Hz, decreases with voluntary movement); Rigidity (cogwheel or lead-pipe resistance to passive movement); Akinesia/Bradykinesia (slowness of movement, difficulty initiating movement, reduced facial expression [masked facies]); Postural instability (impaired balance, tendency to fall). The tremor is typically asymmetric at onset and worsens with stress.

Non-motor features include depression (affects ~40% of PD patients), cognitive impairment progressing to dementia in ~30-40% of cases, REM sleep behavior disorder, anosmia (loss of smell), autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation), and psychosis (visual hallucinations, often medication-related).

Treatment:

Levodopa (L-DOPA) is the gold-standard treatment — it is a dopamine precursor that crosses the blood-brain barrier and is converted to dopamine. It is typically combined with carbidopa (which prevents peripheral conversion) to reduce side effects. Long-term levodopa use can produce motor fluctuations ("wearing off," "on-off" phenomena) and dyskinesias (involuntary choreiform movements). Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole), MAO-B inhibitors, and deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus are additional treatment options.

Huntington's Disease

Huntington's disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat on chromosome 4, producing an abnormal huntingtin protein. The disease is 100% penetrant — anyone who inherits the gene will develop the disease if they live long enough. Typical onset is between ages 30 and 50.

Pathology & Clinical Features:

HD primarily affects the caudate nucleus and putamen (striatum), producing progressive atrophy. The characteristic movement disorder is chorea — involuntary, irregular, dance-like movements affecting the limbs, trunk, and face. The clinical triad includes: (1) choreiform movements, (2) progressive cognitive decline (subcortical dementia pattern with executive dysfunction, slowed processing), and (3) psychiatric disturbances (depression, irritability, personality changes, psychosis). Psychiatric symptoms often precede motor symptoms by years.

EPPP Distinction — Parkinson's vs. Huntington's:

Parkinson's involves too little dopamine (substantia nigra degeneration) and produces hypokinetic movements (slow, reduced). Huntington's involves loss of inhibitory neurons in the striatum and produces hyperkinetic movements (excessive, involuntary chorea). Parkinson's is typically sporadic; Huntington's is autosomal dominant. Both can produce dementia, but the cognitive profiles differ: PD dementia is more posterior-cortical; HD dementia is subcortical-frontal.

Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system in which the immune system attacks oligodendrocytes and the myelin sheath. This produces plaques (lesions) of demyelination scattered throughout the brain and spinal cord, disrupting saltatory conduction and producing a wide range of neurological symptoms.

Common symptoms include optic neuritis (inflammation of the optic nerve causing vision loss), sensory disturbances (numbness, tingling, Lhermitte's sign), motor weakness, spasticity, cerebellar ataxia, bladder dysfunction, fatigue, and cognitive impairment (particularly in processing speed, working memory, and executive function). Symptoms often worsen with heat (Uhthoff's phenomenon).

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis & Other Conditions

ALS (Lou Gehrig's Disease)

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive motor neuron disease affecting both upper and lower motor neurons. It produces progressive muscle weakness, atrophy, fasciculations (muscle twitching), spasticity, and ultimately respiratory failure. Critically, ALS typically spares cognitive function, sensation, and eye movements until very late stages. Approximately 5-10% of cases are familial (genetic); the remainder are sporadic. Average survival is 3-5 years from symptom onset.

Wilson's Disease

Wilson's disease is an autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism causing toxic copper accumulation in the liver, brain (particularly the basal ganglia), and cornea. Neurological symptoms include tremor, dystonia, dysarthria, and psychiatric disturbances (personality changes, depression, psychosis). The pathognomonic sign is Kayser-Fleischer rings — golden-brown copper deposits visible in the cornea. It is treatable with copper chelation therapy (penicillamine) and zinc supplementation if diagnosed early.

Myasthenia Gravis

Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder in which antibodies attack acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, causing progressive muscle weakness that worsens with activity and improves with rest. Characteristic features include ptosis (drooping eyelids), diplopia (double vision), difficulty swallowing, and limb weakness. Treatment includes cholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine), immunosuppressants, and thymectomy.

Cerebellar Disorders:

The cerebellum coordinates voluntary movement, balance, and motor learning. Cerebellar damage produces a characteristic syndrome: ataxia (uncoordinated, staggering gait), dysmetria (inability to accurately reach for objects — overshooting or undershooting), intention tremor (worsens as the hand approaches the target — opposite of Parkinson's resting tremor), dysarthria (slurred speech), and nystagmus (involuntary eye movements). Cerebellar signs are ipsilateral to the lesion (unlike cerebral cortex, which is contralateral).

  • Huntington's disease: autosomal dominant; CAG trinucleotide repeat on chromosome 4; caudate nucleus atrophy; chorea; 100% penetrance

Stroke: Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic

Imagine a 62-year-old man who suddenly cannot move his right arm, struggles to form words, and feels the right side of his face go slack. His wife calls 911, and the ER team races against the clock — because every minute that passes without treatment, nearly two million neurons die. This is the clinical reality of stroke, and understanding its types, mechanisms, and lateralized consequences is essential not only in clinical practice but for any clinician who will encounter patients navigating the aftermath of cerebrovascular events.

A stroke (cerebrovascular accident, CVA) occurs when blood flow to the brain is disrupted, causing neuronal death. Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability and the fifth leading cause of death in the United States. Despite these sobering statistics, outcomes hinge dramatically on the type of stroke involved and how quickly treatment begins — which is why the distinction between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke is one of the most clinically consequential classifications in all of medicine.

The two major categories differ fundamentally in their mechanism. In an ischemic stroke, a blood vessel becomes blocked, starving downstream brain tissue of oxygen and glucose. In a hemorrhagic stroke, a blood vessel ruptures, flooding surrounding tissue with blood and creating pressure that damages neurons. Think of it this way: one is a plumbing blockage, the other is a burst pipe. Both are emergencies, but they demand very different interventions — administering a clot-dissolving drug to someone whose vessel has ruptured, for instance, would be catastrophic.

TypeMechanismPrevalenceKey Features
Ischemic strokeBlockage of a cerebral artery (thrombus or embolus) cutting off blood supply~87% of all strokesThrombotic (clot forms in situ) or embolic (clot travels from elsewhere); treated with tPA within 4.5 hours
Hemorrhagic strokeRupture of a cerebral blood vessel causing bleeding into or around the brain~13% of all strokesIntracerebral hemorrhage or subarachnoid hemorrhage; higher mortality; often associated with hypertension or aneurysm

As the table makes clear, ischemic strokes are far more common, accounting for roughly 87% of all cases. Within this category, a thrombotic stroke forms when a clot develops at the site of an already-narrowed artery — often due to atherosclerosis — while an embolic stroke occurs when a clot forms elsewhere (frequently in the heart, as in atrial fibrillation) and travels to lodge in a cerebral artery. The critical treatment for ischemic stroke is tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), a clot-dissolving medication that must be administered within 4.5 hours of symptom onset. This narrow time window explains why public health campaigns emphasize recognizing stroke symptoms immediately — the phrase "time is brain" is not hyperbole.

Hemorrhagic strokes, while less common at roughly 13% of cases, carry substantially higher mortality. They occur in two forms: intracerebral hemorrhage, in which bleeding occurs directly within brain tissue, and subarachnoid hemorrhage, in which blood spills into the space surrounding the brain. Chronic hypertension is the leading risk factor for intracerebral hemorrhage, while subarachnoid hemorrhage is frequently caused by the rupture of a cerebral aneurysm — a weakened, balloon-like outpouching in an arterial wall. Patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage classically describe "the worst headache of my life," a presentation that should immediately raise clinical suspicion.

In practice, one of the most key topics in cerebrovascular disease concerns the specific deficits that arise depending on which artery is affected — and in which hemisphere. The middle cerebral artery (MCA) deserves special attention because it supplies the vast lateral surface of each hemisphere, including critical motor, sensory, and language areas.

Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) Syndrome:

The MCA is the most commonly affected artery in ischemic stroke, and its lateralized deficit profiles are among the most exam-relevant patterns in biopsychology. A left MCA stroke produces contralateral (right-sided) hemiparesis and sensory loss, with the face and arm typically more affected than the leg. Because the left hemisphere is dominant for language in most individuals, left MCA strokes also produce aphasia — Broca's aphasia if anterior branches are involved (with nonfluent, effortful speech but relatively preserved comprehension), Wernicke's aphasia if posterior branches are affected (with fluent but meaningless speech and impaired comprehension), or global aphasia if damage is extensive. A right MCA stroke also produces contralateral (left-sided) hemiparesis but is characterized by a strikingly different cognitive profile: left hemispatial neglect (the patient behaves as though the left side of space does not exist), anosognosia (a lack of awareness of one's own deficits — the patient may insist nothing is wrong even while unable to move the left side of the body), and constructional apraxia (difficulty with spatial tasks such as drawing or assembling objects). The EPPP frequently tests the lateralized deficits associated with MCA stroke, so anchoring these patterns to their respective hemispheres is essential.

A helpful exam mnemonic: left MCA stroke disrupts language; right MCA stroke disrupts spatial awareness and self-monitoring. Consider two patients sitting side by side in a rehabilitation unit — one struggles to find words but is painfully aware of the deficit and becomes frustrated, while the other speaks fluently but ignores the left side of the lunch tray and cheerfully insists there is nothing wrong. The first likely had a left MCA stroke; the second, a right MCA stroke. These contrasting presentations illustrate how lateralized brain organization shapes the clinical picture in profound ways.

A transient ischemic attack (TIA) produces temporary stroke-like symptoms — such as sudden weakness, slurred speech, or visual disturbance — that resolve completely within 24 hours, and typically within just minutes. Although symptoms are transient, a TIA is emphatically not a benign event. It is a major warning sign: approximately 10–15% of individuals who experience a TIA will go on to have a full stroke within 90 days, with the highest risk concentrated in the first 48 hours. For this reason, TIAs require urgent evaluation, including imaging and assessment of vascular risk factors. Clinicians encountering patients who report "a spell" involving sudden neurological symptoms that resolved on their own should treat this history with the same seriousness as a completed stroke — it may represent the brain's last warning before a catastrophic event.

As we turn next to traumatic brain injury, we shift from vascular disruption to mechanical force as the source of neuronal damage — but many of the same principles apply: the location and extent of the insult determine the pattern of deficits, and understanding neuroanatomy is the key to making sense of the clinical presentation.

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

Traumatic brain injury results from an external mechanical force applied to the head. TBIs are classified as mild (concussion), moderate, or severe, based on the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, duration of loss of consciousness, and duration of post-traumatic amnesia.

Coup-Contrecoup Injury:

A coup injury occurs at the site of impact, while a contrecoup injury occurs at the opposite side of the brain (due to the brain rebounding against the skull). In falls, the frontal and temporal lobes are particularly vulnerable because they contact the rough bony surfaces of the anterior and middle cranial fossae. This explains why personality changes and memory impairment are common after TBI even when the primary impact is elsewhere.

Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) results from rotational acceleration-deceleration forces that shear axons throughout the brain. DAI is the most common cause of prolonged coma and persistent vegetative state following TBI. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) assesses eye opening (1-4), verbal response (1-5), and motor response (1-6), yielding scores from 3 (deep coma) to 15 (fully alert). GCS scores classify TBI as mild (13-15), moderate (9-12), or severe (3-8).

Post-Concussion Syndrome & CTE:

Post-concussion syndrome involves persistent symptoms following mild TBI: headache, dizziness, fatigue, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and sleep disturbance, typically resolving within weeks to months but sometimes persisting longer. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease associated with repeated head impacts (common in contact sports and military personnel). CTE is characterized by accumulation of tau protein, producing mood disturbance, impulsivity, cognitive decline, and eventually dementia. Definitive diagnosis currently requires post-mortem examination.

Epilepsy & Seizure Disorders

Having explored how traumatic brain injury disrupts normal brain function through external forces, we now turn to a condition in which the brain's own electrical activity becomes the source of dysfunction. Imagine a child sitting in a third-grade classroom who suddenly stops mid-sentence, stares blankly for ten seconds, then resumes speaking as if nothing happened — unaware that anything occurred at all. Her teacher assumes she's daydreaming, but a neurologist would recognize this brief lapse as an absence seizure. Across the spectrum, consider a young adult who collapses without warning, body stiffening and then convulsing rhythmically for two minutes, waking afterward in a fog of confusion and exhaustion. Both of these individuals share the same underlying condition — one that affects approximately 1 in 26 people over their lifetime and carries profound implications for psychological functioning, daily safety, and quality of life.

Epilepsy is a neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, unprovoked seizures resulting from abnormal, excessive, or synchronous neuronal activity. The word "unprovoked" is clinically important here — a single seizure triggered by alcohol withdrawal, a high fever in a toddler, or acute metabolic disturbance does not constitute epilepsy. The diagnosis requires either two or more unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure combined with a high probability of recurrence based on EEG findings or structural brain abnormalities. Understanding the distinction between seizure types is essential for clinicians because the type of seizure determines the behavioral presentation, the likely brain region involved, the appropriate pharmacological treatment, and the cognitive and emotional consequences a patient may experience.

Seizures are broadly classified into two major categories based on where the abnormal electrical activity begins. Generalized seizures involve both cerebral hemispheres from onset, which is why they typically produce loss of consciousness — the disruption is bilateral and widespread from the very first moment. Focal seizures (historically called partial seizures) begin in a specific, identifiable brain region, and their symptoms are a direct reflection of the function that region normally serves. A focal seizure originating in the motor cortex may produce involuntary jerking of the contralateral hand; one beginning in the temporal lobe may trigger a wave of déjà vu, an inexplicable surge of fear, or repetitive lip-smacking movements called automatisms. This localization principle makes focal seizures clinically fascinating — they essentially reveal the brain's functional architecture through pathology.

Several features of this classification deserve closer attention for clinical psychologists. The postictal state following a generalized tonic-clonic seizure — that period of confusion, fatigue, and sometimes agitation that follows the convulsion — can last minutes to hours and may be mistaken for psychiatric disturbance if the seizure itself was unwitnessed. Patients may wander, appear disoriented, or become combative during the postictal period, leading to emergency department presentations that initially suggest psychosis or intoxication rather than a neurological event. Clinicians who understand this phenomenology can avoid costly misdiagnosis.

Absence seizures merit particular attention because of their subtlety and their prevalence in pediatric populations. A child experiencing dozens of absence seizures per day may appear inattentive, unfocused, or academically underperforming — a presentation that overlaps substantially with ADHD. The distinguishing features are important: absence seizures produce abrupt onset and offset of the blank stare (unlike the gradual drift of true daydreaming), cannot be interrupted by calling the child's name, and produce the hallmark 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern on electroencephalography (EEG). Psychologists involved in school-based assessment should keep this differential diagnosis in mind whenever attentional difficulties appear sudden in onset or episodic in nature.

Temporal lobe epilepsy — the most common form of focal epilepsy in adults — deserves special emphasis because of its rich psychiatric and psychological comorbidities. Patients with temporal lobe seizure foci frequently experience interictal (between-seizure) personality changes, depression, anxiety, and psychosis-like symptoms. The emotional and experiential auras that precede temporal lobe seizures — intense déjà vu, sudden fear, epigastric rising sensations, or even mystical experiences — illustrate how seizure activity in the limbic system can produce compelling subjective states that patients may interpret in psychological or spiritual terms. This intersection of neurology and psychology makes epilepsy one of the most important conditions for psychologists to understand thoroughly, particularly when conducting differential diagnosis in clinical settings.

Status epilepticus represents the most dangerous end of the seizure spectrum and constitutes a true medical emergency. When seizure activity persists continuously for more than five minutes, or when repeated seizures occur without the patient regaining consciousness between episodes, the sustained excitotoxic activity can produce permanent neuronal damage — particularly in the hippocampus — and can be fatal if untreated. First-line emergency treatment involves intravenous benzodiazepines (such as lorazepam or diazepam), which enhance GABAergic inhibition to terminate the seizure cascade. In practice, remembering that status epilepticus requires emergent pharmacological intervention — and that untreated status epilepticus can cause lasting cognitive impairment or death — is essential.

Hemispatial Neglect:

Hemispatial neglect (typically left-sided) results from right parietal lobe damage and involves a failure to attend to, report, or orient toward stimuli in the contralesional (left) space. Patients may eat food from only the right side of their plate, dress only the right side of their body, or draw only the right half of a clock. This is not a sensory deficit but an attentional one — the visual system can detect the stimuli, but the attentional system fails to process them.

  • TIA: temporary stroke symptoms resolving within 24 hours; major stroke warning sign
  • Coup-contrecoup: injury at impact site AND opposite side; frontal/temporal lobes most vulnerable
  • Glasgow Coma Scale: eye + verbal + motor (3-15); mild TBI = 13-15; severe = 3-8
  • CTE: progressive tauopathy from repeated head impacts; mood/cognitive changes; definitive diagnosis is post-mortem
  • Absence seizures: brief consciousness lapses; 3 Hz spike-and-wave EEG; common in children
  • Status epilepticus: seizure >5 min; medical emergency; treat with IV benzodiazepines
  • Broca's aphasia: nonfluent speech, intact comprehension; Wernicke's: fluent word salad, impaired comprehension
