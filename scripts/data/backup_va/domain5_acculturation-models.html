Acculturation Models

Imagine a family that has just emigrated from Guatemala to Chicago. The parents speak only Spanish and continue preparing traditional meals, celebrating familiar holidays, and relying on a close-knit community of other Guatemalan families. Their teenage daughter, meanwhile, quickly picks up English at school, starts listening to American pop music, and feels torn between her parents' expectations and her desire to fit in with classmates. At dinner, two cultural worlds sit across the table from each other — and the psychological negotiation happening inside each family member is anything but simple. Understanding how people navigate life between cultures is one of the most clinically consequential topics in cross-cultural psychology, and it builds directly on the cultural-level value dimensions we explored in the previous section. While Hofstede's framework helps us characterize entire societies, acculturation models zoom in to the individual level — examining the process of cultural and psychological change that results from continuous, firsthand contact between two distinct cultural groups.

A crucial feature of acculturation is that it is bidirectional: in principle, both the immigrant group and the host culture may change as a result of sustained contact. In practice, however, the minority or immigrant group typically undergoes far greater change, simply because the dominant culture's institutions — schools, workplaces, legal systems, media — exert powerful socializing pressure. Acculturation is not a single event but an ongoing, multidimensional process that unfolds across language use, social relationships, values, identity, and daily habits. Two siblings in the same family may acculturate at very different rates, which is itself a common source of family conflict that clinicians encounter regularly.

Berry's Acculturation Model

John Berry proposed the most widely cited acculturation framework, based on two independent questions: (1) Is it valuable to maintain one's heritage culture? (2) Is it valuable to develop relationships with the larger society? By treating these as separate yes-or-no dimensions rather than opposite ends of a single continuum, Berry captured a critical insight: embracing one culture does not require abandoning the other. The combination of answers to these two questions produces four distinct acculturation strategies, each carrying different implications for psychological well-being and social functioning.

StrategyHeritage Culture?Host Culture?Description
IntegrationYesYesMaintain heritage culture while also engaging with host culture; generally associated with best psychological outcomes
AssimilationNoYesAdopt host culture at the expense of heritage culture
SeparationYesNoMaintain heritage culture while avoiding engagement with host culture
MarginalizationNoNoReject both heritage and host cultures; associated with poorest psychological outcomes

To bring these strategies to life, return to that Guatemalan family in Chicago. The mother who learns English, secures a job at a local business, and joins a neighborhood association — while also maintaining her Spanish, cooking traditional foods, and attending a Spanish-language church — is pursuing integration. Her brother, who arrived at the same time but socializes exclusively within the Guatemalan community, shops only at Latino markets, and avoids English-language media, is leaning toward separation. The teenage daughter who drops her Spanish, anglicizes her name, and distances herself from family traditions to blend in with peers is moving toward assimilation. And a cousin who arrived involuntarily, feels alienated from both the Guatemalan community (which he associates with painful memories) and American culture (which he experiences as hostile), may drift into marginalization — the strategy consistently linked to the worst psychological outcomes.

One key exam-relevant nuance: Berry's model emphasizes that these strategies are not purely individual choices. The host society's attitudes matter enormously. Integration is only possible when the dominant culture is open to diversity and willing to accommodate cultural differences. In societies that demand conformity, the realistic options may narrow to assimilation or separation — a point with direct implications for how clinicians understand the constraints their clients face.

Clinical Application

Research consistently shows that integration (biculturalism) is associated with the best mental health outcomes, including lower depression, higher self-esteem, and better social functioning. Marginalization is associated with the worst outcomes. When assessing clients from immigrant backgrounds, understanding their acculturation strategy provides critical clinical information. For example, a first-generation college student who presents with anxiety and identity confusion may be navigating the tension between her family's separationist stance and the assimilationist pressure of a predominantly White campus. Asking about language use at home versus school, social networks, cultural practices maintained or abandoned, and the client's feelings about each domain can quickly illuminate which acculturation strategy — or which painful conflict between strategies — is operating. Culturally competent treatment planning hinges on this assessment.

Even when acculturation proceeds relatively smoothly, the process itself generates strain. Acculturative stress refers to the psychological burden that arises specifically from the demands of navigating between cultures. It can manifest as anxiety, depression, identity confusion, somatic complaints such as chronic headaches or gastrointestinal distress, and increased substance use. Think of the college student who feels a knot in her stomach every time she code-switches between the formal English expected in class and the dialect spoken at home — that tension, compounded daily across dozens of contexts, is the lived experience of acculturative stress.

Several well-documented risk factors amplify acculturative stress: language barriers that create isolation and limit access to services; experiences of discrimination and microaggressions in the host culture; loss of the social support networks that sustained the person in their country of origin; and involuntary migration, including refugee and asylum-seeking status, which layers trauma onto the acculturation process. Protective factors, conversely, include strong ethnic community ties, bilingual proficiency, family cohesion, and access to culturally responsive mental health services. For the EPPP, remember that acculturative stress is a normative response to an inherently demanding transition — it is not a sign of individual pathology, but rather a predictable product of systemic and cultural forces acting on the person.

Berry, J. W. (2005). Acculturation: Living successfully in two cultures. International Journal of Intercultural Relations, 29(6), 697–712.

Ethnic Identity Development

In the preceding section, we explored how individuals navigate the meeting point of two cultures — their heritage culture and the dominant culture of the society in which they live. Berry's acculturation model gave us a framework for understanding the strategies people adopt when these cultural worlds intersect. But acculturation tells only part of the story. Alongside the question of how a person relates to the broader culture is a deeply personal question: Who am I, as a member of my ethnic group? This is the domain of ethnic identity development — and it matters enormously for psychological well-being.

Consider a Latina teenager growing up in a predominantly White suburb. She celebrates Día de los Muertos at home but rarely talks about it at school. When a classmate asks, "Why does your family have a shrine with skulls on it?", she feels a sudden jolt — a mix of pride, embarrassment, and uncertainty. That moment, and the inner exploration it sparks, is exactly the kind of experience that drives ethnic identity development. Ethnic identity refers to a person's subjective sense of belonging to an ethnic group, including the feelings, attitudes, and knowledge associated with that membership. Crucially, it is a developmental process, not a fixed trait — something that unfolds over time through experiences, reflection, and commitment.

Phinney's Model of Ethnic Identity Development

Just as Erik Erikson proposed that personal identity develops through stages of exploration and commitment, psychologist Jean Phinney recognized that ethnic identity follows a similar developmental arc. Drawing on Erikson's identity theory and Marcia's identity statuses (diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium, and achievement), Phinney proposed a three-stage model of ethnic identity development designed to apply across all ethnic groups — not just one particular community. This universality was a deliberate strength of her framework, making it one of the most widely used models in the field.

Phinney's Stages

1. Unexamined Ethnic Identity (Diffusion/Foreclosure): The individual has not explored their ethnic identity. They may hold internalized stereotypes or show little interest in ethnicity. Like Marcia's diffusion and foreclosure statuses, the person either hasn't considered the question or has absorbed others' views without personal reflection.
2. Ethnic Identity Search (Moratorium): Triggered by a significant experience (e.g., encountering discrimination), the individual actively explores the meaning and implications of their ethnic group membership. This is a period of questioning, seeking, and sometimes emotional upheaval — much like Marcia's moratorium status.
3. Achieved Ethnic Identity: The individual has a secure, committed sense of their ethnicity. This does not require in-group bias — it reflects a confident, internalized understanding of what ethnic membership means to them. The person can hold their ethnic identity with pride while still appreciating other groups.

Notice the parallel to Marcia's identity statuses: the first stage maps onto diffusion and foreclosure, the second onto moratorium, and the third onto identity achievement. This theoretical grounding is worth remembering for the EPPP, as test items may ask you to connect Phinney's model back to its Eriksonian roots. What makes Phinney's contribution distinctive is her application of these developmental principles specifically to the ethnic domain — acknowledging that ethnic identity poses unique challenges that general identity models don't fully capture, such as navigating societal stereotypes, encountering discrimination, and reconciling cultural values that may differ from those of the dominant culture.

Example

A biracial adolescent in high school (Stage 1) may have never questioned their identity. After experiencing a microaggression in which a peer asks "But what are you really?" they enter Stage 2, reading about their cultural history and connecting with cultural organizations. Over time, they arrive at Stage 3 with a secure, self-defined sense of identity. Notice that the catalyst — the microaggression — is precisely the kind of "significant experience" Phinney describes as triggering the moratorium stage. Without that encounter, the adolescent might have remained in an unexamined state for much longer.

Clinically, understanding where a client falls along these stages can shape therapeutic work in meaningful ways. A client in Stage 1 who presents with vague feelings of not belonging may benefit from gentle exploration of their cultural background. A client in Stage 2 — actively searching and perhaps feeling angry or confused about experiences of discrimination — may need a therapist who can sit with that discomfort and validate the exploration process rather than rushing toward resolution. And a client in Stage 3 may draw on their achieved ethnic identity as a source of resilience during stressful life events.

Research indicates that a strong, achieved ethnic identity is associated with higher self-esteem, better academic outcomes, and psychological well-being, particularly for members of minority groups. This finding has been replicated across diverse samples, including African American, Latino, Asian American, and multiracial populations. For clinicians, this underscores an important point: ethnic identity is not merely a sociological curiosity — it is a psychological resource. Helping clients move toward an achieved ethnic identity can be a meaningful therapeutic goal, one that strengthens the very foundation of how they see themselves in the world.

Phinney, J. S. (1992). The Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure: A new scale for use with diverse groups. Journal of Adolescent Research, 7(2), 156–176.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Berry's acculturation model: Four strategies based on two independent dimensions (heritage maintenance, host engagement). Integration = best outcomes; marginalization = worst outcomes. Know that the host society's openness constrains which strategies are available.
  • Acculturative stress is a normative response, not individual pathology. Risk factors: language barriers, discrimination, involuntary migration, loss of social support. Protective factors: ethnic community ties, bilingualism, family cohesion.
  • Phinney's ethnic identity development parallels Marcia's identity statuses: Unexamined (diffusion/foreclosure), Search (moratorium), Achieved. A strong achieved ethnic identity is associated with higher self-esteem, better academic outcomes, and psychological resilience.
  • Do not confuse acculturation (cultural change from sustained contact between groups) with enculturation (socialization into one's own heritage culture) or assimilation (one specific acculturation strategy that abandons heritage culture).
  • Berry's model treats heritage maintenance and host engagement as independent dimensions, not opposite ends of a single continuum. This is a frequently tested distinction.
