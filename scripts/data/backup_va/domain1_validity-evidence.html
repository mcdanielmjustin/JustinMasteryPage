Introduction to Validity

The previous chapters established that reliability is a necessary foundation for measurement — without consistent scores, no meaningful interpretation is possible. But reliability alone is not enough. Imagine you've developed a new screening instrument designed to identify children at risk for reading disabilities. The test has excellent reliability — scores are consistent across administrations and between raters. Parents and teachers find the items sensible and relevant. But here's the critical question: when a child scores in the "at-risk" range, does that score actually mean what you think it means? Are you justified in using that result to recommend early intervention services? These are questions not about reliability, but about something far more fundamental — they are questions about validity.

Validity is often described as the single most important consideration in test development and evaluation, and for good reason. A perfectly reliable test is useless — even dangerous — if the conclusions drawn from its scores are unfounded. A thermometer that consistently reads 98.6°F is wonderfully reliable, but if the patient actually has a 103°F fever, that consistency means nothing. In psychological measurement, the stakes are just as high: clinical diagnoses, educational placements, hiring decisions, and forensic evaluations all hinge on whether test scores genuinely support the inferences we make from them. Validity is what separates meaningful measurement from confident guessing.

Formally, validity refers to the degree to which evidence and theory support the interpretations of test scores for their proposed uses. Notice the careful wording — validity is not simply about whether a test "measures what it claims to measure," though that familiar shorthand captures part of the idea. Rather, it concerns the entire chain of reasoning from a number on a score report to the real-world decision or interpretation that number is meant to support. Unlike reliability, which asks "Is this measurement consistent?", validity asks "Is this measurement meaningful and appropriate for the purpose at hand?" (AERA et al., 2014).

Key Terminology

  • Validity: The degree to which evidence supports the intended interpretation of test scores for a specific purpose
  • Validation: The ongoing process of accumulating evidence to support score interpretations
  • Validity Coefficient: A correlation between test scores and a criterion measure
  • Construct: A theoretical concept or attribute that a test is designed to measure

One of the most important shifts in modern measurement theory is the recognition that validity is not a property of the test itself — it is a property of the interpretations and uses of test scores. This distinction has profound practical implications. Consider the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II): substantial evidence supports its use as a screening tool for the presence and severity of depressive symptoms in clinical populations. But does that same evidence justify using BDI-II scores to differentiate between major depressive disorder and persistent depressive disorder? Or to predict which patients will respond to a particular antidepressant? Not necessarily. The same test, yielding the same scores, may be valid for one purpose and entirely unsupported for another. This means that validation is never a one-time event — it is an ongoing process of accumulating evidence that a particular score interpretation is justified in a particular context for a particular use.

This contemporary understanding owes much to Samuel Messick (1989), who articulated an influential unitary concept of validity. Messick argued that the older practice of treating "content validity," "criterion validity," and "construct validity" as separate, independent types was misleading. Instead, he proposed that all validity evidence ultimately serves to support or undermine our understanding of the construct — the theoretical attribute or concept — that the test is designed to measure. Under this framework, examining whether test items adequately sample a content domain, whether scores predict relevant outcomes, and whether score patterns align with theoretical expectations are not different "kinds" of validity but rather different sources of evidence that together build (or erode) the case for construct validity. As we move into the next section, we will examine these sources of evidence in detail, exploring how each contributes to the broader argument that a test's scores mean what we claim they mean.

Sources of Validity Evidence

Having established that validity is a unitary concept supported by converging lines of evidence, we now turn to the specific types of evidence that researchers and test developers gather to build the validity argument. The Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing (AERA et al., 2014) identifies five major sources of validity evidence, each addressing a different facet of the question "Does this score mean what we claim it means?"

Evidence Based on Test Content

Content validity refers to the degree to which test items adequately sample the domain of content the test is intended to measure. It is established through logical analysis rather than statistical procedures and is particularly important for achievement tests and job performance assessments.

Establishing Content Validity

  • Domain definition: Clearly specify the content domain to be measured
  • Test blueprint: Create a table of specifications linking items to content areas
  • Expert review: Have subject matter experts evaluate item relevance and representativeness
  • Content validity ratio (CVR): Quantify agreement among experts about item essentiality

Content Validity Example

A licensing exam for clinical psychologists should sample knowledge across relevant domains: assessment, diagnosis, treatment, ethics, and research methods. If the exam heavily emphasizes one area while neglecting others, it would have poor content validity for measuring overall competence in clinical psychology.

Face Validity

Face validity refers to whether a test appears to measure what it claims to measure, from the perspective of examinees or untrained observers. While not technically a form of validity evidence, face validity can affect examinee motivation, public acceptance, and perceived fairness. A test can have good construct validity but poor face validity, or vice versa.

Evidence Based on Response Processes

This type of evidence examines whether examinees actually engage in the cognitive processes the test intends to measure. Methods include think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking studies, and analysis of response patterns.

Response Process Investigation

If a reading comprehension test is designed to measure inference-making, validity evidence should demonstrate that examinees actually make inferences to answer questions, rather than relying on prior knowledge or test-taking strategies that bypass the intended processes.

Evidence Based on Internal Structure

Internal structure evidence examines whether the relationships among test items and components conform to the theoretical structure of the construct. Factor analysis is the primary statistical technique for evaluating internal structure.

The initial factor solution extracted through methods such as principal components analysis or principal axis factoring often produces a mathematically optimal but psychologically ambiguous result. Factor loadings in the unrotated matrix tend to be diffuse, with many variables loading moderately on multiple factors, making interpretation difficult. Factor rotation addresses this problem by repositioning the factor axes in multidimensional space to achieve a clearer, more interpretable pattern of loadings—ideally one in which each variable loads highly on one factor and minimally on others.

Rotation does not alter the total variance explained by the factor solution or change the communalities of individual variables. What it changes is how that explained variance is distributed across factors. The goal is to approximate what Thurstone called simple structure: a configuration in which each variable has a strong association with one factor and near-zero associations with the remaining factors. Simple structure makes it far easier to label and interpret factors because the variables clustering together on each dimension share clear conceptual meaning.

Example

An unrotated solution for a personality inventory might show that items measuring both extraversion and agreeableness load moderately (.40–.50) on the first factor. After Varimax rotation, the extraversion items shift to load .70+ on Factor 1 and near .10 on Factor 2, while agreeableness items load .70+ on Factor 2 and near .10 on Factor 1. The underlying data have not changed, but the rotated matrix reveals the two distinct personality dimensions far more clearly.

Rotation methods fall into two broad categories: orthogonal approaches (such as Varimax) that maintain independence among factors, and oblique approaches (such as Promax) that permit factors to correlate. Regardless of method, the fundamental purpose of rotation remains the same—transforming the initial solution into one that is more readily interpretable while preserving the mathematical integrity of the analysis.

Communality (h²)

Communality represents the proportion of variance in a single observed variable that is explained by all extracted factors combined. It is calculated by summing the squared factor loadings for that variable across every retained factor.

In a factor matrix, each variable's communality (denoted h²) quantifies how much of that variable's total variability is accounted for by the complete set of identified factors. A communality of .75 for a particular test means that 75% of the score variance on that test is shared with the extracted factor solution, while the remaining 25% is unique variance—composed of variance specific to that measure plus error variance. Variables with high communalities are well-represented by the factor solution; those with low communalities (e.g., below .30 or .40) may be poorly captured and could be candidates for removal or revision.

Example

Suppose a vocabulary test has a loading of .70 on a Verbal Comprehension factor and .30 on a Processing Speed factor. Its communality would be (.70)² + (.30)² = .49 + .09 = .58. This means 58% of the variance in vocabulary scores is explained by these two factors together, with 42% remaining as unique or error variance.

Communality values range from 0 to 1.0. Examining communalities helps researchers determine whether the factor model adequately represents each measured variable and whether the overall solution captures sufficient shared variance across the test battery. When communalities are uniformly low, it may indicate that additional factors are needed or that the variables do not share a coherent latent structure.

Orthogonal vs. Oblique Factors

In factor analysis, the term orthogonal describes factors that are statistically independent of one another—that is, they share no variance and their correlation is zero. Geometrically, orthogonal factors are positioned at right angles (90°) to each other in multidimensional space. By contrast, oblique factors are permitted to correlate with one another, reflecting the reality that many psychological constructs share some degree of overlap.

The choice between orthogonal and oblique solutions carries important theoretical implications. When a researcher selects an orthogonal rotation method (e.g., Varimax), they are imposing the assumption that the underlying dimensions are completely independent. This produces a simpler, more parsimonious solution but may distort the true relationships among factors if the constructs genuinely covary. Oblique rotation methods (e.g., Promax, Direct Oblimin) allow factors to correlate freely, often providing a more psychologically realistic representation. For instance, cognitive abilities such as verbal reasoning and working memory are conceptually distinct yet empirically correlated; forcing them into an orthogonal framework would misrepresent their relationship.

In practice, many researchers recommend beginning with an oblique rotation. If the interfactor correlations turn out to be near zero, the oblique solution will closely approximate an orthogonal one, confirming that independent factors are appropriate. If substantial correlations emerge, however, the oblique solution provides a more accurate depiction of the construct space.

Factor correlations Fixed at zero Freely estimated
Common methods Varimax, Quartimax, Equamax Promax, Direct Oblimin

After extracting factors in a factor analysis, the initial solution is often difficult to interpret because variables may load moderately on multiple factors. Factor rotation is applied to achieve a simpler, more interpretable structure by redistributing the variance among factors. Two broad categories of rotation exist: orthogonal and oblique.

Varimax rotation, developed by Kaiser (1958), is the most widely used orthogonal rotation method. When a researcher applies varimax rotation, the resulting factors are constrained to be orthogonal—meaning they are completely uncorrelated with one another (i.e., the correlation between any pair of rotated factors is zero). Varimax achieves this by maximizing the variance of the squared loadings within each factor, which pushes each variable toward having a high loading on one factor and near-zero loadings on the remaining factors.

Feature Orthogonal Rotation (e.g., Varimax) Oblique Rotation (e.g., Promax, Oblimin)
Factor correlations Constrained to zero (uncorrelated) Permitted to be non-zero (correlated)
Assumption Underlying dimensions are independent Underlying dimensions may overlap
Interpretation Simpler; factor loadings directly interpretable More complex; requires pattern and structure matrices
When preferred When constructs are theoretically distinct When constructs are expected to be related

Clinical Note

The choice between orthogonal and oblique rotation should be guided by theory. In psychological measurement, many constructs (e.g., anxiety and depression) are expected to correlate, making oblique rotation more realistic. However, varimax remains common in practice because of its simplicity, and when factors are truly independent, orthogonal and oblique solutions converge on similar results.

Communality

Communality (denoted h²) represents the total proportion of variance in a test or variable that is explained by all extracted factors combined. It is calculated by summing the squared factor loadings for a given variable across every retained factor. A communality of .40 indicates that the identified factors collectively account for 40% of the variability in that variable's scores, while the remaining 60% reflects unique variance (specific variance plus error variance) not captured by the factor solution.

Communality values range from 0 to 1.0. Higher communalities suggest that the factor model does a good job of representing the variable, whereas low communalities indicate that the variable shares little in common with the other variables in the analysis and may not belong in the factor solution. A variable with a communality below .20, for example, contributes minimally to the factor structure and may warrant removal. In contrast, communalities above .60 suggest that the extracted factors capture the majority of meaningful variance in that measure.

Example

A depression inventory item loads .50 on a "negative affect" factor and .30 on a "somatic complaints" factor. Its communality equals .50² + .30² = .25 + .09 = .34. This means 34% of the variability in responses to that item is explained by the two-factor solution, while 66% remains unexplained by the model.

Factor Loadings

A factor loading represents the correlation between a test (or item) and a given factor. Because it is a correlation coefficient, squaring the factor loading yields the proportion of variance in the test that is accounted for by that factor. For instance, if a test has a factor loading of .30 on Factor I, this means that Factor I accounts for .30² = .09, or 9%, of the variability in scores on that test. Higher factor loadings indicate that the factor plays a larger role in explaining test performance. A loading of .70, for example, would indicate that 49% of score variance is attributable to the factor. Researchers commonly use a threshold of .30 to .40 as the minimum loading necessary to consider a variable meaningfully associated with a factor, though this cutoff varies by context and sample size.

Example

Suppose a vocabulary subtest loads .80 on a verbal comprehension factor and .25 on a processing speed factor. The verbal comprehension factor explains .80² = 64% of the variance in vocabulary scores, while the processing speed factor explains only .25² = 6.25%. This pattern confirms that the vocabulary subtest is primarily a measure of verbal comprehension, with minimal contribution from processing speed.

When conducting factor analysis, researchers must select a rotation method to produce an interpretable factor solution. In an orthogonal rotation, the extracted factors are constrained to be uncorrelated with one another—that is, each factor is statistically independent of every other factor. Common orthogonal rotation techniques include Varimax (which maximizes the variance of squared loadings within each factor to produce simple structure) and Quartimax (which simplifies the interpretation of variables rather than factors). Orthogonal solutions are mathematically simpler and easier to interpret because the factor loading matrix simultaneously represents both the unique and total relationship between each variable and each factor. However, the assumption that psychological dimensions are truly uncorrelated is often unrealistic. For example, forcing cognitive ability factors to be independent may misrepresent their known empirical overlap.

Orthogonal vs. Oblique Rotation

Orthogonal rotation assumes factors are uncorrelated (perpendicular in geometric space), producing a single loading matrix. Oblique rotation permits factors to correlate, yielding separate pattern and structure matrices. The choice between them should be guided by theoretical expectations about the relationships among the constructs being measured.

The correlation between a test (or individual variable) and a factor is referred to as a factor loading. Like any correlation coefficient, squaring a factor loading yields the proportion of variance in the variable that is accounted for by the factor. For example, if a test has a factor loading of .60, squaring this value (.60² = .36) indicates that 36% of the variability in that test's scores is explained by variability in the underlying factor. The remaining 64% of variance reflects other influences—additional factors, measurement error, or construct-irrelevant sources. This calculation is directly analogous to computing a coefficient of determination (r²) in correlational analysis. Factor loadings are central to interpreting factor analytic results because they indicate how strongly each observed variable is associated with each latent dimension.

Factor Loading Interpretation

A factor loading is the correlation between an observed variable and a latent factor. Squaring the loading provides the proportion of shared variance. Loadings of .30 to .40 are often considered minimally meaningful, while loadings above .60 indicate that the factor accounts for a substantial portion (≥ 36%) of the variable's variance.

Factor Loading Variance Explained (Loading²) Interpretation
.30 9% Minimal; borderline meaningful
.40 16% Moderate association
.60 36% Strong; substantial shared variance
.80 64% Very strong; factor is primary determinant

Factor analysis can employ different rotation methods to clarify the relationships among extracted factors. One fundamental distinction concerns whether factors are permitted to correlate with one another. In an oblique rotation, the extracted factors are allowed to be correlated. This approach assumes that the underlying dimensions of a construct may share variance—a theoretically reasonable assumption in many psychological domains. For instance, cognitive abilities such as verbal reasoning and working memory are conceptually distinct yet empirically related, making oblique rotation (e.g., Promax or Direct Oblimin) an appropriate analytic choice. When factors are correlated, the solution produces both a pattern matrix (showing unique contributions of each factor to each variable) and a structure matrix (showing total correlations between variables and factors, including shared variance among factors). Oblique solutions often provide a more realistic representation of psychological constructs, since pure independence among dimensions is rare in behavioral science.

Clinical Note

When developing or evaluating a multidimensional clinical instrument—such as a measure of personality pathology—oblique rotation is typically preferred because the dimensions assessed (e.g., emotional dysregulation, interpersonal dysfunction, behavioral disinhibition) are expected to co-occur and share variance. Choosing an oblique approach allows the analyst to empirically examine the degree of factor intercorrelation rather than forcing an assumption of independence.

Factor Analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identifies the underlying factor structure without prior hypotheses about the number or nature of factors. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tests whether data fit a hypothesized factor structure based on theory.

Factor Analysis Example

An anxiety measure theorized to assess three components (cognitive, somatic, behavioral) should show three distinct factors in factor analysis. If items from different subscales load on the same factor, or if a single general factor emerges, the theoretical structure is not supported.

Evidence Based on Relations to Other Variables

This evidence demonstrates that test scores relate to external variables in ways consistent with the theoretical understanding of the construct. This category includes convergent and discriminant validity evidence, as well as criterion-related validity.

One practical application of examining relationships with external variables involves concurrent validity, a form of criterion-related validity in which a new measure and an established criterion are administered during the same time period. Concurrent validity is especially relevant when developing brief screening instruments intended to approximate the information captured by longer, well-validated tools. The logic is straightforward: if a shorter measure correlates highly with a gold-standard instrument when both are given to the same individuals at approximately the same time, the new measure can be considered a reasonable substitute for specific purposes such as initial screening.

Example

A researcher develops a 10-item depression screening tool and wants to evaluate its concurrent validity. To do so, the researcher administers the new screener alongside an established, well-validated 50-item depression inventory to a sample of outpatient clinic patients. Both instruments are completed during the same assessment session. The researcher then calculates the Pearson correlation between scores on the two measures. A high positive correlation (e.g., r = .82) would provide strong concurrent validity evidence, suggesting that the brief screener captures much of the same depressive symptomatology as the longer instrument. A low or moderate correlation would raise concerns about whether the shorter tool adequately represents the construct as measured by the criterion.

Concurrent validity differs from predictive validity primarily in timing: concurrent studies collect test and criterion data simultaneously, whereas predictive studies measure the criterion at a future point. Both are forms of criterion-related evidence, but they address different questions. Concurrent designs ask whether a test can substitute for an existing measure now, while predictive designs ask whether current test scores can forecast future outcomes.

Convergent Validity

Convergent validity is demonstrated when a test correlates strongly with other measures of the same or similar constructs. High correlations with conceptually related measures support the interpretation that the test measures the intended construct.

Discriminant (Divergent) Validity

Discriminant validity is demonstrated when a test shows low correlations with measures of theoretically unrelated constructs. This evidence supports the distinctiveness of the measured construct.

Multitrait-Multimethod Matrix

Campbell and Fiske (1959) introduced the multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) matrix to evaluate both convergent and discriminant validity simultaneously. The matrix examines correlations among multiple traits measured by multiple methods.

  • Convergent validity: High correlations between the same trait measured by different methods (monotrait-heteromethod)
  • Discriminant validity: Lower correlations between different traits, even when measured by the same method (heterotrait-monomethod)

Evidence Based on Consequences of Testing

This evidence considers the intended and unintended consequences of test use, including potential benefits and harms. It addresses whether test use achieves its intended outcomes and whether any negative consequences are related to construct-irrelevant factors such as bias.
