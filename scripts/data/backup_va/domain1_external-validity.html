External Validity

Having explored the many threats to internal validity — from maturation and history to resentful demoralization — you now have a strong foundation for evaluating whether a study can credibly claim that its independent variable caused the observed effect. But even a study with impeccable internal validity faces a crucial follow-up question: So what? If a therapy reduces anxiety in a controlled university lab with undergraduate volunteers, does that mean it will help a 45-year-old veteran at a community mental health center? This is the domain of external validity — and it is where the practical value of research lives or dies.

External validity (also called generalizability) refers to the degree to which research findings can be extended beyond the specific conditions under which they were obtained. A study with high external validity produces results that hold up across different populations, settings, time periods, and variations of the treatment. Without it, even the most tightly controlled experiment risks becoming a curiosity — true within the confines of the laboratory, but irrelevant to the real-world problems clinicians and policymakers are trying to solve.

Not all research relies on comparing groups of participants, and understanding these alternative designs is essential for appreciating how questions of generalizability play out differently depending on the research framework. Single-subject designs (also called single-case experimental designs or N-of-1 designs) focus on the intensive study of individual participants or a small number of cases. Rooted in the behaviorist tradition, these designs are widely used in applied behavior analysis, clinical psychology, and educational research — particularly when group comparisons are impractical (imagine trying to randomly assign 60 children with a rare genetic syndrome to treatment and control groups) or when the research question centers on individual-level change rather than group averages.

The defining methodological feature of all single-subject designs is repeated measurement: the dependent variable is assessed multiple times at regular intervals throughout every phase of the study — both during baseline (no-treatment) phases and during treatment phases. Think of it as creating a detailed behavioral diary rather than taking a single snapshot. This continuous data collection generates a time-series record that allows the researcher to observe trends, variability, and level shifts within and across phases. Rather than relying on group means and inferential statistics, the researcher typically evaluates treatment effects through visual inspection of graphed data, looking for clear changes in level, trend, or variability that coincide with phase transitions. If a child's disruptive behaviors are hovering around 12 per hour during baseline and then drop abruptly to 3 per hour the moment an intervention begins, that sharp discontinuity tells a compelling visual story.

Common Single-Subject Designs

ABA (Reversal/Withdrawal): A baseline phase (A) is followed by a treatment phase (B), then a return to baseline (A). Causal inference is strengthened when the dependent variable changes with the introduction of treatment and reverts when treatment is withdrawn. An ABAB extension adds a second treatment phase.

Multiple Baseline: Treatment is introduced at staggered time points across different behaviors, settings, or participants. If the dependent variable changes only when treatment is applied to each specific target, rival explanations like history and maturation become implausible.

Alternating Treatments: Two or more treatments are rapidly alternated within the same phase, allowing direct comparison of their relative effects on the dependent variable.

Each of these designs has distinct strengths worth appreciating. The ABA (reversal) design offers perhaps the clearest demonstration of causation — if the behavior worsens each time treatment is withdrawn and improves each time it is reinstated, coincidence becomes an increasingly implausible explanation. However, there is a significant ethical and practical limitation: some behaviors cannot or should not be reversed. If a child learns to read through an intervention, you cannot "un-teach" reading by withdrawing treatment. The multiple-baseline design elegantly sidesteps this problem. By staggering the introduction of treatment across, say, three different classrooms, the researcher can demonstrate that improvements occur only when and where the intervention is applied — no withdrawal necessary. The alternating treatments design, meanwhile, is uniquely suited for comparing the relative effectiveness of two or more interventions within the same individual.

Because these designs lack random assignment and typically study one or very few individuals, their primary vulnerability involves external validity — findings from a single case may not generalize broadly. A token economy that dramatically reduces one child's aggression may not work for a child whose behavior serves different functions. However, the tight experimental control achieved through repeated measurement and phase manipulation can yield strong internal validity, particularly in reversal and multiple-baseline configurations. Replication across participants — conducting the same single-subject study with multiple individuals — strengthens confidence in the generalizability of observed effects, building a cumulative evidence base one case at a time.

Example

A behavior analyst uses an ABAB design to evaluate a token economy for reducing a child's disruptive classroom behavior. During each five-day baseline phase (A), the analyst records the frequency of disruptions every 30 minutes. During each treatment phase (B), the same recording schedule continues while the token system is in place. The analyst graphs all data points and observes that disruptions decrease sharply each time the token economy is introduced and increase each time it is withdrawn, providing compelling evidence that the intervention—not some extraneous factor—is responsible for the behavioral change.

When studies involve repeated exposure to multiple treatment conditions — as in alternating treatments designs or within-subjects experiments more broadly — a new set of validity threats emerges. Specifically, order effects and carryover effects can muddy the interpretation of results in ways that threaten both internal and external validity. Order effects occur when a participant's performance changes simply because of the position of a condition in the sequence (e.g., performing better on the third task regardless of what it is, due to practice). Carryover effects are more insidious: the lingering influence of one specific condition on responses to the next (e.g., a relaxation exercise still calming participants when they begin a subsequent stress condition). Counterbalancing addresses these threats by systematically varying the sequence in which participants experience conditions, and one particularly efficient approach is the Latin square design.

Latin Square Design

A counterbalancing arrangement in which each treatment condition appears exactly once in every ordinal position across different groups of participants. If there are k treatment levels, the Latin square requires k different orderings, ensuring that every condition occupies the first position, the second position, and so on, with equal frequency.

Consider a study with three treatment conditions (A, B, C). A full counterbalance would require all 3! = 6 possible orderings — ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, and CBA. The Latin square reduces this to just three sequences — for example, ABC, BCA, and CAB — while still guaranteeing that each condition occupies each serial position exactly once. This economy is especially valuable when the number of conditions is large, because full counterbalancing becomes impractical (e.g., four conditions produce 24 orderings, five conditions produce 120, and the numbers escalate rapidly from there).

By distributing each condition evenly across ordinal positions, the Latin square controls for linear practice effects, fatigue, and similar time-related confounds. However, it does not fully control for all possible carryover interactions between specific pairs of treatments. For instance, if receiving Condition A immediately before Condition B produces a unique interactive effect that does not occur when Condition C precedes Condition B, a standard Latin square might miss this asymmetry. When such asymmetric carryover is suspected, researchers may use a balanced Latin square, which adds the constraint that each condition precedes and follows every other condition an equal number of times — offering a more thorough, though still economical, safeguard against sequence-dependent confounds.

Example

A cognitive psychologist tests the effects of three background noise levels (silence, white noise, music) on reading comprehension. Rather than running all six possible orders, she uses a Latin square: Group 1 receives Silence → White Noise → Music; Group 2 receives White Noise → Music → Silence; Group 3 receives Music → Silence → White Noise. Each noise condition appears once in the first, second, and third testing block, so any improvement or decline due to practice or fatigue is distributed equally across conditions.

Types of External Validity

External validity is not a single, monolithic concept — it encompasses several distinct dimensions, each addressing a different facet of generalizability. Understanding these types helps researchers and clinicians pinpoint exactly where a study's findings might break down when applied beyond the original research context. A study might generalize beautifully to new populations but fail to translate to different settings, or it might hold across environments but prove time-bound to a particular cultural moment.

  • Population validity: Can results be generalized to other people? A study conducted exclusively with young adults from a single university may not extend to children, older adults, or individuals from different cultural backgrounds.
  • Ecological validity: Can results be generalized to other settings? Findings from a highly controlled laboratory environment may look very different when replicated in a busy community clinic or a participant's home.
  • Temporal validity: Can results be generalized to other time periods? Research on attitudes conducted in the 1970s may not reflect contemporary values, and intervention studies conducted before the smartphone era may yield different results today.
  • Treatment variation validity: Can results be generalized to other versions of the treatment? A manualized therapy delivered by its developer in a research trial may not produce the same results when adapted by a clinician with different training and style.

Threats to External Validity

Just as internal validity can be undermined by specific confounds, external validity has its own set of threats — each representing a way in which the treatment effect observed in a study might be bound to particular people, places, times, or procedural features. These threats typically take the form of interactions: the treatment works, but only under specific conditions that may not be replicated in the broader world. Recognizing these threats is critical for clinicians who must decide whether published evidence applies to the client sitting across from them.

Interaction of Selection and Treatment

This threat emerges when research findings apply only to the specific type of participants studied, failing to generalize to people with different characteristics. For example, a cognitive-behavioral intervention shown to reduce test anxiety in college students might not work as well for older adults facing different stressors, or for adolescents with co-occurring learning disabilities. Volunteer bias is a particularly common form of this threat — people who sign up for research studies tend to differ from those who do not in motivation, openness to new experiences, and even symptom severity. If an intervention is tested only on eager volunteers, its effectiveness in a broader population of reluctant or mandated clients remains an open question.

Interaction of Setting and Treatment

Results obtained in one setting may not translate to another, and this threat is especially relevant when laboratory findings are applied to clinical practice. A therapy that produces robust effects in a quiet, well-resourced university clinic — where therapists have small caseloads and clients have reliable transportation — may falter in a community mental health center serving clients who face housing instability, waitlists, and frequent session cancellations. The setting itself becomes part of the treatment context, and stripping it away can diminish or alter the effect.

Interaction of History and Treatment

This threat reminds us that research findings are not timeless truths — they are embedded in particular historical and cultural moments. A study on the effectiveness of group therapy for social isolation conducted before the rise of social media may not replicate in an era where "social connection" has been fundamentally redefined. Similarly, interventions developed and validated during periods of relative economic stability may prove less effective during recessions, pandemics, or other societal upheavals that alter the psychological landscape in which treatment operates.

Clinical Implications: Generalizability

When applying research findings to practice, clinicians must consider whether results generalize to their specific clients and settings. If a treatment was validated primarily on white, middle-class college students, its effectiveness with diverse clinical populations should be evaluated carefully. A practitioner working with refugees, for instance, should not assume that an anxiety intervention developed and tested with undergraduates will function identically in a population dealing with trauma, displacement, and language barriers. Evidence-based practice requires attention to both internal validity (does it work?) and external validity (will it work for my clients?). The best clinicians hold research findings with what might be called "informed tentativeness" — respecting the evidence while remaining alert to the conditions under which it was produced.

Reactive Effects of Testing

Sometimes the very act of measuring participants before a treatment changes how they respond to that treatment. A pretest on attitudes toward diversity, for example, might prime participants to think more carefully about the topic, making them more receptive to a subsequent diversity training program. Without that pretest, the training alone might have produced weaker results. This threat is particularly troublesome because it means a treatment's effectiveness may depend on the presence of a measurement procedure that would not exist outside the research context. In the real world, clients do not typically complete a comprehensive assessment battery immediately before beginning therapy — so treatment effects observed in studies with pretests may overestimate real-world outcomes.

Multiple Treatment Interference

When participants receive multiple treatments in sequence — common in within-subjects designs and clinical settings where clients cycle through various interventions — the effects of earlier treatments may persist and influence responses to later ones. A participant who first receives relaxation training and then cognitive restructuring carries the benefits (or side effects) of relaxation into the second phase, making it impossible to know whether cognitive restructuring alone would have produced the same result. This threat connects directly to the order effects and carryover effects discussed earlier in the context of counterbalancing. In clinical practice, multiple treatment interference is the rule rather than the exception, as clients typically arrive having already tried other therapies, medications, or self-help strategies — each of which may interact with the current treatment in unpredictable ways.

Internal vs. External Validity Trade-offs

Having examined external validity threats in detail, a pattern becomes clear: many of the design features that strengthen internal validity — tight laboratory controls, homogeneous samples, standardized protocols — simultaneously weaken external validity by creating artificial conditions that do not mirror real-world practice. This tension is not a flaw in the research enterprise; it is a fundamental trade-off that every investigator must navigate deliberately.

The Trade-off

High Control (Lab): Strong internal validity, weaker external validity. We know the treatment works in controlled conditions but may not generalize to the real world.

Naturalistic Settings (Field): Stronger external validity, weaker internal validity. Results generalize better, but we're less certain the treatment caused the effects.

Researchers address this trade-off through programmatic research—conducting multiple studies that vary in methodology to build evidence that is both internally valid and generalizable.

Construct Validity in Research

Imagine a researcher who wants to study whether "anxiety" causes poor test performance. She designs an elegant experiment with random assignment, careful controls, and sufficient statistical power. Her results are internally valid — she can confidently say that her independent variable caused changes in her dependent variable. But here's the critical question that remains: Did her independent variable actually represent anxiety, or did it really induce something closer to frustration, confusion, or general arousal? And did her dependent variable — a single timed math quiz — truly capture "test performance" as most psychologists understand it? These are questions of construct validity in research, and they cut to the heart of whether a study's findings mean what the researcher claims they mean.

In the preceding section, we explored how researchers navigate the tension between internal and external validity, often relying on programmatic research to build evidence that is both tightly controlled and broadly applicable. But even when a study achieves strong internal validity and reasonable generalizability, a third form of validity can quietly undermine the entire enterprise. Construct validity in research refers to the degree to which the independent and dependent variables represent the theoretical constructs of interest. This is distinct from test validity (covered in later chapters) and addresses whether the operational definitions used in research adequately capture the intended constructs. A study can demonstrate a clear causal relationship between Variable A and Variable B, but if Variable A doesn't truly represent the construct the researcher intended to manipulate — and Variable B doesn't truly measure the outcome of theoretical interest — then the study's conclusions are built on shaky conceptual ground.

Think of it this way: internal validity asks, "Did X cause Y?" Construct validity asks, "Is X really what we think it is, and is Y really what we think it is?" A study of "social support" that operationalizes the construct solely as the number of Facebook friends a person has may have excellent internal controls, but its construct validity is questionable — the operational definition captures something far narrower (and arguably quite different) than most theoretical accounts of social support.

Threats to Construct Validity

Construct validity is compromised when the operational definitions or measurement procedures fail to capture the theoretical constructs under investigation. Several systematic threats can undermine the accuracy of these inferences, ranging from narrow operationalization of variables to biases introduced by participants and researchers alike. Understanding these threats is essential because even the most methodologically rigorous study produces misleading conclusions if the constructs themselves are poorly represented. Let's examine each threat in turn, along with examples that make them concrete and clinically relevant.

Inadequate Explication of Constructs

Before a researcher can measure or manipulate a construct, she must define it with precision and depth. Poor or incomplete definition of the constructs being studied leads to operationalizations that miss important aspects of the construct or include irrelevant elements. Consider a researcher studying "depression" who fails to specify whether she means the clinical syndrome (with its somatic, cognitive, affective, and behavioral components), depressed mood as a transient emotional state, or a personality trait of depressiveness. Without careful explication, the resulting operational definition may capture only a sliver of the intended construct — or inadvertently drift into measuring something else entirely. This threat is particularly common when researchers borrow everyday language (like "stress" or "self-esteem") without grounding their usage in established theoretical frameworks.

Mono-Operation Bias

Suppose a clinical researcher wants to study the effects of loneliness on health outcomes, and she measures loneliness using a single item: "How often do you feel lonely?" This approach exemplifies mono-operation bias — using only one operationalization of a construct. A single measure captures only part of a construct and includes irrelevant variance. Loneliness might also involve perceived social isolation, absence of a confidant, or lack of group belonging, none of which are tapped by that single question. Multiple operationalizations provide stronger evidence because they allow the researcher to triangulate on the construct, capturing its full dimensionality while reducing the influence of any one measure's idiosyncratic features. When you see a study that relies on a single questionnaire to represent a complex psychological construct, mono-operation bias should be the first concern that comes to mind.

Mono-Method Bias

Even when a researcher uses multiple measures of a construct, she may still fall prey to mono-method bias if all of those measures rely on the same method of measurement. For instance, assessing marital satisfaction with three different self-report questionnaires is better than using one, but all three share the limitations inherent to self-report: social desirability, limited introspective accuracy, and shared method variance that may inflate apparent relationships between variables. Using multiple methods — such as combining self-report with behavioral observation during a conflict discussion task and physiological measures like cortisol reactivity — provides substantially stronger evidence for construct validity. When two variables measured by the same method correlate highly, researchers must ask: is this a genuine relationship between the constructs, or an artifact of the shared measurement approach?

Hypothesis Guessing

Participants in research studies are not passive receptacles for experimental manipulations — they are active, curious human beings who often try to figure out what the study is really about. When participants guess what the study is about and alter their behavior accordingly, construct validity is threatened because their responses no longer reflect the construct of interest but rather their theories about what the researcher wants. These demand characteristics lead participants to behave as they think the researcher expects, sometimes confirming the hypothesis (the "good subject" effect) and other times deliberately contradicting it. A participant in a therapy outcome study who figures out she's in the treatment group, for example, might report feeling better not because the therapy worked but because she wants to please the researchers — fundamentally changing what the dependent variable is actually measuring.

Experimenter Expectancies

The influence doesn't flow in only one direction. Researchers' expectations can also influence participant behavior or data interpretation, a phenomenon famously demonstrated in Robert Rosenthal's classic studies on experimenter expectancy effects. A clinician-researcher who believes strongly in a new treatment may unwittingly convey warmth and enthusiasm to participants in the treatment group while being more neutral toward control-group participants, effectively confounding therapist behavior with the treatment construct. Even in data coding and scoring, researchers who know which condition a participant was assigned to may unconsciously interpret ambiguous responses in hypothesis-confirming ways. Double-blind procedures — in which neither the participants nor the individuals administering or scoring the measures know the group assignments — are the primary safeguard against this threat, and their absence should always raise questions about construct validity.

Confounding

Finally, construct validity is compromised when the operationalization of one construct is confounded with another construct — that is, when two (or more) conceptually distinct elements are bundled together in a way that makes it impossible to determine which one is responsible for the observed effects. For example, a treatment that includes both cognitive restructuring and relaxation training confounds these two components; if participants improve, the researcher cannot attribute the improvement to either construct individually. This threat extends beyond treatment studies: a measure of "intelligence" that requires extensive reading comprehension is confounded with verbal ability, and a "stress" manipulation that involves both time pressure and social evaluation conflates two distinct stressors. The solution often involves dismantling designs that isolate individual components, or careful selection of operational definitions that cleanly represent only the intended construct.

Statistical Conclusion Validity

Imagine a researcher conducts a carefully designed study examining whether a new therapy reduces anxiety symptoms. The treatment is well-defined, the control group is appropriate, and participants are randomly assigned. Everything looks methodologically sound. Yet when the results come back, the researcher concludes the therapy doesn't work — not because it truly lacks efficacy, but because the sample was too small to detect the real effect, or because dozens of statistical tests were run without correction, inflating false-positive rates. The problem here isn't with the study's design or its constructs — it's with the statistical machinery used to draw conclusions. This is the domain of statistical conclusion validity.

In the preceding section, we explored how construct validity in research concerns whether your variables truly represent the theoretical constructs you intend to study — as when a treatment confounds cognitive restructuring with relaxation training. Now we turn to a different but equally critical question: once you've measured your variables, are the statistical methods you use to analyze them appropriate, and are the conclusions you draw from those analyses accurate? Statistical conclusion validity refers to the appropriateness of the statistical methods employed and the accuracy of inferences about whether relationships or differences exist and how strong they are. Poor statistical conclusion validity leads to two dangerous outcomes: concluding that an effect exists when it doesn't, or missing a real effect that is genuinely there.

Think of statistical conclusion validity as the lens through which you examine your data. If the lens is cracked or distorted — through underpowered designs, violated test assumptions, or unreliable measurement — you'll misread what the data are actually telling you. A clinician-researcher who misinterprets statistical evidence may abandon a promising intervention or, worse, champion an ineffective one. Understanding the threats to statistical conclusion validity is therefore essential not just for passing an exam, but for becoming a thoughtful consumer and producer of psychological research.

Threats to Statistical Conclusion Validity

Statistical conclusion validity concerns the accuracy of inferences about the covariation between variables based on statistical evidence. When we ask, "Is there a real relationship between these variables, and if so, how strong is it?" we are squarely in this validity's territory. Several factors can compromise a researcher's ability to detect true relationships or, conversely, can lead to erroneous conclusions about statistical significance. Each threat distorts the statistical signal in a different way — some make real effects invisible, while others manufacture the illusion of effects that don't exist.

Low Statistical Power

When a study has insufficient power to detect a true effect, the researcher may incorrectly conclude that no effect exists — committing a Type II error. Consider a clinical trial testing a promising antidepressant with only 12 participants per group. Even if the drug genuinely reduces depression scores, the study may lack the statistical sensitivity to pick up the signal amidst the noise of individual variability. Small sample sizes and high within-group variability are the two most common culprits that reduce power. A study with low power is like trying to hear a conversation across a crowded, noisy restaurant — the message may be real, but the conditions make it nearly impossible to detect.

Violated Assumptions

Every statistical test rests on a set of assumptions — normality of distributions, homogeneity of variance, independence of observations, and others depending on the specific analysis. Using statistical tests when these assumptions are violated can produce misleading p-values and confidence intervals, leading to inaccurate conclusions. For instance, running an independent-samples t-test when one group's scores are severely skewed and the variances differ dramatically across groups may inflate or deflate the test statistic in unpredictable ways. The resulting conclusion — whether "significant" or "not significant" — cannot be trusted.

Fishing and Error Rate Problems

Conducting multiple statistical tests without appropriate correction inflates the probability of Type I errors — concluding that an effect is real when it is actually due to chance. "Fishing" for significant results, sometimes called a "fishing expedition," involves running test after test on the same dataset, hoping that something emerges as statistically significant. With a conventional alpha of .05, running 20 independent tests means you'd expect at least one to reach significance purely by chance. This is why techniques like the Bonferroni correction or controlling the false discovery rate are critical when multiple comparisons are involved. Without such safeguards, researchers risk capitalizing on chance and reporting findings that will not replicate.

Unreliability of Measures

Unreliable measures attenuate — that is, systematically weaken — observed relationships between variables. If your anxiety questionnaire produces inconsistent scores from one administration to the next, the random measurement error it introduces acts like static on a radio signal, obscuring the true association between your independent and dependent variables. Low reliability in either the manipulation of the IV or the measurement of the DV reduces the ability to detect true effects. This is one reason why psychometrically sound instruments are so important in research: a study can be perfectly designed in every other respect, but if the measures are unreliable, the statistical conclusions will be undermined from the start.

Restriction of Range

Limited variability in scores attenuates correlations and reduces the ability to detect relationships that genuinely exist in the broader population. Imagine studying the relationship between GRE scores and graduate school performance, but your sample consists only of students admitted to a highly selective program. Because nearly everyone in the sample scored in the top 5% on the GRE, there is very little spread in scores — and the resulting correlation will appear weak, even if the relationship is strong in the general applicant pool. Restriction of range is a particularly insidious threat because it can make a researcher conclude that two variables are unrelated when, in reality, the study simply failed to capture enough variability to reveal the true association.

Controlling Threats to Validity

Now that we have cataloged the threats to all four types of research validity — internal, external, construct, and statistical conclusion — the practical question becomes: what can a researcher actually do about them? No single study eliminates every threat, but a well-designed study employs a combination of design features and procedural safeguards to minimize the most serious ones. The strategies below represent the toolkit that every researcher and critical consumer of research should know.

Controlling Internal Validity Threats

  • Random assignment: The most powerful control for selection and related threats
  • Control groups: Comparison groups that don't receive treatment
  • Pretesting: Establishes baseline and helps detect selection differences
  • Matching: Pairing participants on relevant variables
  • Blinding: Participants and/or researchers unaware of condition
  • Standardized procedures: Consistent administration controls instrumentation
  • Follow-up of dropouts: Assess and address attrition bias

Controlling External Validity Threats

  • Random selection: Sampling from the target population
  • Heterogeneous samples: Including diverse participants
  • Multiple settings: Conducting studies in various contexts
  • Replication: Repeating studies with different populations and settings
  • Field experiments: Conducting research in naturalistic settings

Understanding the Distinction

Random assignment controls internal validity threats by ensuring groups are equivalent. Random selection (from the population to the sample) controls external validity threats by ensuring the sample represents the population. Many students confuse these concepts on the EPPP. Random assignment creates equivalent groups; random selection creates representative samples.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

Commonly Tested Topics for the EPPP

  • Internal Validity: Did the IV cause the DV change? Threats include history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression, selection, mortality
  • External Validity: Can we generalize findings? Population, ecological, and temporal generalizability
  • History: External events during study affect DV
  • Maturation: Natural changes in participants over time
  • Testing: Practice effects from repeated testing
  • Regression to Mean: Extreme scores move toward average on retest
  • Selection: Groups differ before treatment (main threat in quasi-experiments)
  • Mortality/Attrition: Differential dropout between groups
  • Random Assignment: Controls internal validity threats; creates equivalent groups
  • Random Selection: Controls external validity threats; creates representative samples
  • True Experiments: Have random assignment; strongest internal validity
  • Quasi-Experiments: No random assignment; selection is main threat
  • Trade-off: Lab studies have high internal/low external validity; field studies opposite
