Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

The t-tests introduced in the previous chapter handle the comparison of two group means beautifully — but psychological research rarely limits itself to two conditions. A clinical trial might compare CBT, psychodynamic therapy, medication, and a waitlist control. A developmental psychologist might examine cognitive performance across four age groups. Running multiple t-tests to compare each pair of groups inflates the Type I error rate dramatically, making false positives nearly inevitable. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), developed by Ronald Fisher (1925), solves this problem by testing whether at least one group mean differs significantly from the others in a single omnibus test, using an F-ratio that compares between-group variance to within-group variance.

One-Way ANOVA

Used when there is one independent variable with three or more levels. For example, comparing therapy outcomes across three treatment conditions (CBT, psychodynamic, medication).

Understanding the components of the F-ratio is essential for interpreting ANOVA results. The F-ratio is calculated as between-group variance divided by within-group variance. Critically, the numerator (between-group mean square) reflects both systematic differences due to treatment effects and random error. The denominator (within-group mean square) reflects only random variability—individual differences and measurement error unrelated to the independent variable. When the null hypothesis is true and there are no treatment effects, both the numerator and denominator estimate the same error variance, yielding an F-ratio near 1.0. When a genuine treatment effect exists, the numerator becomes larger than the denominator because it contains the additional systematic variance, pushing the F-ratio above 1.0. The larger the treatment effect relative to error, the larger the F-ratio and the more likely the result will be statistically significant.

Factorial ANOVA

Used when there are two or more independent variables. A 2×3 factorial ANOVA, for example, examines two IVs—one with 2 levels and one with 3 levels. Factorial ANOVA can detect:

  • Main effects: The effect of each independent variable separately
  • Interaction effects: Whether the effect of one IV depends on the level of another IV

Understanding Interactions

An interaction occurs when the effect of one independent variable differs depending on the level of another independent variable. For example, a medication might be more effective for women than for men—the effect of medication (IV1) depends on sex (IV2). Interactions are often depicted as non-parallel lines in a graph of means.

Repeated Measures ANOVA

Used when the same participants are measured under multiple conditions or at multiple time points. This is the extension of the paired t-test to three or more measurements. Also called within-subjects ANOVA.

Split-Plot (Mixed) ANOVA

When a research design includes both a between-subjects factor and a within-subjects factor, the appropriate analysis is a split-plot ANOVA, also referred to as a mixed-design ANOVA or mixed-factorial ANOVA. This design is extremely common in clinical research because it allows investigators to compare different groups of participants (e.g., treatment vs. control) across repeated measurement occasions (e.g., pre-treatment, post-treatment, and follow-up).

The split-plot ANOVA produces three F-tests: one for the between-subjects main effect, one for the within-subjects main effect, and one for the interaction between the two factors. The interaction is often of greatest theoretical interest because it reveals whether changes over time (or across conditions) differ depending on group membership. For instance, if a treatment group shows significant symptom reduction from pre- to post-treatment but a waitlist group does not, this would manifest as a significant group × time interaction.

Example

A researcher randomly assigns 60 participants to either CBT or a waitlist control condition (between-subjects factor: group) and measures anxiety at baseline, 8 weeks, and 16 weeks (within-subjects factor: time). A split-plot ANOVA is used to analyze the data. The key finding is a significant group × time interaction (F = 8.42, p < .01), indicating that the pattern of anxiety change over the three time points differed between CBT and waitlist participants. Follow-up analyses reveal that the CBT group showed a steady decline in anxiety while the waitlist group remained relatively stable.

Assumptions of the split-plot ANOVA include independence of observations between groups, normality, homogeneity of variance for the between-subjects factor, and sphericity for the within-subjects factor (tested via Mauchly's test). When sphericity is violated, corrections such as Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt are applied to the degrees of freedom.

Post Hoc Tests

When ANOVA indicates significant differences, post hoc tests are used to determine which specific groups differ. Common post hoc tests include:

  • Tukey's HSD: Controls familywise error rate; most commonly used
  • Bonferroni: Conservative; divides α by number of comparisons
  • Scheffé: Most conservative; appropriate for complex comparisons
  • Fisher's LSD: Least conservative; does not control familywise error well

Extensions of ANOVA: ANCOVA, MANOVA, and MANCOVA

In the previous section, we saw how ANOVA allows us to compare means across multiple groups and how post hoc tests pinpoint which specific groups differ. But real-world research is rarely so tidy. Imagine you're comparing three therapy approaches for anxiety, and you discover that one group happened to start with significantly higher baseline anxiety than the others. Or suppose you're not just measuring anxiety—you're also tracking depression, sleep quality, and daily functioning. The basic ANOVA framework, powerful as it is, was not designed to handle these complications. This is where three critical extensions come in, each tailored to a specific research scenario that clinicians and researchers routinely encounter.

These extensions either add covariates to control for pre-existing differences, accommodate multiple outcome measures simultaneously, or do both at once. Understanding when and why to use each one is essential for both the EPPP and for reading clinical research critically (Tabachnick & Fidell, 2019).

Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA)

Here's a common frustration in clinical research: you've randomly assigned participants to three different treatment groups, but despite randomization, the groups aren't perfectly equivalent at baseline. One group's average pre-treatment depression score is noticeably higher than the others. When you compare post-treatment scores, how do you know whether the differences reflect your treatments or simply the head start one group had? This is exactly the problem that ANCOVA was built to solve. By blending the logic of ANOVA with the precision of regression, ANCOVA lets you statistically adjust for pre-existing differences on continuous variables called covariates. In essence, it asks: "If all groups had started at the same level on the covariate, would their outcomes still differ?"

The covariate functions like a statistical equalizer. By partialing out its influence, ANCOVA simultaneously accomplishes two goals: it removes the confounding effect of the covariate, and it reduces within-group error variance—the "noise" that makes it harder to detect real group differences. Less noise means more statistical power, which means you're more likely to detect a genuine treatment effect if one exists. However, ANCOVA carries an important assumption that is frequently tested on the EPPP: homogeneity of regression slopes. This means the relationship between the covariate and the dependent variable must be roughly the same across all groups. If therapy type moderates the relationship between pre-treatment anxiety and post-treatment anxiety (i.e., the slopes differ), ANCOVA's adjustments become misleading.

ANCOVA Key Points

  • Purpose: Statistically control for confounding variables that cannot be controlled experimentally
  • Covariate requirement: The covariate must be measured before treatment (pre-treatment) so it is not affected by the independent variable
  • Effect: Reduces within-group error variance, increasing statistical power
  • Assumption: Homogeneity of regression slopes—the relationship between the covariate and the DV must be similar across groups

ANCOVA Example

A researcher compares the effectiveness of three therapy approaches on post-treatment anxiety scores. Because groups may differ in pre-treatment anxiety, the researcher uses pre-treatment anxiety as a covariate in an ANCOVA. This statistically adjusts post-treatment means to reflect what they would be if all groups had started with equal pre-treatment anxiety levels. For instance, if CBT group participants happened to start with lower anxiety, ANCOVA adjusts their post-treatment mean upward and adjusts the other groups' means accordingly—giving a fairer picture of each treatment's true impact.

Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA)

Now consider a different complication. You're evaluating a new group therapy program and you want to assess its effects on depression, anxiety, and quality of life simultaneously. Your first instinct might be to run three separate ANOVAs—one for each outcome. But here's the problem: each time you run a statistical test at α = .05, you accept a 5% chance of a Type I error. Run three tests, and the probability of making at least one Type I error climbs to roughly 14%. Run ten tests and you're virtually guaranteed a false positive. This is the familywise error rate problem, and it's a serious threat to the integrity of your conclusions.

MANOVA solves this by evaluating multiple dependent variables in a single omnibus test. Rather than examining each DV in isolation, MANOVA creates an optimal linear combination of the DVs and tests whether groups differ on that composite. Think of it this way: instead of asking three separate questions ("Do groups differ on depression? On anxiety? On quality of life?"), MANOVA asks one elegant question: "Do groups differ on the best weighted combination of all three outcomes?" This approach not only controls the familywise error rate but also captures relationships among the DVs that separate ANOVAs would miss entirely.

MANOVA produces several possible test statistics, and knowing which to report is a favorite EPPP topic. Wilks' Lambda is the most commonly reported—it's based on the ratio of within-group to total variance in a multivariate sense, with smaller values indicating greater group separation. Pillai's Trace is the most robust to violations of assumptions such as unequal variance-covariance matrices and is often recommended when sample sizes are unequal or assumptions are questionable. When the omnibus MANOVA is significant, researchers need follow-up analyses to determine which specific DVs contribute to the group difference. The preferred approach is discriminant function analysis, because it accounts for the intercorrelations among DVs rather than treating them as independent—which, after all, was the whole reason for using MANOVA in the first place.

MANOVA Key Points

  • Purpose: Compare groups on multiple DVs simultaneously while controlling familywise Type I error rate
  • Test statistics: Wilks' Lambda (most commonly reported; based on ratio of determinants), Pillai's Trace (most robust to violations of assumptions), Hotelling's Trace, Roy's Largest Root
  • Assumptions: Multivariate normality, homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices (Box's M test), independence of observations
  • Follow-up analyses: If the omnibus MANOVA is significant, follow up with discriminant function analysis (preferred, as it accounts for DV intercorrelations) or individual ANOVAs on each DV with Bonferroni correction

MANOVA Example

A researcher compares two therapy groups on three outcome measures simultaneously: depression, anxiety, and quality of life. Running three separate ANOVAs would inflate the Type I error rate (approximately 1 - .95^3 = .14 rather than .05). MANOVA tests all three DVs in one analysis, maintaining the overall alpha at .05. The researcher reports Wilks' Lambda and, if significant, conducts follow-up analyses to determine which DVs contribute to the group difference. Suppose the MANOVA yields a significant Wilks' Lambda of .72—this means the groups account for 28% of the multivariate variance across the combined outcomes, and the researcher can now confidently examine which specific outcomes drive that difference.

Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA)

What happens when you face both complications at once—multiple outcome measures and pre-existing group differences you need to control for? This is where MANCOVA enters the picture. As its name suggests, MANCOVA combines the features of both MANOVA and ANCOVA—it analyzes multiple dependent variables simultaneously while statistically controlling for one or more covariates. Picture a large clinical trial comparing medication, psychotherapy, and a combined treatment across outcomes including symptom severity, social functioning, and treatment satisfaction. The groups differ at baseline in illness duration and socioeconomic status. MANCOVA handles all of this in a single analysis: multiple DVs evaluated together while adjusting for the influence of both covariates.

MANCOVA inherits the assumptions of both MANOVA (multivariate normality, homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices) and ANCOVA (homogeneity of regression slopes for each covariate across groups). Because it combines the complexities of both techniques, MANCOVA requires larger sample sizes and careful attention to assumption checking. On the EPPP, the key is recognizing when a research scenario calls for MANCOVA: look for the combination of multiple measured outcomes and one or more continuous confounds that need statistical control.

When to Use Each Extension

  • ANCOVA: One DV + need to control for a covariate (e.g., comparing treatment groups on post-test scores while controlling for pre-test)
  • MANOVA: Multiple DVs + no covariates needed (e.g., comparing treatment groups on depression, anxiety, and functioning simultaneously)
  • MANCOVA: Multiple DVs + need to control for a covariate (e.g., comparing treatment groups on multiple outcomes while controlling for baseline severity)

A helpful mnemonic for keeping these extensions straight: the "M" in MANOVA and MANCOVA signals multiple DVs, while the "C" in ANCOVA and MANCOVA signals covariate control. If a question describes both features—multiple outcomes and a confound to adjust for—MANCOVA is the answer. These three extensions, together with the basic ANOVA covered in the previous section, form a flexible family of techniques that can handle the vast majority of group comparison designs you'll encounter in clinical research and on the EPPP.

Non-Parametric Tests

Every parametric test we have discussed — t-tests, ANOVA, ANCOVA, MANOVA — rests on assumptions about the data: normally distributed populations, interval or ratio scales, homogeneous variances. But what happens when those assumptions are clearly violated? A researcher working with ranked preference data, severely skewed distributions, or small samples that cannot justify normality assumptions needs an alternative toolkit. Non-parametric tests (also called distribution-free tests) fill this role, providing valid inference without requiring the stringent assumptions of their parametric counterparts.

Parametric Test Non-Parametric Alternative Use Case
Independent t-test Mann-Whitney U Compare two independent groups
Paired t-test Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Compare two related samples
One-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis H Compare three+ independent groups
Repeated measures ANOVA Friedman Test Compare three+ related samples

A key trade-off to understand is that parametric tests are generally more powerful than their nonparametric counterparts. Because parametric tests make use of all the quantitative information in the data—means, variances, and the precise distances between scores—they extract more statistical information from the same dataset. This means that when an effect truly exists in the population, a parametric test is more likely to detect it (i.e., correctly reject a false null hypothesis). Nonparametric tests, by contrast, typically convert raw scores to ranks or frequencies, discarding some of the information contained in interval- or ratio-level data. This loss of information reduces their sensitivity to real effects. For this reason, nonparametric tests should generally be reserved for situations where parametric assumptions are clearly untenable—such as when data are ordinal, sample sizes are very small and distributions are markedly skewed, or when the dependent variable is categorical. When assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance are reasonably met, parametric tests remain the preferred choice because of their superior ability to detect true differences.

Chi-Square Tests

The chi-square (χ²) test is used for categorical data:

  • Chi-square goodness of fit: Tests whether observed frequencies match expected frequencies in a single categorical variable
  • Chi-square test of independence: Tests whether two categorical variables are related (using a contingency table)

The chi-square test of independence is equally applicable when one categorical variable has only two levels. For instance, a researcher interested in whether gender (male vs. female) is associated with department of admission (six departments) would arrange the data in a 2 × 6 contingency table. Each cell represents the count of applicants of a given gender who were accepted into a specific department. Because both variables are categorical and the research question concerns the relationship between them, a multiple-sample chi-square test is the correct analytic strategy. A significant result would indicate that the proportion of males and females admitted is not uniform across departments—suggesting that gender and department acceptance are statistically dependent. This design parallels the classic Berkeley admissions study, which famously illustrated how aggregate patterns can differ from department-level patterns (Simpson's paradox), underscoring the importance of examining contingency tables at the appropriate level of analysis.

Clinical Note

When expected cell frequencies are small (conventionally below 5 in more than 20% of cells), the chi-square approximation becomes unreliable. In such cases, researchers may collapse categories, collect additional data, or use Fisher's exact test (particularly for 2 × 2 tables) to obtain accurate p-values.

Effect Sizes

After running a significance test — whether parametric or non-parametric — you know whether an effect is statistically significant. But significance alone can be deeply misleading. With a sample of 10,000 participants, even a half-point difference on a 100-point depression scale can reach p < .05. Does that mean the treatment is clinically meaningful? Not necessarily. This is why modern research standards and the APA Publication Manual insist on reporting effect sizes, which quantify the magnitude of a difference or relationship independent of sample size, telling us how large the effect actually is.

Cohen's d

Cohen's d measures the standardized difference between two means, expressed in standard deviation units. It is commonly used with t-tests.

Cohen's d Interpretation
0.2 Small effect
0.5 Medium effect
0.8 Large effect

Eta-Squared (η²)

Eta-squared represents the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable(s). It is commonly used with ANOVA.

η² Interpretation
.01 Small effect
.06 Medium effect
.14 Large effect

Other Effect Size Measures

  • Partial eta-squared (η²ₚ): Variance explained by one IV while controlling for other IVs; used in factorial ANOVA
  • Omega-squared (ω²): Less biased estimate of population effect size than η²
  • Odds ratio: Used with categorical outcomes; commonly reported in logistic regression
  • r: Correlation coefficient itself is an effect size measure

Statistical vs. Clinical Significance

Clinical significance refers to whether a treatment effect is meaningful in practice. A treatment might produce a statistically significant reduction in symptoms that is too small to matter clinically. Effect sizes, confidence intervals, and measures like the Reliable Change Index (RCI) help determine whether changes are clinically meaningful, not just statistically detectable.

The Reliable Change Index (RCI), developed by Jacobson and Truax (1991), provides a method for evaluating whether the change in an individual client's score from pre-treatment to post-treatment represents genuine improvement (or deterioration) rather than fluctuation attributable to measurement error. The RCI is calculated by dividing the difference between pre- and post-treatment scores by the standard error of the difference, which is derived from the test's reliability coefficient and the standard deviation of scores. An RCI value exceeding ±1.96 indicates that the observed change is statistically reliable at the .05 level—meaning there is less than a 5% probability that the change resulted from imprecision of the measure alone. This metric is particularly valuable in clinical practice and single-case research where the focus is on individual-level change rather than group-level averages. When combined with the concept of clinically significant change—which requires that the client's post-treatment score falls within the functional (non-clinical) population range—the RCI provides a comprehensive framework for determining whether therapy has produced meaningful real-world benefit for a specific client.

Confidence Intervals

Effect sizes tell us how large an effect is, but they are still point estimates — single numbers that carry their own sampling error. A more complete picture comes from wrapping that estimate in a range that conveys the precision of our knowledge. A confidence interval (CI) provides exactly that: a range of values within which the population parameter is likely to fall. A 95% CI means that if we repeated the study many times, 95% of the calculated intervals would contain the true population parameter.

Bayesian Inference: An Alternative Framework

The hypothesis testing and confidence interval approaches discussed throughout this chapter fall within the frequentist tradition of statistics, which defines probability as the long-run frequency of events across repeated sampling. An alternative framework—Bayesian inference—defines probability as a degree of belief and provides a fundamentally different method for drawing conclusions from data.

Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem is a mathematical rule for updating beliefs in light of new evidence. It specifies how to combine a prior probability distribution (what we believed about a parameter before collecting data) with a likelihood function (how probable the observed data are under different parameter values) to produce a posterior probability distribution (our updated belief about the parameter after seeing the data).

In Bayesian analysis, parameters such as a treatment effect are treated as having probability distributions rather than being fixed, unknown constants. Before a study begins, the researcher specifies a prior distribution reflecting existing knowledge or theoretical expectations. After data collection, the prior is mathematically combined with the likelihood of the observed results to yield the posterior distribution. This posterior directly answers questions like "What is the probability that the treatment effect exceeds zero?"—a question that frequentist methods cannot address.

A key output of Bayesian analysis is the credible interval, which differs conceptually from a confidence interval. A 95% credible interval indicates that there is a 95% probability the parameter falls within that range, given the data and the prior. By contrast, a 95% confidence interval means that 95% of intervals constructed from repeated sampling would contain the true value—a statement about the procedure, not about the specific interval obtained.

Feature Frequentist Approach Bayesian Approach
Definition of probability Long-run relative frequency Degree of belief or certainty
Parameters Fixed but unknown values Random variables with distributions
Prior information Not formally incorporated Explicitly specified as prior distribution
Interval estimate Confidence interval (about the procedure) Credible interval (about the parameter)
Result interpretation Probability of data given H₀ Probability of hypothesis given the data

Clinical Note

Bayesian methods are increasingly used in clinical research, particularly in adaptive clinical trials and meta-analyses where accumulating evidence across studies naturally fits the framework of updating prior beliefs. For the EPPP, understand that Bayesian analysis yields direct probability statements about parameters by incorporating prior knowledge, whereas frequentist p-values only express the probability of obtaining the observed (or more extreme) data under the null hypothesis.

Confidence intervals provide more information than p-values alone because they indicate both the precision of the estimate (width of the interval) and the direction and magnitude of effects. Narrow intervals indicate precise estimates; wide intervals indicate imprecise estimates.

Interpreting Confidence Intervals

A study finds that the mean difference in depression scores between treatment and control is 5 points, with a 95% CI of [2.3, 7.7].

Interpretation: We are 95% confident that the true population difference lies between 2.3 and 7.7 points. Because the interval does not include zero, the effect is statistically significant at α = .05.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

External Validity and Generalizability

While much of inferential statistics focuses on internal validity—whether we can draw causal conclusions from our data—exam items also test understanding of external validity, which concerns whether research findings can be generalized beyond the specific study conditions. External validity asks: Do these results apply to other populations, settings, times, and outcome measures? A study may demonstrate a statistically significant treatment effect with strong internal controls, yet lack generalizability if the sample was narrowly defined (e.g., only college students), the setting was artificial (e.g., a highly controlled laboratory), or the measures were idiosyncratic. Threats to external validity include selection bias in sampling, reactive effects of the experimental arrangement (e.g., participants behaving differently because they know they are being studied), and interactions between treatment and specific participant characteristics. For EPPP purposes, remember that random assignment strengthens internal validity, while random selection from the population of interest strengthens external validity. Both are essential for drawing conclusions that are simultaneously trustworthy and broadly applicable.

Commonly Tested Topics for the EPPP

  • Type I Error: Rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive); probability = α
  • Type II Error: Failing to reject a false null hypothesis (false negative); probability = β
  • Power: 1 - β; probability of correctly rejecting false H₀; typically .80 desired
  • Increasing Power: Larger n, larger effect, higher α, lower variability, one-tailed test
  • p-value: Probability of data given H₀ is true; NOT probability H₀ is true
  • Independent t-test: Between-subjects, two groups
  • Paired t-test: Within-subjects, same participants measured twice
  • ANOVA: Compare 3+ groups; F-ratio = between variance / within variance
  • Interaction: Effect of one IV depends on level of another IV
  • Post hoc tests: Determine which groups differ after significant ANOVA
  • Cohen's d: Small = .2, Medium = .5, Large = .8
  • Eta-squared: Small = .01, Medium = .06, Large = .14
  • ANCOVA: ANOVA + covariate; statistically controls confounds; covariate must be measured before treatment; assumes homogeneity of regression slopes
  • MANOVA: ANOVA with multiple DVs; controls Type I error inflation; key statistics are Wilks' Lambda and Pillai's Trace; follow up with discriminant function analysis
  • MANCOVA: Multiple DVs + covariate(s); combines MANOVA and ANCOVA
  • Statistical vs. Clinical Significance: Statistical significance doesn't guarantee practical importance
  • Non-parametric alternatives: Mann-Whitney U, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis, Friedman
