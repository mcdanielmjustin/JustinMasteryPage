Cognitive Development: The Sensorimotor Stage

In the previous chapter, we traced the biological journey from a single-celled zygote to a breathing, reflexive newborn. But a newborn is more than a bundle of reflexes waiting to be triggered. From the first moments of life, infants are actively working to make sense of a world that bombards them with sensation. How does a creature who cannot speak, walk, or even deliberately reach for an object begin to build an understanding of reality? Jean Piaget spent decades observing his own three children to answer that question, and his account of the sensorimotor stage (birth to approximately 2 years) remains one of the most influential descriptions of early cognitive development ever proposed. During this period, infants construct knowledge not through language or abstract thought but through physical actions and sensory experiences -- by grasping, sucking, shaking, dropping, and watching what happens next.

The Six Substages in Detail

Piaget divided the sensorimotor stage into six substages, each marked by increasingly sophisticated cognitive achievements. Understanding the progression through these substages is essential In practice, because exam questions frequently present clinical milestones and ask which substage they represent.

  1. Reflexive schemes (0–1 month): Behavior is limited to innate reflexes such as sucking, grasping, and rooting. The newborn sucks anything that touches the lips without discrimination. There is no intentionality or awareness that the self and the world are separate entities.
  2. Primary circular reactions (1–4 months): The infant begins to repeat pleasurable actions centered on its own body. A baby who accidentally brings a thumb to the mouth and finds the sucking sensation pleasurable will repeat the action again and again. These reactions are "primary" because they involve the infant's own body and "circular" because they are repeated in a loop.
  3. Secondary circular reactions (4–8 months): The infant now repeats actions that produce interesting effects on the external environment. A baby who accidentally kicks a mobile and notices it spin will kick again deliberately. The crucial shift here is outward -- the infant has discovered that actions can make things happen in the world beyond the body.
  4. Coordination of secondary schemes (8–12 months): For the first time, behavior becomes truly intentional and goal-directed. The infant can combine two separate schemes to achieve a desired end -- for example, pushing aside a barrier (one scheme) to reach a hidden toy (another scheme). This is means-end coordination, and it marks the beginning of genuine problem-solving. This is also the substage where the famous A-not-B error appears.
  5. Tertiary circular reactions (12–18 months): The toddler becomes a "little scientist," actively experimenting with new ways of acting on objects. Rather than simply repeating a successful action, the child deliberately varies the action to observe different results -- dropping a toy from different heights, banging objects on different surfaces, pouring water from different angles. This systematic experimentation drives rapid learning about cause and effect.
  6. Mental representation (18–24 months): The crowning achievement of the sensorimotor stage. The child develops the capacity for internal depiction of objects and events, enabling deferred imitation (reproducing a behavior observed earlier, without the model present) and the beginnings of symbolic thought. A child at this substage can solve problems mentally rather than through physical trial-and-error, and pretend play emerges -- a block becomes a telephone, a banana becomes a walkie-talkie.
Clinical Scenario: Substage Recognition

A pediatric psychologist observes a 14-month-old who drops a spoon from her highchair, watches it fall, then picks up a cracker and drops it from the same height, then drops a toy car from a different angle. The child giggles as each object lands differently. This systematic variation of actions to observe novel effects is the hallmark of substage 5 (tertiary circular reactions) -- the "little scientist" phase of the sensorimotor period.

Object Permanence: The Signature Achievement

If there is one concept from Piaget's sensorimotor stage that appears on virtually every EPPP exam, it is object permanence -- the understanding that objects continue to exist even when they are no longer visible. For the young infant, out of sight is literally out of mind. When a toy is covered with a cloth, the three-month-old behaves as if it has ceased to exist, showing no attempt to retrieve it. The gradual acquisition of object permanence across the sensorimotor substages reveals the infant's developing capacity to maintain mental representations of the physical world.

Object Permanence

The understanding that objects continue to exist even when they are out of sight. This is the hallmark achievement of the sensorimotor stage. It begins to emerge around 4–8 months (searching for partially hidden objects) and is fully developed by 18–24 months, when the child can represent invisible displacements -- tracking an object that has been moved through a series of hidden locations.

The A-not-B Error

One of Piaget's most replicated and theoretically important observations occurs during substage 4, when object permanence is still developing. The A-not-B error (sometimes called the "perseverative search error") demonstrates that the 8- to 12-month-old's understanding of object location is still fragile and tied to prior motor actions rather than to a fully independent mental representation.

A-not-B Error

An error observed around 8–12 months in which an infant who has repeatedly found an object at location A continues to search there even after watching the object being hidden at location B. The infant's search behavior appears to be driven by the motor habit of reaching toward A rather than by an updated representation of the object's true location. This error reflects immature object representation and is one of Piaget's most replicated findings.

Exam Example

A researcher hides a toy under blanket A several times, and the 9-month-old successfully retrieves it. Then, in full view of the infant, the researcher hides the toy under blanket B. The infant searches under blanket A. This is the classic A-not-B error, characteristic of Piaget's substage 4 (coordination of secondary schemes).

Challenges to Piaget: Baillargeon's Violation of Expectation

Piaget's account has not gone unchallenged. Beginning in the 1980s, Renée Baillargeon and colleagues developed the violation-of-expectation paradigm, which uses looking time rather than manual search to assess what infants know about hidden objects. The logic is elegant: if an infant looks longer at an event that should be impossible (a screen appearing to pass through a solid block that was placed behind it), the extended gaze suggests that the infant expected the block to still be there -- that is, the infant possesses some understanding of object permanence that Piaget's manual-search tasks failed to detect.

Baillargeon's research demonstrated that infants as young as 3.5 to 5 months show surprise at physically impossible events, suggesting that the foundations of object permanence emerge considerably earlier than Piaget proposed. This finding has profound implications: Piaget's original tasks may have underestimated infant knowledge by requiring motor skills (reaching, searching) that young infants have not yet developed. The infant may "know" the object is there but lack the motor coordination and planning ability to demonstrate that knowledge through action.

Clinical Application: Early Cognitive Assessment

The distinction between what infants know and what they can demonstrate through action has direct implications for early cognitive assessment. The Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development (now in its fourth edition) is the most widely used standardized assessment for infants aged 1–42 months, evaluating cognitive, language, motor, social-emotional, and adaptive behavior domains. Clinicians should be aware that performance on motor-dependent items may underestimate cognitive ability in infants with motor delays or physical disabilities. A child with cerebral palsy, for example, may fail to retrieve a hidden object not because they lack object permanence but because they cannot coordinate the reaching and grasping movements required by the task. This is why Baillargeon's looking-time research matters clinically -- it reminds us that competence and performance are not the same thing, particularly in the earliest months of life.

Exam Example: Baillargeon

In a violation-of-expectation experiment, a 4-month-old watches a screen rotate toward a block placed behind it. In the "impossible" condition, the screen appears to rotate through the space the block occupies. The infant looks significantly longer at the impossible event than at a control event where the screen stops appropriately. This extended looking time suggests the infant expected the block to continue existing behind the screen -- evidence of earlier object permanence than Piaget's manual search tasks would detect.

Brain Development and Motor Milestones

The cognitive achievements we just explored -- from reflexive sucking to mental representation, from the A-not-B error to the beginnings of symbolic thought -- do not occur in a biological vacuum. They unfold against the backdrop of an organ undergoing the most explosive transformation of any period in the lifespan: the developing brain. The infant brain undergoes extraordinary growth during the first two years of life, doubling in weight by the first birthday and reaching roughly 75% of adult size by age 2. This growth is driven by synaptogenesis (the formation of new synaptic connections) and myelination (the coating of neural axons with myelin to increase signal speed).

Synaptic Pruning

The infant brain produces far more synapses than it will ultimately retain—a process of neural overproduction that peaks at different times in different brain regions (visual cortex around 3–4 months, prefrontal cortex not until the second year). Synaptic pruning is the experience-dependent elimination of underused or redundant connections, sculpting the brain's architecture based on environmental input. This process follows the principle of "use it or lose it" and is critical for efficient neural functioning. By adolescence, approximately 40% of the synapses present in early childhood will have been pruned away, leaving a more streamlined and efficient neural network.

Clinical Relevance

The concept of experience-expectant development refers to neural processes that require typical environmental input (visual stimulation, language exposure, social interaction) to develop normally. Experience-dependent development involves the formation of synapses in response to unique individual experiences. Both processes underscore the importance of enriched early environments for optimal brain development. When a clinician encounters a child who has experienced severe early deprivation -- institutionalization, neglect, or sensory loss -- understanding these two mechanisms helps explain why intervention timing matters so profoundly and why some capacities may be harder to recover than others.

Motor Development

Motor milestones follow a predictable cephalocaudal (head-to-toe) and proximodistal (center-to-periphery) sequence. These principles mean that head control develops before trunk control, and reaching develops before fine finger manipulation:

  • 2–3 months: Lifts head while on stomach
  • 4–5 months: Rolls over
  • 6–7 months: Sits without support
  • 8–9 months: Crawls; pulls to standing
  • 10–12 months: Stands alone; takes first steps
  • 13–18 months: Walks independently; begins to run

While the sequence of motor milestones is largely invariant across cultures, the timing can vary considerably across individuals and cultural contexts. Motor development reflects the maturation of the nervous system, muscular strength, body proportions, and opportunities for practice. Cross-cultural research has shown, for example, that infants in some West African cultures who receive systematic motor practice sit and walk earlier than Western norms, while infants in cultures that restrict movement (such as the traditional Hopi practice of cradleboarding) may show temporary motor delays that resolve once restriction ends.

Dynamic Systems Theory of Motor Development

Esther Thelen's dynamic systems theory challenged the traditional maturational view by proposing that motor development emerges from the interaction of multiple systems -- neural maturation, body mass and proportions, muscle strength, motivation, and environmental support -- rather than from a simple unfolding of a genetic blueprint. This theory explains why motor milestones are not rigidly fixed: the same motor achievement (walking, for instance) requires different solutions from infants with different body proportions and strengths.

EPPP Exam Focus Points

  • Sensorimotor substages: Know all six substages and their approximate ages. Exam questions frequently present a behavioral description and ask you to identify the substage. Primary circular reactions involve the infant's own body; secondary involve the external environment; tertiary involve deliberate experimentation.
  • Object permanence: The hallmark achievement of the sensorimotor stage. Develops gradually from substage 3 (partial) through substage 6 (complete, including invisible displacements). Fully mature by 18–24 months.
  • A-not-B error: Occurs in substage 4 (8–12 months). The infant searches at location A even after watching the object being hidden at B. Reflects immature object representation and the pull of prior motor habits.
  • Baillargeon's violation-of-expectation: Looking-time studies suggest object permanence emerges earlier (3.5–5 months) than Piaget's manual-search tasks indicated. Piaget may have underestimated infant competence by relying on motor-dependent tasks.
  • Deferred imitation: Reproducing a previously observed behavior after a delay, without the model present. Emerges in substage 6 and marks the transition to symbolic thought.
  • Brain development: Synaptogenesis followed by pruning; experience-expectant vs. experience-dependent processes. Myelination proceeds in a cephalocaudal direction and continues into adolescence.
  • Motor milestones: Follow cephalocaudal and proximodistal patterns. Sequence is invariant; timing varies across individuals and cultures.
  • Bayley Scales: The most widely used standardized infant cognitive assessment (ages 1–42 months). Evaluates cognitive, language, motor, social-emotional, and adaptive behavior domains.
  • Dynamic systems theory (Thelen): Motor development emerges from the interaction of multiple systems, not just neural maturation. Explains individual variation in milestone timing.
  • Competence vs. performance: Infants may possess cognitive understanding (competence) that they cannot demonstrate through action (performance) due to motor limitations. This distinction is clinically important when assessing infants with physical disabilities.
