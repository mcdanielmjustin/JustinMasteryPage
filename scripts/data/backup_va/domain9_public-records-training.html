Overview of Standard 5: Advertising and Other Public Statements

Imagine browsing a psychology practice's website and encountering a clinician who claims to be "board certified in neuropsychology" when they hold no such credential, or a therapist whose online profile promises "guaranteed results for anxiety in just three sessions." How would a prospective client — someone already vulnerable, already searching for help — know the difference between legitimate expertise and inflated self-promotion? This is precisely the territory that Standard 5 (Advertising and Other Public Statements) of the APA Ethics Code was designed to govern. It addresses truthfulness in all professional representations — from business cards and social media profiles to media interviews and workshop brochures. Psychologists have an obligation to ensure that every public statement about their services, qualifications, credentials, and research is accurate, not deceptive, and does not mislead the public in any way.

At its core, Standard 5 rests on a straightforward ethical principle: the public's ability to make informed decisions about psychological services depends entirely on receiving honest information. Unlike purchasing a consumer product where exaggerated marketing might result in minor disappointment, misleading advertising in psychology can lead individuals to entrust their mental health — sometimes their safety — to a provider who lacks the competence being claimed. A person struggling with substance use disorder who selects a therapist based on falsely advertised expertise in addiction treatment may not only fail to improve but could deteriorate during a critical window for intervention. The stakes of deceptive professional communication are not abstract; they are deeply clinical and deeply human.

This standard is important in practice because violations in advertising can directly harm the public and undermine trust in the profession. The scope of Standard 5 is broader than many candidates initially expect — it does not apply only to paid advertisements or formal marketing materials. It encompasses any public-facing communication, including professional directory listings, curriculum vitae entries, social media bios, statements made during media appearances, and even descriptions offered during informed consent. Understanding Standard 5 requires recognizing that psychologists bear responsibility not only for their own statements but also for reasonable efforts to prevent others (such as office staff, marketing agencies, or media outlets) from making deceptive statements on their behalf. As we move into the specific subsections of Standard 5, keep this overarching principle in mind: the ethical psychologist is not merely avoiding outright lies but is actively ensuring that the public receives a clear, accurate, and complete picture of who they are and what they offer.

Standard 5: Advertising and Other Public Statements

5.01: Avoidance of False or Deceptive Statements

Psychologists do not knowingly make public statements that are false, deceptive, or fraudulent concerning their research, practice, or other work activities. This prohibition applies to:

  • Training, experience, or competence
  • Academic degrees and credentials
  • Institutional or association affiliations
  • Services offered and their scientific or clinical basis
  • Fees charged
  • Publications or research findings

Psychologists do not make false, deceptive, or fraudulent statements concerning the degree of success of their services, and they claim only degrees as credentials that were earned from regionally accredited educational institutions or that are the basis for psychology licensure.

Example

A psychologist advertises a "95% success rate" for treating anxiety disorders without any empirical basis for this claim. This violates Standard 5.01 because it is a deceptive statement about the degree of success of services. Vague or unsubstantiated claims of effectiveness are prohibited.

5.02: Statements by Others

Psychologists who engage others to create or place public statements that promote their professional practice retain professional responsibility for such statements. If psychologists learn of deceptive statements about their work made by others, they make reasonable efforts to correct such statements.

  • Psychologists do not compensate employees of press, radio, television, or other communication media in return for publicity in a news item.
  • A paid advertisement must be identified as such.

5.03: Descriptions of Workshops and Non-Degree-Granting Educational Programs

Psychologists responsible for workshops, seminars, and other non-degree programs ensure that descriptions are accurate regarding:

  • The target audience
  • Educational objectives
  • The presenters and their qualifications
  • Fees and any prerequisites

5.04: Media Presentations

When psychologists provide public advice or comment via print, internet, mail, television, radio, or other electronic media, they take precautions to ensure that statements are:

  • Based on appropriate psychological literature and practice
  • Consistent with the Ethics Code
  • Not indicative of a professional relationship with the recipient

5.05: Testimonials

Psychologists do not solicit testimonials from current therapy clients or other persons who, because of their particular circumstances, are vulnerable to undue influence. This reflects the power differential in the therapy relationship and the potential for coercion, even if unintentional.

EPPP Tip

The prohibition on soliciting testimonials applies to current therapy clients and others vulnerable to undue influence. The standard does not explicitly prohibit testimonials from former clients, but careful ethical analysis is warranted because former clients may remain vulnerable to the influence of their former therapist.

5.06: In-Person Solicitation

Psychologists do not engage, directly or through agents, in uninvited in-person solicitation of business from actual or potential therapy clients or other persons who, because of their particular circumstances, are vulnerable to undue influence. This does not prohibit:

  • General marketing or advertising that is not targeted at vulnerable individuals
  • Providing disaster relief or community outreach services
  • Responding to individuals who initiate contact seeking services

Key Takeaways in clinical practice

Insurance Fraud: Billing for Missed Appointments

A specific ethical and legal concern arises when psychologists bill insurance companies for sessions that did not actually take place. When a client fails to attend a scheduled appointment, the psychologist has not rendered a professional service, and submitting a claim to an insurer for that missed session—without the insurer's explicit authorization—constitutes insurance fraud. This conduct violates both ethical standards regarding honest financial practices and federal or state laws governing fraudulent billing. Some insurance contracts include provisions for no-show fees, but these must be billed in accordance with the specific terms of the payer agreement. Psychologists should review their contracts with third-party payers carefully and charge clients directly for missed appointments according to policies disclosed at the outset of treatment, rather than misrepresenting the nature of a claim to an insurer.

Example

Dr. Patel's client does not appear for a scheduled therapy session. Dr. Patel submits a claim to the client's insurance company using a standard psychotherapy CPT code for that date of service. Because no service was provided and the insurer has not agreed to cover missed appointments, this billing practice constitutes fraud—regardless of whether Dr. Patel views the charge as compensation for reserved time. The ethical and legal approach would be to charge the client a no-show fee consistent with the office's disclosed cancellation policy.

  • All public statements must be truthful and non-deceptive -- psychologists cannot claim unsubstantiated success rates, misleading credentials, or false affiliations.
  • Psychologists retain responsibility for statements made by others on their behalf (Standard 5.02).
  • Testimonials may not be solicited from current therapy clients or others vulnerable to undue influence.
  • In-person solicitation of vulnerable individuals is prohibited, but general marketing is permissible.
  • Workshop and program descriptions must accurately represent objectives, presenters, fees, and prerequisites.
  • Media presentations must be based on appropriate literature and must not imply a professional relationship with the audience.

Overview of Standard 6: Record Keeping and Fees

Imagine a psychologist who sees a client for two years of intensive therapy, carefully documenting session notes, treatment plans, and progress summaries. When the client later requests records for a new provider, the psychologist realizes that months of documentation are missing, fee agreements were never formalized in writing, and several insurance claims contain diagnostic codes that don't match the actual treatment provided. What began as simple administrative oversight has now created a cascade of ethical, legal, and clinical problems — problems that could harm the client, expose the psychologist to board complaints, and undermine public trust in the profession. Scenarios like this are exactly why the APA devoted an entire standard to the seemingly mundane but critically important topics of record keeping and fees.

Standard 6 (Record Keeping and Fees) of the APA Ethics Code addresses the full landscape of documentation practices, fee arrangements, bartering, and billing accuracy. At first glance, these may seem like administrative details far removed from the therapeutic encounter itself. But consider how deeply financial and documentation practices are woven into the fabric of the professional relationship. A client who doesn't understand the fee structure may feel blindsided by an unexpected bill — and that sense of betrayal can rupture therapeutic trust in an instant. A psychologist who fails to maintain adequate records may be unable to ensure continuity of care, defend clinical decisions in a legal proceeding, or provide the documentation a client needs for disability benefits or custody evaluations. These provisions exist to protect both clients and the integrity of the profession by ensuring transparent financial dealings and proper record management throughout every phase of service delivery.

It is worth noting that fee-related ethical violations are not merely technicalities — they can directly exploit clients and cause real harm to the therapeutic relationship. Consider the inherent power differential in therapy: clients are often vulnerable, may be experiencing financial stress alongside psychological distress, and may feel unable to question or challenge a clinician's billing practices. Standard 6 establishes guardrails that require psychologists to be forthright about costs, honest in their billing, and conscientious in their documentation. These principles apply whether a psychologist is working in private practice, a hospital system, a forensic setting, or a community mental health clinic.

Standard 6 is important in practice because the board recognizes that ethical competence extends beyond the therapy room and into the business practices that frame psychological services. Examinees should expect questions that test their understanding of when and how to establish fee arrangements, the conditions under which bartering may be ethically permissible, the requirements for maintaining and disposing of records, and the obligations surrounding accurate billing and reporting. As we move through the specific subsections of Standard 6, keep in mind the overarching principle: transparency and honesty in record keeping and financial dealings are not ancillary to good clinical practice — they are fundamental to it.

Standard 6: Record Keeping and Fees

6.01: Documentation of Professional and Scientific Work

Psychologists create and maintain documentation in sufficient detail to facilitate provision of services later by themselves or other professionals, to allow for replication of research design and analysis, to meet institutional requirements, to ensure accuracy of billing and payments, and to ensure compliance with law.

6.02: Maintenance, Dissemination, and Disposal of Records

Psychologists maintain confidentiality in creating, storing, accessing, transferring, and disposing of records. They are responsible for:

  • Planning in advance to facilitate the appropriate transfer and protection of records in the event of withdrawal from practice, incapacity, or death
  • Maintaining records for the period required by applicable laws and regulations
  • Securely disposing of records to protect confidentiality

6.03: Withholding Records for Nonpayment

Psychologists may not withhold records under their control that are requested and needed for a client's emergency treatment solely because payment has not been received. This provision prioritizes client welfare over financial considerations in emergency situations.

Key Rule

In a non-emergency situation, psychologists may have legal grounds to withhold records for nonpayment, depending on state law. But in an emergency, records needed for treatment must be provided regardless of payment status. The EPPP frequently tests this distinction.

6.04: Fees and Financial Arrangements

As early as feasible in a professional or scientific relationship, psychologists and recipients of psychological services reach an agreement specifying compensation and billing arrangements. Fee agreements should include:

  • The fee amount and basis (per session, per hour, per assessment)
  • Payment schedule and accepted methods of payment
  • Policies regarding missed appointments and cancellations
  • Procedures for handling insurance claims and copayments
  • Consequences of nonpayment (including potential use of collection agencies)

Psychologists do not misrepresent their fees. If limitations to services can be anticipated because of limitations in financing, this is discussed with the client as early as feasible.

6.05: Bartering with Clients

Bartering is the acceptance of goods, services, or other nonmonetary remuneration from clients in return for psychological services. Psychologists may barter only if:

  1. It is not clinically contraindicated, and
  2. The resulting arrangement is not exploitative
Example

A client who is a carpenter offers to build bookshelves for the psychologist's office in exchange for therapy sessions. This arrangement may be acceptable if: (a) the exchange is fair and agreed upon by both parties, (b) there is no power imbalance that could lead to exploitation, and (c) the arrangement is not clinically contraindicated (e.g., the client is not in treatment for issues related to self-worth or financial dependency). The psychologist should document the arrangement and consult with a colleague.

EPPP Tip

The APA Ethics Code does NOT prohibit bartering outright. It allows bartering when it is not exploitative and not clinically contraindicated. This is a nuanced position -- many EPPP questions test whether you know that bartering is permissible under the right conditions, especially in rural or underserved communities where financial options may be limited.

6.06: Accuracy in Reports to Payors and Funding Sources

Psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure the accurate reporting of the nature of the service provided, the fees, charges, or payments, and where applicable, the identity of the provider, the findings, and the diagnosis. They do not knowingly make fraudulent claims, which includes:

  • Billing for services not rendered
  • Misrepresenting the type of service provided
  • Providing an inaccurate diagnosis to justify insurance reimbursement
  • Billing at a higher rate than the service warrants (upcoding)

6.07: Referrals and Fees

When psychologists pay, receive payment from, or divide fees with another professional, the payment must be based on the services provided (clinical, consultative, administrative, or other) and is not based on the referral itself. Psychologists do not receive or pay referral fees (also called fee splitting or kickbacks) simply for sending or receiving client referrals.

Key Takeaways in clinical practice

  • Records needed for emergency treatment may not be withheld for nonpayment (Standard 6.03).
  • Bartering is permissible when it is not clinically contraindicated and not exploitative.
  • Fee arrangements should be established as early as feasible in the professional relationship.
  • Referral fees (paying or receiving fees solely for the referral) are prohibited; fees must be based on services actually provided.
  • Fraudulent billing practices (upcoding, billing for services not rendered, inaccurate diagnoses for reimbursement) are serious ethical violations.
  • Psychologists must plan in advance for the transfer and protection of records in the event of withdrawal from practice, incapacity, or death.

Overview of Standard 7: Education and Training

Imagine a first-year doctoral student who receives harsh, unexplained feedback on a clinical practicum evaluation — feedback that seems to reflect her supervisor's personal frustration rather than any objective assessment of her skills. She wants to challenge it, but she also knows this supervisor will be grading her for the next two semesters and writing her internship recommendation letters. Where does she turn? What protections exist for her? Now consider the reverse: a training director who must address a student's emerging impairment that could endanger future clients, yet wants to handle the situation fairly and without overstepping ethical boundaries. These are the kinds of dilemmas that make Standard 7 (Education and Training) of the APA Ethics Code not merely an administrative guideline but a vital safeguard for everyone involved in the formation of new psychologists.

Standard 7 governs the ethical conduct of psychologists who design academic programs, teach courses, supervise trainees, and evaluate student performance. Its provisions span a wide range of concerns — from the accuracy and currency of what is taught in the classroom, to the boundaries around requiring personal therapy or self-disclosure from students, to the critical prohibition against sexual relationships between educators and those they train. Each of these provisions reflects a single, unifying principle: the training relationship is characterized by an inherent and significant power differential, and that imbalance demands explicit ethical guardrails to prevent exploitation, favoritism, and harm.

If you have already studied Standard 3 (Multiple Relationships) and Standard 10 (Therapy), you will notice familiar themes here — boundary management, informed consent, and the responsible use of authority. Standard 7 applies those broad principles specifically to the academic and supervisory context, where the stakes are uniquely high: students depend on faculty for grades, recommendations, and ultimately the professional gatekeeping decisions that determine who enters the field. A supervisor's ethical lapse in this context can derail a career, compromise a student's wellbeing, or — if impaired trainees are not properly identified — put future clients at risk.

As you work through the subsections that follow, pay attention to how each provision balances two sometimes competing obligations: protecting the welfare and autonomy of students and supervisees, while also upholding the profession's responsibility to ensure that only competent, ethical practitioners advance through the training pipeline. Understanding this tension is essential not only in clinical practice but for the everyday ethical reasoning you will use throughout your career as a psychologist.

Both the APA Ethics Code (Standard 8.10b) and the Canadian Code of Ethics require psychologists to take corrective action when they discover errors in their published research. If a psychologist becomes aware that a statistical analysis was reported incorrectly in a published article—whether due to a computational mistake, a data entry error, or an inadvertent misstatement of results—the psychologist has an ethical obligation to pursue reasonable steps to correct the public record. Common corrective actions include publishing a formal correction (erratum), issuing a retraction if the error substantially undermines the findings, or taking other appropriate measures to alert readers to the inaccuracy.

This obligation exists regardless of whether the error was intentional. The ethical mandate stems from the profession's commitment to honesty and the recognition that other researchers, clinicians, and policymakers may rely on published findings to inform their work. Allowing an erroneous result to stand uncorrected risks propagating misinformation, potentially influencing clinical practice, subsequent research, or public policy in harmful ways. The duty to correct errors reflects the broader ethical principles of integrity and responsibility to the scientific community and to the public.

Example

Dr. Hoffman publishes a study on treatment outcomes for social anxiety disorder. Six months later, while preparing a follow-up study, she realizes that a coding error inflated the effect sizes reported in her original article. Ethically, Dr. Hoffman must contact the journal editor and submit a formal correction notice so that readers are made aware of the accurate results.

Standard 8.12 (Publication Credit) establishes clear guidelines regarding authorship credit for scholarly work. A particularly important provision addresses the situation in which a journal article is derived primarily from a student's doctoral dissertation. In such cases, the ethics code mandates that the student be listed as the principal (first) author. This rule recognizes that the dissertation represents the student's original intellectual contribution—the student conceived of the research questions, designed the methodology, collected and analyzed data, and wrote the resulting manuscript. Relegating the student to a secondary authorship position when the published work is substantially based on their dissertation would constitute an inappropriate appropriation of credit.

The code acknowledges that exceptional circumstances may occasionally warrant a different arrangement, though such circumstances are rare and must genuinely justify the deviation. For example, if a faculty advisor substantially reconceptualized and rewrote the manuscript to such a degree that it no longer closely resembles the original dissertation, a different authorship order might be defensible. However, in the vast majority of cases, the student earns first authorship as a matter of ethical obligation. Faculty advisors who contributed to the work through supervision, guidance, or editorial input are appropriately credited as co-authors in a secondary position.

Clinical Note

This standard reflects the broader ethical principle that publication credit should accurately reflect the relative contributions of each individual. Supervisors and mentors should discuss authorship expectations with students early in the research process to prevent misunderstandings and ensure that credit allocation aligns with ethical requirements.

Standard 7: Education and Training

7.01: Design of Education and Training Programs

Psychologists responsible for education and training programs take reasonable steps to ensure that programs are designed to provide appropriate knowledge and proper experiences, and that the programs meet the requirements for licensure, certification, or other professional goals described in the program.

7.02: Descriptions of Education and Training Programs

Psychologists responsible for education and training programs take reasonable steps to ensure accurate descriptions of program content, training goals and objectives, stipends and benefits, and requirements for completion including required supervised experience and practicum.

7.03: Accuracy in Teaching

Psychologists take reasonable steps to ensure that course syllabi are accurate regarding subject matter, basis for evaluating progress, and the nature of course experiences. When teaching, psychologists present information accurately and do not deliberately teach false or misleading information.

7.04: Student Disclosure of Personal Information

Psychologists do not require students to disclose personal information in course- or program-related activities regarding:

  • Sexual history
  • History of abuse and neglect
  • Psychological treatment
  • Relationships with parents, peers, and spouses or significant others

Exception: Such disclosure may be required if (a) the program clearly identified this requirement in its admissions materials, or (b) the information is necessary to evaluate or obtain assistance for students whose personal problems could reasonably be judged to be preventing them from performing competently or posing a threat to others.

7.05: Mandatory Individual or Group Therapy

When a program requires individual or group therapy as part of its training, students are permitted to select the therapist from practitioners unaffiliated with the program. Faculty who are or are likely to be responsible for evaluating students' academic performance do not themselves provide that therapy.

EPPP Tip

Standard 7.05 is often tested: If a program mandates personal therapy, students must be able to choose their own therapist from outside the program. A faculty member who evaluates the student should NEVER be that student's therapist, as this creates an impermissible dual relationship.

7.06: Assessing Student and Supervisee Performance

Psychologists in academic and supervisory roles establish a timely and specific process for providing feedback to students and supervisees. Evaluations must be based on actual performance and relevant, established program requirements.

7.07: Sexual Relationships with Students and Supervisees

Psychologists do not engage in sexual relationships with students or supervisees who are in their department, agency, or training center, or over whom psychologists have or are likely to have evaluative authority. This prohibition is absolute and has no exceptions.

Key Takeaways in clinical practice

  • Faculty may not serve as therapists for students they evaluate (Standard 7.05); students in mandatory therapy programs must choose from unaffiliated practitioners.
  • Sexual relationships with students/supervisees are always prohibited (Standard 7.07).
  • Students cannot be required to disclose personal information (sexual history, abuse history, etc.) unless the program clearly identified this requirement in admissions materials or the information is needed to address competence/safety concerns.
  • Education and training programs must accurately describe their content, goals, requirements, and benefits.
  • Student evaluations must be based on actual performance and established program requirements (Standard 7.06).
