How Memory Fails — Forgetting, Interference & Amnesia Syndromes

Fundamental Content

Recall, Recognition & the Serial Position Effect

A 72-year-old retired professor sits across from his neuropsychologist, unable to recall a single word from a list of fifteen he studied just two minutes ago. The examiner then presents the same words mixed among distractors and asks him to point to the ones he recognizes — and he correctly identifies twelve of the fifteen. His problem is not that the memories are gone; it is that he cannot pull them out of storage without a cue. This simple clinical observation — that the same patient can fail spectacularly at one memory test while succeeding at another — reveals a fundamental truth about how retrieval works, and it carries direct implications for differential diagnosis, rehabilitation planning, and EPPP preparation.

Retrieval processes vary in the amount of environmental support they provide. Recall requires generating information from memory with minimal cues (e.g., "Name the four lobes of the cortex"), while recognition requires identifying previously encountered information from a set of alternatives (e.g., multiple-choice questions). Recognition is generally easier because the correct answer serves as its own retrieval cue.

Serial Position Effect:

The serial position effect describes the tendency to recall items at the beginning and end of a list better than items in the middle. The primacy effect (superior recall of early items) is attributed to greater rehearsal and transfer to long-term memory. The recency effect (superior recall of recent items) is attributed to the items still being active in short-term/working memory. A distractor task between learning and recall eliminates the recency effect but not the primacy effect, supporting the distinction between STM and LTM stores.

EPPP Application:

If an EPPP question describes a participant who learns a word list, performs 30 seconds of mental arithmetic, and then recalls the list, the expected finding is that the primacy effect is preserved (early items still recalled well) but the recency effect is eliminated (recent items are no longer in STM). This classic finding supports the Atkinson-Shiffrin dual-store model.

The Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve & Interference Theory

Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) conducted the first systematic study of forgetting using nonsense syllables. His forgetting curve demonstrated that forgetting is most rapid immediately after learning, with the rate of forgetting decelerating over time. Approximately 50% of newly learned material is forgotten within the first hour, and about 70% within 24 hours, but the remaining material is retained relatively stably over longer periods.

Proactive vs. Retroactive Interference

Interference theory proposes that forgetting occurs because memories compete with one another during retrieval. Two forms of interference are critical for the EPPP:

Clinical Relevance:

Proactive interference increases with age and is a significant contributor to age-related memory complaints. Older adults are particularly susceptible to proactive interference because they have accumulated more competing memories and may have reduced inhibitory control. In neuropsychological testing, proactive interference is assessed through paradigms such as the release-from-PI procedure and is a sensitive indicator of frontal lobe dysfunction.

Cue-Dependent Forgetting

Cue-dependent forgetting occurs when information is stored in long-term memory but cannot be accessed because the appropriate retrieval cues are absent. This is distinct from trace decay — the memory exists but is temporarily inaccessible. The tip-of-the-tongue (TOT) phenomenon is a vivid example: the person feels certain they know the word, can often recall partial information (first letter, number of syllables), but cannot produce the complete word until the right cue appears.

False Memories & the Misinformation Effect

Elizabeth Loftus's research on the misinformation effect demonstrates that post-event information can alter memory for the original event. In her classic studies, participants who watched a video of a car accident and were later asked "How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" (vs. "contacted" or "hit") reported higher speed estimates and were more likely to falsely remember seeing broken glass.

Misinformation Effect:

The misinformation effect occurs when misleading post-event information is incorporated into an individual's memory of the original event. This can take the form of misleading questions, exposure to inaccurate accounts from other witnesses, or suggestive therapeutic techniques. The misinformation effect is more powerful when the original memory is weak, when the misleading information is presented with authority, and when there is a long delay between the event and the misinformation.

The DRM Paradigm

The Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) paradigm demonstrates spontaneous false memory formation. Participants study a list of words all related to a non-presented critical lure (e.g., "bed," "rest," "awake," "tired," "dream" — the critical lure being "sleep"). At test, participants frequently recall or recognize the critical lure with high confidence, sometimes claiming to remember specific details of its presentation. This demonstrates that semantic activation during encoding can create false memories that feel phenomenologically real.

Loftus, E. F. (2005). Planting misinformation in the human mind: A 30-year investigation of the malleability of memory. Learning & Memory, 12(4), 361-366.

Source Monitoring Errors

Source monitoring refers to the process of attributing a memory to its correct source. Source monitoring errors occur when individuals correctly remember information but misattribute its origin — confusing something they imagined with something they actually experienced, something they read with something someone told them, or something one person said with something another person said. These errors are particularly common in children, older adults, and individuals with frontal lobe dysfunction.

Recovered Memories & the Repression Debate

The recovered memory debate centers on whether traumatic memories can be repressed and later accurately recovered, or whether "recovered" memories are typically false memories created through suggestive therapeutic techniques.

APA Position:

The American Psychological Association acknowledges that (1) both genuine recovered memories and false memories exist, (2) it is impossible to distinguish recovered memories from false memories based on their vividness, confidence, or emotional intensity alone, (3) suggestive therapeutic techniques (e.g., hypnosis, guided imagery, dream interpretation for memory recovery) can create false memories, and (4) therapists should avoid using such techniques specifically to "recover" memories. The EPPP may test the position that strong emotions and high confidence do NOT guarantee memory accuracy.

Flashbulb Memories

Flashbulb memories are vivid, apparently detailed memories for the circumstances surrounding the learning of a surprising, consequential, and emotionally arousing event (e.g., "Where were you when you heard about 9/11?"). Brown and Kulik (1977) proposed a special neural mechanism for their formation. However, subsequent research has demonstrated that flashbulb memories, despite their vividness and the high confidence with which they are held, are not as accurate as they feel. They are subject to the same distortions and reconstructive errors as ordinary memories, though people are more confident in their accuracy.

Motivated Forgetting & Repression

Motivated forgetting refers to the concept that individuals may forget information because it is unpleasant, threatening, or anxiety-provoking. Freud's concept of repression posits that traumatic memories are actively pushed out of conscious awareness as a defense mechanism. While there is evidence for intentional suppression of unwanted memories (Anderson & Green's "Think/No-Think" paradigm), the scientific evidence for Freudian repression of entire traumatic events is weak and contested.

The Hippocampus & Memory Consolidation

The hippocampus, a seahorse-shaped structure in the medial temporal lobe, plays a critical role in the consolidation of new declarative (explicit) memories. It serves as a temporary indexing system that binds together distributed cortical representations of an experience, enabling the gradual transfer of memories to neocortical storage over time.

Memory Consolidation:

Consolidation is the process by which newly encoded, fragile memory traces are stabilized and integrated into long-term storage. Synaptic consolidation occurs within hours at the level of individual synapses (involving protein synthesis and structural changes). Systems consolidation occurs over weeks to years, involving the gradual transfer of memory representations from the hippocampus to distributed neocortical networks. Once fully consolidated, memories can be retrieved independently of the hippocampus.

The hippocampus is essential for encoding new episodic memories and spatial navigation (containing "place cells" that fire when an organism occupies a specific location). It is also part of the Papez circuit, which links the hippocampus to the mammillary bodies, anterior thalamus, and cingulate cortex in a circuit important for memory formation.

Patient H.M. & the Amnesia Syndromes

The case of H.M. (Henry Molaison, 1926-2008) is the most important single case in the history of memory neuroscience. In 1953, neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville performed a bilateral medial temporal lobectomy (removing the hippocampus, amygdala, and surrounding cortex bilaterally) to treat H.M.'s severe epilepsy. The surgery controlled his seizures but produced profound amnesia.

H.M.'s Memory Profile:

H.M. demonstrated severe anterograde amnesia (inability to form new declarative memories after the surgery) and moderate retrograde amnesia (loss of memories for events from approximately 1-3 years before surgery, with more remote memories relatively preserved — a temporal gradient). Critically, his intelligence, personality, working memory, procedural learning, and priming were all intact. He could learn new motor skills (mirror drawing) despite having no memory of ever having practiced the task. This dissociation provided the first clear evidence that explicit and implicit memory depend on different neural systems.

Scoville, W. B., & Milner, B. (1957). Loss of recent memory after bilateral hippocampal lesions. Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery, and Psychiatry, 20(1), 11-21.

Korsakoff's Syndrome

Korsakoff's syndrome is a chronic neurological condition caused by severe thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, most commonly associated with chronic alcohol use disorder. The thiamine deficiency produces damage to the mammillary bodies, dorsomedial thalamus, and periventricular regions — all structures critical to memory circuits.

Clinical Features of Korsakoff's Syndrome:

The hallmark features include severe anterograde amnesia, variable retrograde amnesia, and confabulation — the production of fabricated, distorted, or misinterpreted memories without the conscious intention to deceive. Confabulation occurs because the patient fills memory gaps with plausible-sounding but inaccurate information. Patients may also show apathy, lack of insight (anosognosia), and difficulty with temporal ordering of events. Procedural memory and priming are typically preserved.

EPPP Distinction:

Wernicke's encephalopathy (acute phase: confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) and Korsakoff's syndrome (chronic phase: amnesia, confabulation) are often considered a continuum — Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. Wernicke's is treated emergently with IV thiamine; if untreated, approximately 80% of cases progress to Korsakoff's, which is largely irreversible. The EPPP tests the connection between chronic alcohol use, thiamine deficiency, and the specific memory symptoms of Korsakoff's.

Long-Term Potentiation & Hebb's Rule

Long-term potentiation (LTP) is the persistent strengthening of synaptic connections following high-frequency stimulation. First described in the hippocampus by Bliss and Lomo (1973), LTP is widely considered the primary cellular mechanism underlying learning and memory.

LTP involves several key molecular steps: (1) high-frequency stimulation of the presynaptic neuron releases glutamate, (2) this activates AMPA and NMDA receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, (3) NMDA receptor activation (which requires both glutamate binding and sufficient postsynaptic depolarization) allows calcium influx, (4) calcium triggers intracellular signaling cascades that strengthen the synapse through insertion of additional AMPA receptors and, eventually, structural changes (new dendritic spines).

Hebb's Rule:

Donald Hebb (1949) proposed that "cells that fire together wire together" — when a presynaptic neuron repeatedly contributes to firing a postsynaptic neuron, the synaptic connection between them is strengthened. This Hebbian learning rule anticipated the discovery of LTP by over two decades and remains a foundational principle of neural plasticity and memory formation. Hebb also proposed the concept of the "cell assembly" — a network of interconnected neurons that represents a memory and becomes increasingly coordinated through repeated co-activation.

Hebb, D. O. (1949). The Organization of Behavior. New York: Wiley. — Hebb's theoretical framework provided the conceptual foundation for modern theories of synaptic plasticity and learning.

Neural Substrates of Different Memory Systems

Amygdala & Emotional Memory

The amygdala modulates the encoding and consolidation of emotionally arousing memories. It does not store the declarative content of emotional memories but enhances hippocampal consolidation through stress hormone and norepinephrine signaling. This explains why emotionally charged events are generally better remembered — the amygdala amplifies the hippocampal encoding process. Amygdala damage impairs the emotional enhancement of memory without affecting the formation of non-emotional declarative memories.

Cerebellum & Procedural Memory

The cerebellum is essential for motor learning, classical conditioning of motor responses (e.g., eyeblink conditioning), and the fine-tuning of skilled movements. Cerebellar damage impairs the acquisition of conditioned motor responses and the performance of skilled procedural tasks.

Basal Ganglia & Habit Learning

The basal ganglia (particularly the striatum) are critical for habit learning, stimulus-response associations, and the automatization of behaviors through reinforcement. Patients with Huntington's disease (caudate nucleus atrophy) or Parkinson's disease (substantia nigra degeneration) show impaired procedural and habit learning despite relatively preserved declarative memory in early stages.

Reconsolidation Theory

Reconsolidation theory proposes that when a consolidated memory is reactivated (retrieved), it temporarily returns to a labile state and must be reconsolidated to persist. During this reconsolidation window, the memory is vulnerable to modification, disruption, or updating. This has clinical implications for treating PTSD — reactivating a traumatic memory during a therapeutic window (e.g., with propranolol administration or extinction training) may allow the emotional component to be weakened during reconsolidation.

Clinical Application — Memory Reconsolidation in Therapy:

Reconsolidation theory has informed trauma therapies that aim to reactivate traumatic memories in safe therapeutic conditions, then introduce new, corrective information during the reconsolidation window. This may update the original memory trace rather than simply creating a competing inhibitory memory (as in standard extinction). The approach is still being researched but represents a promising integration of neuroscience and psychotherapy.
