Overview of Feeding and Eating Disorders

Food is among the most fundamental human needs, yet for millions of individuals the act of eating becomes a battleground — a domain of rigid control, chaotic bingeing, terrifying loss of autonomy, or all three at once. These conditions are not lifestyle choices or phases of vanity; they are serious psychiatric disorders with the highest mortality rates in the DSM, capable of destroying cardiovascular function, bone density, fertility, and ultimately life itself.

The DSM-5 chapter on feeding and eating disorders includes conditions characterized by persistent disturbances in eating or eating-related behavior that result in altered consumption or absorption of food, causing significant impairment in physical health or psychosocial functioning. These disorders have among the highest mortality rates of any psychiatric condition, particularly anorexia nervosa.

Key Concept: Transdiagnostic Model

Fairburn's (2003) transdiagnostic model proposes that eating disorders share a core psychopathology: the over-evaluation of eating, shape, and weight, and their control. This model is the basis for enhanced cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT-E), which can be applied across eating disorder diagnoses.

Anorexia Nervosa (AN)

Having established that eating disorders share a core psychopathology — the over-evaluation of eating, shape, and weight — we now turn to the disorder where this disturbance reaches its most physically dangerous expression. Consider a 16-year-old cross-country runner whose coach praises her increasingly lean frame. She begins skipping meals, then cutting entire food groups, then exercising through injuries. Her parents notice her shrinking body, but she insists she feels "fine" and cannot see what everyone else sees. By the time she reaches the emergency department with a resting heart rate of 38 beats per minute, she weighs 85 pounds — and still believes her thighs are too large. This is the clinical reality of anorexia nervosa, a disorder characterized by restriction of energy intake leading to a significantly low body weight, intense fear of gaining weight, and a profound disturbance in the way body weight or shape is experienced.

What makes anorexia nervosa uniquely challenging — both clinically and conceptually — is that the individual's relentless pursuit of thinness often feels ego-syntonic. Patients frequently do not present for treatment voluntarily, and many actively resist intervention. The disorder entangles biology, cognition, and identity in ways that make it among the most difficult psychiatric conditions to treat, and as we will see, the most lethal.

DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

The DSM-5 organizes the diagnosis of anorexia nervosa around three core criteria, each capturing a different facet of the disorder — behavioral restriction, affective and motivational disturbance, and cognitive-perceptual disruption. Together, they paint a picture of someone who actively limits food intake, dreads weight gain, and experiences a fundamental distortion in how they perceive their own body.

  1. Criterion A: Restriction of energy intake relative to requirements, leading to significantly low body weight in the context of age, sex, developmental trajectory, and physical health
  2. Criterion B: Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, or persistent behavior that interferes with weight gain, even though at a significantly low weight
  3. Criterion C: Disturbance in the way body weight or shape is experienced, undue influence of body weight/shape on self-evaluation, or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness of the current low body weight

Notice how these criteria work together in clinical practice. A patient who meets Criterion A (dangerously low weight from restriction) but who recognizes the problem and wants to gain weight would not meet Criterion C. The DSM-5 requires the convergence of all three elements — the behavior, the fear or weight-interfering behavior, and the body image disturbance or denial — to distinguish anorexia nervosa from other conditions that cause weight loss, such as major depressive disorder, medical illness, or food insecurity.

Subtypes

Clinicians further classify anorexia nervosa into two subtypes based on the methods the individual uses to maintain their low weight. These subtypes carry different medical risk profiles and can shift over the course of the illness, so clinicians assign the subtype based on the patient's behavior over the preceding three months.

  • Restricting type: Weight loss achieved primarily through dieting, fasting, and/or excessive exercise. No recurrent binge-eating or purging in the past 3 months.
  • Binge-eating/purging type: Recurrent episodes of binge eating or purging (self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives, diuretics, or enemas) during the past 3 months.

The distinction matters because individuals with the binge-eating/purging subtype tend to have higher rates of impulsivity, substance use, and mood instability compared to those with the restricting subtype. They also face additional medical risks from purging behaviors, such as esophageal tears and severe dental erosion. Importantly, the binge-eating/purging type of anorexia nervosa differs from bulimia nervosa primarily by body weight — individuals with AN are at a significantly low weight, while those with bulimia nervosa typically maintain a normal or above-normal weight. This distinction will become clearer when we discuss bulimia nervosa in the next section.

Clinical Pearl

DSM-5 made several important changes to AN criteria: (1) removed the specific BMI threshold and the word "refusal" to eat, replacing it with "restriction"; (2) removed the amenorrhea criterion, recognizing that AN occurs in males and post-menopausal women; (3) Criterion B no longer requires explicit acknowledgment of fear of weight gain -- persistent behavior interfering with weight gain suffices.

These DSM-5 revisions deserve special attention in clinical practice. The shift from "refusal" to "restriction" was not merely semantic — "refusal" implied a willful, oppositional stance that stigmatized patients and poorly captured the complex motivational dynamics underlying food avoidance. Similarly, dropping the amenorrhea requirement broadened diagnostic access considerably. Under DSM-IV, a male patient who met every other criterion for anorexia nervosa could not technically receive the diagnosis, which clearly did not reflect clinical reality. Finally, the change to Criterion B acknowledges that many patients deny any fear of weight gain yet engage in persistent behaviors — such as excessive exercise, food rituals, or body checking — that actively prevent weight restoration. Clinicians can now diagnose AN based on observable behavior rather than relying solely on the patient's verbal report of subjective fear.

Medical Complications

Perhaps the single most critical fact to know about anorexia nervosa is this: AN carries the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder, approximately 5-10% per decade of illness. To put this in perspective, a young woman diagnosed at age 18 faces a sobering probability of death by her late twenties simply from the medical ravages of sustained starvation or from suicide, which accounts for a significant proportion of AN-related deaths. The lethality of this disorder underscores why clinicians must take it seriously even when patients minimize their symptoms.

The medical complications of anorexia nervosa span virtually every organ system. Cardiac complications — including bradycardia (dangerously slow heart rate), hypotension, and fatal cardiac arrhythmias caused by electrolyte imbalances — represent the most common cause of medical death. The body's attempt to conserve heat in the face of lost subcutaneous fat produces lanugo hair, a fine downy growth over the body that patients often find distressing. Chronic nutritional deprivation leads to osteoporosis, which can cause fractures in young women who should be at the peak of bone density. Clinicians should also be prepared for the paradoxical danger of treatment itself: refeeding syndrome occurs when nutritional restoration triggers dangerous electrolyte shifts — particularly hypophosphatemia — as the body rapidly transitions from a catabolic to an anabolic state. This potentially fatal complication requires careful medical monitoring during the early stages of weight restoration, with slow caloric increases and frequent electrolyte monitoring.

In practice, remember the key medical findings associated with AN: bradycardia, hypotension, lanugo hair, osteoporosis, electrolyte imbalances, and the risk of refeeding syndrome during treatment. Also remember that deaths result from both medical complications and suicide — a reminder that psychological assessment of suicidality remains essential even when the primary clinical focus is nutritional rehabilitation.

Bulimia Nervosa (BN)

Having examined anorexia nervosa — with its hallmark restriction and dangerously low body weight — we now turn to a disorder that shares many of the same preoccupations with weight and shape but follows a strikingly different behavioral pattern. Consider a college student who maintains a normal weight and appears healthy to friends and family. In private, however, she consumes enormous quantities of food in a single sitting — sometimes thousands of calories in under an hour — feeling utterly unable to stop. Afterward, overwhelmed by guilt and fear of weight gain, she forces herself to vomit before anyone notices. This cycle repeats several times a week, shrouded in secrecy and shame. This is the clinical picture of bulimia nervosa, a disorder defined by recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by inappropriate compensatory behaviors aimed at preventing weight gain.

What makes bulimia nervosa particularly insidious is its invisibility. Unlike anorexia nervosa, individuals with BN typically maintain a body weight that falls within or even above the normal range, which means the disorder can persist for years without detection by loved ones or even healthcare providers. The emotional toll, however, is enormous: individuals often describe feeling trapped in a cycle they cannot break, where binges provide momentary relief from negative emotions only to be replaced by intense self-loathing that fuels the compensatory behavior. Understanding this cycle — and the specific diagnostic criteria that distinguish BN from related disorders — is essential for accurate clinical assessment and effective intervention.

DSM-5 Criteria

The DSM-5 specifies five criteria that must be met for a diagnosis of bulimia nervosa. Clinicians should pay particular attention to the frequency and duration thresholds, which were revised from DSM-IV, as well as the critical weight-based distinction from anorexia nervosa.

  1. Recurrent episodes of binge eating (eating a definitively large amount of food in a discrete period with a sense of lack of control)
  2. Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors to prevent weight gain (purging, fasting, excessive exercise)
  3. Both occur at least once per week for 3 months (reduced from twice per week in DSM-IV)
  4. Self-evaluation is unduly influenced by body shape and weight
  5. Does not occur exclusively during episodes of AN

The reduction in frequency threshold from twice per week (DSM-IV) to once per week (DSM-5) is a clinically meaningful change worth noting in clinical practice. This revision reflected research showing that individuals who binged and purged once weekly experienced levels of distress and impairment comparable to those meeting the older, stricter threshold. In practice, this means more individuals now qualify for a BN diagnosis rather than falling into an "other specified" category, which can improve access to evidence-based treatment.

EPPP Example

A key differential: AN binge-eating/purging type vs. BN. The distinguishing factor is body weight. If the individual is at a significantly low body weight and meets AN criteria, the diagnosis is AN binge-eating/purging type, even if binge-purge cycles are present. BN is diagnosed when the individual is at or above minimally normal weight. Note that BN no longer has purging/nonpurging subtypes in DSM-5. This is a important distinction — when an exam vignette describes someone who binges and purges, always check the stated body weight before choosing between AN binge-eating/purging type and BN. The behaviors may look identical; the weight is what determines the diagnosis.

Medical complications of BN are numerous and often serve as the first clinical clues that the disorder is present. These include dental erosion from repeated exposure to stomach acid — often noticed first by dentists — and parotid gland enlargement, sometimes called "chipmunk cheeks," which results from the chronic stimulation of salivary glands during purging episodes. Russell's sign, a classic physical finding, refers to calluses or scarring on the dorsum of the hand and knuckles caused by repeated contact with the teeth during self-induced vomiting. More serious complications include esophageal tears (Mallory-Weiss syndrome), which can lead to life-threatening bleeding, and metabolic alkalosis from the loss of hydrochloric acid through vomiting. Electrolyte disturbances — particularly hypokalemia — can lead to cardiac arrhythmias, making regular metabolic monitoring an essential component of care for individuals with BN.

Treatment for bulimia nervosa has a stronger evidence base than for many other eating disorders. Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) is considered the gold-standard psychological treatment, targeting the distorted beliefs about weight and shape that maintain the binge-purge cycle. Among pharmacological interventions, fluoxetine (Prozac) is the only FDA-approved medication for BN and is typically prescribed at higher doses (60 mg) than those used for depression. In practice, remember that BN tends to have a better prognosis than AN, though relapse rates remain significant, and comorbid conditions — particularly depression, anxiety disorders, and substance use disorders — are common and must be addressed in comprehensive treatment planning.

Binge-Eating Disorder (BED)

Having explored Bulimia Nervosa and its hallmark cycle of bingeing followed by compensatory behaviors, we now turn to a condition that shares the binge-eating component but diverges in one critically important way: the absence of regular purging, fasting, or excessive exercise to "undo" the binge. Imagine a 42-year-old accountant who, after a stressful day at work, sits down with a container of ice cream and a bag of chips and eats rapidly and continuously — not because she is hungry, but because the eating feels automatic, almost trance-like. When she finishes, she feels intensely ashamed and disgusted with herself, yet she does not induce vomiting or take laxatives. This pattern has repeated two or three times a week for the past year. Her distress is profound, but her compensatory behavior is absent. This clinical picture captures the essence of Binge-Eating Disorder.

Binge-Eating Disorder was elevated to a full, standalone diagnosis in DSM-5 — a significant reclassification, as it had previously been housed in the appendix of DSM-IV as a condition warranting further study. This promotion reflected decades of research confirming that BED is clinically distinct from Bulimia Nervosa and carries its own trajectory of impairment, comorbidity, and treatment needs. The central diagnostic feature is recurrent episodes of binge eating — consuming an objectively large amount of food in a discrete period of time, accompanied by a sense of loss of control — without the regular use of compensatory behaviors such as purging, fasting, or excessive exercise that define Bulimia Nervosa.

Binge-Eating Episode Features (3 or more)

To qualify for a diagnosis, binge-eating episodes must be associated with at least three of the following behavioral and emotional markers. Notice how these features reflect both the loss-of-control quality of the eating and the intense negative affect that follows:

  • Eating much more rapidly than normal
  • Eating until feeling uncomfortably full
  • Eating large amounts when not feeling physically hungry
  • Eating alone because of embarrassment
  • Feeling disgusted, depressed, or very guilty afterward

A useful mnemonic for remembering these five features is to think of the word RFHAG — Rapid eating, uncomfortably Full, not Hungry, eating Alone, and Guilt/disgust afterward. Clinically, the last two features are particularly noteworthy: the secrecy and the shame form a self-reinforcing cycle. The individual eats alone to hide the behavior, feels terrible afterward, and the resulting negative affect becomes a trigger for the next binge episode. This emotional cycle distinguishes BED from simple overeating, which many people experience on occasion without the pervasive distress and loss of control.

Episodes must occur at least once per week for 3 months to meet the duration and frequency threshold — the same temporal criterion used for Bulimia Nervosa, which can help you anchor both diagnoses on the exam. In addition, marked distress regarding the binge eating must be present. This distress criterion is essential: a person who occasionally overeats at holiday meals but feels unconcerned would not meet the diagnostic bar. The individual with BED, by contrast, often describes a profound sense of helplessness, shame, and preoccupation with their eating behavior that permeates daily life.

From an epidemiological standpoint, BED is the most common eating disorder in the United States, with a lifetime prevalence of approximately 2.8% — more common than Anorexia Nervosa and Bulimia Nervosa combined. Its gender ratio is also more balanced than the other eating disorders, affecting roughly three women for every two men, compared to the much higher female-to-male ratios seen in AN and BN. Age of onset tends to be later as well, often emerging in late adolescence or adulthood rather than early to mid-adolescence.

One relationship that frequently trips up exam-takers is the connection between BED and obesity. While BED is strongly associated with obesity — and many individuals with BED do carry excess weight — the two conditions are not synonymous. Not all individuals with BED are obese, particularly earlier in the disorder's course, and the vast majority of individuals who are obese do not meet criteria for BED. Obesity is a medical condition defined by body mass index; BED is a psychiatric disorder defined by behavioral and emotional features. This distinction has important treatment implications: addressing binge eating directly (through cognitive-behavioral therapy, interpersonal therapy, or pharmacotherapy) is the clinical priority, rather than simply targeting weight loss. In fact, research consistently shows that CBT for BED reduces binge-eating episodes effectively, even when significant weight loss does not occur, underscoring that symptom reduction and improved quality of life — not the number on the scale — are the primary treatment outcomes.

Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

ARFID is a new diagnosis in DSM-5, replacing and expanding the DSM-IV diagnosis of "feeding disorder of infancy or early childhood." It involves an eating or feeding disturbance manifested by persistent failure to meet nutritional needs, associated with significant weight loss, nutritional deficiency, dependence on enteral feeding or supplements, or marked interference with psychosocial functioning.

Key Distinction

Unlike AN, ARFID does not involve distress about body shape or weight, nor fear of weight gain. The food avoidance may be based on sensory characteristics of food (texture, color, smell), concern about aversive consequences of eating (e.g., choking, vomiting), or apparent lack of interest in eating. ARFID can occur in children and adults.

Pica and Rumination Disorder

The DSM-5 feeding and eating disorders chapter includes two additional conditions that are clinically important: Pica and Rumination Disorder. These may co-occur with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, or other conditions.

Pica

Pica is defined as the persistent eating of nonnutritive, nonfood substances for a period of at least 1 month. The diagnostic criteria require:

  • The eating behavior is developmentally inappropriate -- mouthing and ingesting nonfood items is not considered abnormal before approximately age 2
  • The behavior is not part of a culturally sanctioned or socially normative practice
  • If the behavior occurs in the context of another mental disorder (e.g., intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder), it is sufficiently severe to warrant independent clinical attention

Pica is associated with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), pregnancy, and iron deficiency. Substances commonly ingested include dirt, clay, chalk, paper, soap, cloth, hair, string, and paint chips. Medical complications can be serious and include bowel obstruction, intestinal perforation, lead poisoning (from paint chips), parasitic infections, and dental injury.

Pica during pregnancy is a well-documented phenomenon that occurs across diverse cultural contexts. Pregnant women with pica commonly ingest substances such as clay (geophagia), coffee grounds, laundry starch (amylophagia), ice (pagophagia), chalk, and baking soda. While some of these practices may have cultural roots, they can pose significant health risks including lead exposure, intestinal blockage, and interference with nutrient absorption. The association between pica in pregnancy and iron-deficiency anemia is particularly robust, though the causal direction remains debated — iron deficiency may drive cravings for nonfood items, or the ingested substances may impair iron absorption, creating a self-reinforcing cycle. Clinicians should routinely screen for pica during prenatal assessments, as patients may not spontaneously disclose these behaviors due to embarrassment.

Rumination Disorder

Rumination Disorder involves the repeated regurgitation of food for a period of at least 1 month. Regurgitated food may be re-chewed, re-swallowed, or spit out. The diagnostic criteria require:

  • The behavior is not attributable to an associated gastrointestinal or other medical condition (e.g., gastroesophageal reflux, pyloric stenosis)
  • The behavior does not occur exclusively during the course of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder, or avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)
  • If occurring in the context of another mental disorder (e.g., intellectual disability), the symptoms are sufficiently severe to warrant independent clinical attention

Rumination disorder can occur across the lifespan, from infancy through adulthood. In infants, it may present with characteristic straining and arching movements. Treatment typically involves habit reversal training (learning incompatible behaviors such as diaphragmatic breathing after meals) and behavioral interventions. Diaphragmatic breathing -- breathing deeply using the diaphragm to create abdominal pressure that counteracts regurgitation -- has the strongest evidence base for this condition.

EPPP Example

Key distinctions: Pica involves eating nonfood substances (not regurgitation) and is not diagnosed before age 2. Rumination disorder involves regurgitation of previously ingested food (not nonfood substances). Both require at least 1 month duration and both can occur across the lifespan but must be distinguished from developmentally normal behaviors and medical conditions.

Etiology of Eating Disorders

Biological Factors

  • Genetics: Heritability estimates for AN are approximately 50-80%, among the highest for any psychiatric disorder. BN shows heritability of approximately 55%. Twin studies suggest shared genetic vulnerability across eating disorder diagnoses (Bulik et al., 2006).
  • Neurotransmitters: Serotonin dysregulation is implicated in both AN and BN. Individuals with BN show reduced serotonergic activity, which may contribute to impulsivity and binge eating. The efficacy of fluoxetine (the only FDA-approved medication for BN) supports serotonergic involvement.
  • Neuroendocrine changes: Leptin, ghrelin, and other appetite-regulating hormones are disrupted in eating disorders, though many changes appear to be consequences rather than causes of disordered eating.

Psychological and Sociocultural Factors

  • Perfectionism and low self-esteem: Perfectionism is a robust risk factor, particularly for AN. The combination of high standards and self-critical evaluation creates vulnerability.
  • Internalization of the thin ideal: Stice's (2002) dual-pathway model proposes that sociocultural pressure for thinness leads to body dissatisfaction, which promotes both dietary restraint (risk for AN) and negative affect (risk for binge eating).
  • Cognitive rigidity: Individuals with AN show set-shifting difficulties and weak central coherence (tendency to focus on details rather than global picture), which may be trait markers rather than state-dependent features.
  • Trauma history: Childhood sexual abuse is a nonspecific risk factor, associated with increased rates of BN and BED in particular.

EPPP Example

Treatment approaches differ by disorder: CBT-E (enhanced cognitive-behavioral therapy) is the first-line treatment for BN and BED. For AN, family-based treatment (FBT/Maudsley approach) is the best-supported treatment for adolescents, empowering parents to take charge of refeeding. For adults with AN, no single therapy has demonstrated clear superiority, though CBT-E, MANTRA (Maudsley model of anorexia treatment), and specialist supportive clinical management have the most evidence. Fluoxetine at 60 mg/day is effective for BN but medications have limited efficacy for AN.
